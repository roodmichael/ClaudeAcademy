# Lab 01 Debrief: Sensory Calibration

---

## What Just Happened

You tasted the full roast spectrum without the noise of your own production decisions. That is rare, and it is useful. Every roast you produce for the rest of this course now exists in relation to a calibrated reference point.

Notice a few things about what you experienced:

**The light roast was probably more acidic than you expected.** Light roasting preserves the naturally occurring fruit acids in the bean — malic, citric, and others — that darker roasting breaks down. If it tasted sharp or even a little sour, that is origin character at maximum expression. Whether it reads as "sour" or "bright" depends on your palate, and that distinction matters: "sour" is a defect, "bright" is a quality.

**The dark roast was probably simpler than the light roast.** Counterintuitive. The longer a bean roasts, the more its individual origin character gets replaced by roast character — bitterness, smoke, dark chocolate. Dark roasts are not "more" coffee. They are different chemistry. Some people love that. Many specialty roasters consider very dark roasting to mask a bean's best qualities.

**The medium roast probably felt the most approachable.** This is by design — medium roasts balance acidity and sweetness, body and finish. They are forgiving. They are also where most beginner home roasters aim.

---

## What Your Vocabulary Will Do for You

If you struggled to use the flavor wheel — if you found yourself writing "smells like coffee" — that is completely normal for session one. Sensory vocabulary is a skill, and it develops with practice. Here is the framework that makes it easier:

Start at the center of the wheel, not the edges. Ask: is this **fruity**? **floral**? **nutty**? **sweet**? Once you have a general family, then move outward. "Fruity" — is it more **berry** or **stone fruit** or **citrus**? "Berry" — is it **blackberry** or **blueberry** or **strawberry**?

You do not need to nail a specific descriptor to be doing useful work. "Something berry-ish, maybe blueberry, in the light roast" is more useful than "tastes like coffee."

---

## Red Flags to Watch For

**If all three tasted the same to you:** This is more likely a technique issue than a palate issue. Check that you used different beans (roasted at actually different levels), that you brewed them identically, and that you tasted them at a similar temperature. Temperature has an enormous effect on perceived flavor — a cup that tastes flat at 80C may open up beautifully at 65C.

**If the light roast tasted sour and unpleasant:** The beans may be old, or from a commercial brand that roasts light-labeled beans as a marketing move without the actual quality to support it. Try again with a well-regarded local roaster's light roast.

**If you could not identify acidity:** Acidity in coffee is not the same as acidity in orange juice. It is more like the crisp quality of a Granny Smith apple — not sharp or painful, but lively and bright. If you felt a slight tingling on the sides of your tongue, that was acidity.

---

## The Bigger Picture

Lab 01 is unusual in this course because nothing was produced. No roasting happened. The product is your sensory baseline — an internal reference that exists only in your notes and your memory.

Every time you cup your own roasts in Labs 02-06, you will unconsciously compare them to this session. That is exactly the point. You have calibrated your instruments. Now you get to make something.
