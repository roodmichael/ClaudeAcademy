# Lab 05 Debrief: Intentional Defect

---

## What You Just Built

You built a diagnostic instrument. By tasting a defect on purpose, with full knowledge of what caused it, you trained your palate to recognize the specific flavor signature of a roasting mistake. You will not forget that taste.

The next time a cup tastes sour and grassy, you will not guess at the problem — you will know it is underdevelopment, because you tasted underdevelopment on purpose and you know what it is. Same for the papery flatness of a baked roast.

This is why Lab 05 comes before Lab 06 rather than after it. You need a calibrated defect-detection system before you start iterating, so that when an iteration produces an unintended defect, you can identify and explain it.

---

## Reading Your Diagnosis

Your diagnosis sentence should sound like this:

*For underdeveloped:* "This cup tastes sour, grassy, and harsh because I stopped the roast 20 seconds before first crack fully ended. To fix it, I would let first crack complete fully and develop for at least 30 more seconds."

*For baked:* "This cup tastes flat, dull, and papery with no sweetness or acidity because I dragged the drying phase over twice as long as normal. The Maillard reactions ran out of energy. To fix it, I would use higher heat through the first 70% of the roast and reduce total roast time by 4-5 minutes."

If your diagnosis sentence feels obvious — good. It should. The point was to make the obvious relationship between process and outcome undeniable and permanent.

## The Diagnostic Framework, Grounded

From COFF-100 research-03.md, the diagnostic table:

| Flavor Problem | Likely Roast Cause |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Sour, grassy, harsh | Underdeveloped — short development time |
| Flat, papery, dull | Baked — slow roast, lost momentum |
| Thin, overly acidic | Maillard phase too short |
| Bitter, ashy | Overdeveloped — too long past second crack |

You have now tasted at least one of these live. When you encounter any of the others in your future roasts, you have the framework to name it and fix it.

## An Important Observation About "Bad" Coffee

The defect roast was drinkable, right? Even an underdeveloped or baked roast produces coffee you can drink. This is both reassuring and a trap. Many home roasters drink their mediocre roasts without ever quite knowing why they are not as good as the coffee at the local specialty shop. The gap between "drinkable" and "the best this bean can be" is the gap that systematic practice closes.

You are now on the other side of that gap. You can taste the difference. You know what causes it. That is the entire point of this lab.

---

## What This Means for Your Equipment

Every home roasting setup has characteristic tendencies. Some run hot and fast (prone to underdevelopment if you are not careful to extend development, or overdevelopment if you move too fast). Some run slower (prone to baking if heat is not managed in the drying phase). Your defect roast, combined with your normal roasts from Labs 02-04, is building a picture of how your equipment tends to behave.

Take a moment to articulate: is your machine's natural tendency toward faster-and-hotter or slower-and-flatter? What does that mean for what you need to watch?
