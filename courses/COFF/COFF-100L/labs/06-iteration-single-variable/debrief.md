## Reading Your Results

**If your overall scores improved across the three roasts:** You demonstrated that systematic adjustment produces measurable improvement. This is not a small thing. Many roasters roast for years without ever getting meaningfully better, because they change multiple things at once, do not log, or do not cup systematically. You did everything right.

**If your scores were inconsistent or went down at some point:** This is also valuable data. A dip in Roast 2 followed by improvement in Roast 3 usually means you overcorrected in the first adjustment, then found the middle path. That is not failure — it is how iterative calibration works in any domain. The information in the dip is as valuable as the improvement.

**If all three roasts scored similarly:** Either your baseline was already very good (excellent — you hit a ceiling), or the variable you chose was not the bottleneck (the limiting factor was something else). The question to ask: what would I change next?

## The Single-Variable Principle

Why one variable at a time?

Because the cup is the only output. If you change development time, charge temperature, and batch size simultaneously and the roast improves, you have no way to know which change drove the improvement. Next time, you cannot replicate it confidently.

Single-variable adjustment is slower. It requires patience. It also means that every positive result is a real data point you can reproduce and build on.

The compounding value of this approach is significant: after 20 controlled single-variable roasts on the same bean, you understand that bean deeply. Your log is a model of the bean's behavior. You are making decisions based on evidence, not instinct.
