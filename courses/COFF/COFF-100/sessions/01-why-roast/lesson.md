# Session 01: Why Roast? The Case for Fresh Coffee
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain why freshness is the central value of home roasting and describe how coffee culture evolved to make home roasting a meaningful hobby.

---

## Opening

Here is a question worth sitting with: the coffee sitting in most kitchen cabinets right now was probably roasted six months ago. Maybe more. It was roasted in a factory, packed in a bag, shipped to a warehouse, loaded onto a truck, placed on a shelf, and then purchased — all while slowly going stale. By the time you open it, the volatile aromatic compounds that make coffee extraordinary have largely escaped.

What if you could change that? What if the gap between "roasted" and "in your cup" was measured in days instead of months?

That is the core promise of home roasting. This first session isn't about technique — it's about *why* roasting at home is worth doing, and how we got to a world where it's even possible.

---

## A Very Short History of Coffee

Coffee's story begins in the highlands of Ethiopia, where legend holds that a goat herder named Kaldi noticed his animals became unusually energetic after eating red cherries from a particular shrub. Monks made a drink from those cherries and found it helped them stay alert through long evening prayers. The plant spread east to Yemen, where it was cultivated and traded — and where, in the 15th century, the world's first coffeehouses appeared.

Those coffeehouses, called **qahveh khaneh** (coffee houses), were social institutions. People gathered there for conversation, music, games, and news. They were nicknamed "Schools of the Wise." As pilgrims traveled to Mecca, they brought coffee knowledge west — to Cairo, Istanbul, Damascus, and eventually to Europe, where it arrived in the 17th century and sparked its own coffeehouse culture.

The important thing to notice: for most of coffee's history, roasting happened in small batches, close to the point of consumption. A coffeehouse roasted its own beans. A household roasted what it needed. Freshness was the default.

**Industrialization changed that.** As coffee became a global commodity in the 19th and 20th centuries, roasting centralized into large factories. Coffee was pre-ground, canned, and sold with a shelf life measured in months. Freshness disappeared from most people's experience of coffee — without them even knowing it was missing.

---

## The Three Waves of Coffee

Coffee culture in the 20th and 21st centuries is often described in three waves. Understanding these waves gives you context for why specialty coffee — and home roasting — exist.

**The first wave** is mass-produced, commodity coffee. Think Folgers, Maxwell House, Nescafé. The goal was convenience and consistency, not quality. Pre-ground, pre-packaged, always available. Most Americans drank this for most of the 20th century.

**The second wave** began in the 1970s and accelerated through the 1990s and 2000s. Alfred Peet — often called the father of specialty coffee — opened Peet's Coffee in Berkeley in 1966 and introduced Americans to darker, fresher-roasted coffee with real flavor. He mentored the founders of Starbucks. The second wave brought the café as a social space, espresso drinks, and the idea that coffee could be *experienced* rather than merely consumed.

**The third wave** is where we are now. Coffee treated like wine. Single-origin beans. Specific farms. Specific processing methods. Roasters who name and explain every decision. Consumers who want to understand what they're drinking. Home roasting is a natural extension of third-wave thinking: why buy someone else's roasting decisions when you can make your own?

---

## Why Home Roast?

There are three compelling reasons to roast at home.

**Freshness.** This is the biggest one. Coffee is best within 1–4 weeks of roasting, depending on the brew method. Most commercially roasted coffee purchased at a grocery store is well past this window. Home roasting closes that gap completely. You roast on Sunday; you're drinking peak-flavor coffee by Tuesday.

**Cost.** Green (unroasted) coffee beans typically cost 40–60% less per pound than roasted beans of equivalent quality. If you drink a lot of good coffee, the savings add up quickly.

**Control and discovery.** When you roast your own coffee, you choose the origin, the roast level, and the process. You can roast the same Ethiopian bean two different ways and taste the difference. You can explore varietals and origins that no local roaster carries. You are no longer a passive consumer — you are a craftsperson.

---

## What Changes When Coffee Is Roasted?

We'll go deep on the chemistry in Session 04. But here's the essential idea: a green coffee bean is not inert. It contains hundreds of chemical compounds — sugars, amino acids, chlorogenic acids, lipids — that are the *precursors* to flavor. Roasting applies heat, and that heat triggers chemical reactions that transform those precursors into the aromatic compounds responsible for coffee's aroma and taste.

The same green bean roasted lightly tastes entirely different from the same bean roasted dark. Roasting isn't just heating. It's a controlled chemical transformation that you, the roaster, direct.

---

## Why This Matters

The thing most people don't know about coffee is that it's a perishable agricultural product, not a stable commodity. The moment coffee is roasted, a clock starts. Volatile aromatic compounds begin escaping. Oxygen starts breaking down the oils. What makes coffee taste extraordinary is time-sensitive.

Home roasting puts you in control of that clock. You decide when the roast ends. You decide when you brew. You decide what you're tasting. That's the craft — and this course is going to teach you how to practice it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Coffee's origins trace to Ethiopia and Yemen; coffeehouses were historically centers of social and intellectual life.
- Three waves of coffee culture: mass-produced commodity (1st), quality and experience (2nd), origin and transparency (3rd).
- Home roasting's primary value is freshness — closing the gap between roasting and drinking to days instead of months.
- Green coffee beans cost significantly less than pre-roasted beans of equivalent quality.
- Roasting is a controlled chemical transformation, not just heating — you are the one directing it.
