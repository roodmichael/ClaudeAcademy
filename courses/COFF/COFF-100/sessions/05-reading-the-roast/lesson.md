# Session 05: Reading the Roast — First Crack, Roast Levels, and Heat Control
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to use color, aroma, and sound to navigate a roast in real time, identify roast level by visual and auditory cues, and explain why managing heat matters at each stage.

---

## Opening

A professional roaster once described their job this way: "I'm not operating machinery. I'm having a conversation with the beans." That sounds poetic, but it is also literally accurate. During a roast, the beans are constantly sending signals — color changes, aromas, sounds — that tell you exactly where you are in the process. Your job as the roaster is to listen.

Session 04 taught you the chemistry. This session teaches you to read it in real time.

---

## Your Three Senses Are Your Instruments

In the absence of sophisticated temperature sensors and logging software, home roasters navigate by observation. Three senses do the work.

### 1. Sight: Color Progression

Bean color changes continuously throughout a roast. Learning to read this progression is the most accessible skill in home roasting, because you can see it in any setup.

| Stage | Bean Color | What's Happening |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| Start | Pale green | Raw — moisture intact |
| Drying phase | Yellow-gold | Moisture evaporating, pre-Maillard |
| Early browning | Tan / cinnamon | Maillard beginning; caramelization starting |
| Late browning | Medium brown | Maillard reaction well underway |
| First crack | Brown, expanding | Exothermic fracture; development begins |
| Light roast | Light-medium brown, no surface oil | Origin character preserved |
| Medium roast | Medium brown, no surface oil | Balanced profile |
| Medium-dark | Darker brown, slight sheen | Bittersweet; body increasing |
| Dark roast | Dark brown to nearly black, oily surface | Pyrolysis territory; roast dominates |

A **coffee color reference card** is a simple tool that helps you match your beans to a target roast color. Specialty suppliers often include these or sell them separately. Alternatively, take photos of your beans at each roast and compare batches visually over time.

### 2. Sound: First and Second Crack

Sound is the most reliable landmark in roasting — especially for first crack, which is hard to miss.

**First crack:** Sounds like popcorn popping, but lighter and less forceful. Each pop is a cell wall fracturing. First crack is not instantaneous — it typically unfolds over 1–3 minutes. Pay attention to:
- **When it starts:** Note the time.
- **Rolling crack:** The sound becomes steady and continuous.
- **When it ends:** The pops become sporadic and then stop.

The development phase begins at the *start* of first crack and continues until you stop the roast. How much time you add after first crack determines your roast level.

**Second crack:** Sounds different from first crack. It is faster, more hissing, almost like crinkling paper or a sharper, more rapid spitting sound. It is also more urgent — the beans are degrading quickly. If you hear second crack and want to stay in light-to-medium territory, you are past your window.

Tip: When you're learning, roast near silence so you can hear the beans clearly. Distractions cost you crack recognition time.

### 3. Smell: The Aroma Progression

Aroma tracks the roast accurately and gives you advance warning of stage transitions.

| Stage | Aroma |
|-------|-------|
| Drying | Grassy, hay, fresh grain |
| Early browning | Bread, toast, grain baking |
| Late browning | Nutty, chocolate, toast |
| First crack | Rich, full roast aroma; complex |
| Light-medium | Bright, floral, fruit notes possible |
| Medium-dark | Caramel, chocolate, smoke beginning |
| Dark | Smoky, pungent, roast-dominant |

Smell is often the first sense to alert you when something is going wrong — a sharp, acrid note earlier than expected means the heat is too high and you may be scorching.

## Roast Level Reference

Here is a practical roast level guide for home roasters:

| Roast Level | Stop At | Cup Character | Suited For |
|-------------|---------|--------------|-----------|
| Light | 30–60 sec after first crack starts | High acidity, fruity/floral, lighter body | Pour-over, drip, delicate origins |
| Medium-Light | First crack fully completed | Balanced acidity, some sweetness | Pour-over, Chemex, Aeropress |
| Medium | 1–2 min after first crack ends | Sweet, balanced, medium body | Drip, pour-over, French press |
| Medium-Dark | Toward onset of second crack | Bittersweet, full body, low acidity | French press, espresso blends |
| Dark | Into or past second crack | Smoky, bitter, oily, roast-dominant | Espresso, strong milk drinks |

For beginners, **aim for medium to medium-light on your first roasts.** These levels are forgiving — a little variation in timing doesn't produce dramatically bad results — and they produce balanced, approachable coffee that's easy to evaluate.

## Heat Control: Why It Matters Even Without Instruments

Most beginner setups don't have precise temperature readouts. But you still have heat control — through the settings on your equipment, how long you preheat, and how you respond to what you observe.

The key principle is this: **the roast should progress steadily.** Temperature should rise continuously from start to finish. A roast that stalls — where temperature plateaus or drops — produces a baked result. A roast where temperature rises too fast can scorch the bean exterior.

For popcorn poppers and simple home roasters, your heat control is limited. But you can:
- **Preheat properly:** Starting with a cold machine delays the drying phase and risks uneven roasting.
- **Respect batch size:** Overloading reduces airflow and heat transfer, leading to uneven results.
- **Manage time, not temperature:** Since you can't read the bean temperature directly, track time carefully and adjust your next roast based on tasting results.

The roast log (introduced more in Session 08) is how you use time as a proxy for heat control.

## Your First Real Decision: When to Stop

Everything you've learned in Sessions 01–05 leads to one moment: the moment you end the roast. You've chosen your beans (Session 03). You understand what the heat is doing (Session 04). You've learned the signals (this session). Now you have to decide.

A practical framework for beginners:
1. Note the exact minute first crack begins.
2. Let first crack fully roll through and complete.
3. For a first medium roast: set a timer for 90 seconds after first crack ends. Stop there.
4. Transfer to cooling immediately.

That's it. You will refine this over time — but your first roast doesn't need to be perfect. It needs to exist. You cannot learn by reading alone.

---

## Why This Matters

All the chemistry in Session 04 is invisible. What you actually work with during a roast is what you can see, hear, and smell. This sensory vocabulary is the bridge between the theory of roasting and the craft of roasting. After your first roast, you will understand first crack in a way that no written description can fully convey. The sound of it is distinctive — and once you've heard it, you never forget it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Color, sound, and smell are your primary instruments during a roast.
- Bean color progresses from green through yellow, tan, brown to dark brown; each stage corresponds to chemical changes.
- First crack is audible, exothermic, and essential — it marks the start of the development phase and is the primary timing landmark.
- Second crack is faster, hissing, and signals pyrolysis — avoid for light-to-medium roasts.
- For beginners: aim for medium to medium-light roast; stop 90 seconds after first crack ends on your first attempts.
- You cannot learn to roast by reading alone. Session 06 is your first hands-on roast.
