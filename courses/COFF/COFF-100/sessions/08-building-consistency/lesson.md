## Opening

By now you have roasted at least one batch, evaluated it systematically, and identified at least one thing you'd do differently. Congratulations — you are already doing the thing that separates improving home roasters from stagnating ones.

This final session is about turning that single cycle into a sustained practice. Roasting well is not about executing the perfect roast once. It's about getting better, batch by batch, in a way that you can understand and control.

---

## The Core Principle: Change One Variable at a Time

The most important discipline in home roasting improvement is this: **when you change something between batches, change only one thing.**

If you change two things — say, you buy a different origin AND extend your development time — and the result is better, you won't know which change helped. If the result is worse, you won't know which change hurt. You'll loop back to guessing.

The single-variable principle applies to everything:
- Roast level: adjust development time by 30–60 seconds at a time
- Green coffee: try one new origin; keep your process identical
- Equipment: if you change equipment, run the same bean you know well so you can feel the difference
- Batch size: change in small increments, never double all at once

This is how scientists think. It is also how craftspeople think, even if they don't call it that.

## The Roasting Log: Your Memory

Your roasting log is the institutional memory of your home roastery. Without it, every batch is your first batch.

A complete log entry for every roast should include:

**Pre-roast:**
- Date
- Green coffee: origin, processing method, supplier, freshness (harvest year)
- Green weight
- Equipment (especially if it varies or changes)
- Target roast level
- What you're adjusting from last time, and why

**During the roast:**
- Time to yellow (approximate)
- Time to first crack start
- Time to first crack end
- Time to end of roast
- Any observations (unusual smells, color anomalies, equipment behavior)

**Post-roast:**
- Roasted weight
- Weight loss percentage
- Bean color (light/medium-light/medium/medium-dark/dark)
- Aroma of cooled beans

**Tasting notes (after 48+ hours):**
- Brew method used
- Acidity, sweetness, body, aftertaste, balance (1–5 scale or descriptive)
- Flavor descriptors (from the flavor wheel or your own vocabulary)
- Overall assessment
- Adjustment plan for next roast

It sounds like a lot. After a few batches, it takes 5 minutes. The cumulative value is enormous.

## Recognizing Progress

Home roasters often feel like they're not improving because they're comparing their coffee to a vague ideal, not to their own previous work. A more useful comparison: is today's batch better than Batch 3? Can you see in your log why?

Progress in home roasting looks like:
- First crack becoming predictable — you know roughly when to expect it
- Weight loss percentages becoming consistent for a target roast level
- Flavor assessments shifting from "I'm not sure what I'm tasting" to "I can identify at least two distinct attributes"
- Batch-to-batch variation shrinking
- Tasting notes showing a logical connection to roast log entries

None of this requires expensive equipment or a professional palate. It requires paying attention and writing things down.

## Common Plateaus and How to Get Past Them

**"All my roasts taste the same."**
Most likely cause: not changing enough variables to learn the range. Solution: deliberately roast two batches of the same bean — one ending 60 seconds before your normal point, one ending 60 seconds after. Cup all three. You'll suddenly have a reference range.

**"I can hear first crack but I can't tell when it starts vs. ends."**
This is normal for the first 5–10 roasts. First crack has a beginning (sporadic pops), a rolling middle (continuous pops), and an end (pops becoming sporadic again). The rolling phase is the clearest signal. Record the beginning of rolling first crack as your marker.

**"My roasts are inconsistent batch to batch."**
Most likely cause: environmental variables. Same equipment behaves differently on a hot vs. cold day, with a full vs. nearly empty propane canister, with slightly different batch sizes. Solution: standardize everything you can (batch size, preheat time, indoor vs. outdoor), and track weather or room temperature in your log.

**"I don't feel like I'm tasting anything interesting."**
This is common before your palate is trained. Solution: cup your home roast against a high-quality specialty coffee from a local roaster or online. Side-by-side comparison trains the palate faster than tasting alone. Or try a naturally-processed Ethiopian — the fruit-forward character is so distinctive that it's hard to miss.

## Storage and Freshness: The Daily Practice

Roasting is only part of the freshness equation. How you store and handle your coffee between roasting and brewing matters too.

**Best practices:**
- Store in an airtight container with a one-way CO₂ valve. A simple mason jar works, but one-way valve bags or lids are better.
- Keep in a cool, dark location — a cupboard away from heat sources.
- Do not refrigerate (condensation as beans warm up damages freshness).
- Do not grind until immediately before brewing.
- Label every container: origin, roast date, roast level.
- Plan your roast schedule so you're drinking coffee within 2–4 weeks of roasting.

## Where to Go Next

This course has given you the foundations. Where do you go from here?

**Keep a roasting journal for 10 more batches.** The difference between batch 1 and batch 11 is almost always remarkable, and having the log to read the journey is satisfying.

**Explore different origins.** Try a washed Ethiopian vs. a natural Ethiopian. Try a Brazilian vs. a Kenyan. The contrast is education.

**Try a new processing method.** If all your early roasts were washed coffees, try a natural or honey-processed coffee and notice the difference in how the bean behaves under heat.

**Find a local specialty roaster and ask questions.** Third-wave roasters generally love talking about their craft. Tasting their coffee alongside yours is instructive.

**Consider COFF-101** (when available): core concepts — roast profiling, temperature management, and brew method matching. COFF-100 gave you the foundations. COFF-101 gives you the tools to build on them.

## Key Takeaways

- Change only one variable at a time when adjusting between roasts — otherwise you can't learn from the change.
- The roasting log is your most important long-term tool; the tasting notes must connect to it.
- Progress is measurable: track weight loss consistency, crack timing predictability, and tasting vocabulary development.
- Most plateaus have a specific cause and a specific fix — usually standardizing a variable or introducing deliberate contrast.
- Store roasted coffee in airtight containers, in a cool dark place, for up to 2–4 weeks. Grind immediately before brewing.
- The feedback loop — roast, taste, adjust — is not just a course exercise. It is the practice.
