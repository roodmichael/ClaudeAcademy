# Session 03: Origins and Processing Methods
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to describe the flavor characteristics of major coffee-growing regions and explain how washed, natural, and honey processing methods affect green coffee flavor before roasting begins.

---

## Opening

Imagine buying two bags of green coffee from the same supplier, roasting both identically, and ending up with cups that taste completely different from each other. One is bright and citrusy; the other is rich, sweet, and berry-forward. You didn't change anything in your roasting process. What happened?

The answer was already in the beans before you got them. Where a coffee grew, how high above sea level, and — critically — how the cherry was processed after harvest all shape the flavor ceiling that your roasting can reveal. This session is about understanding those upstream variables.

---

## The Coffee Belt and Why Location Matters

Coffee grows in a band around the equator called the **coffee belt**, stretching from about 25° north to 30° south latitude. Within this zone, conditions vary enormously: altitude, rainfall, soil type, and local microclimate all affect how a coffee plant develops and what its beans taste like. This combination of environmental factors is sometimes called **terroir** — a term borrowed from wine to describe place-based flavor.

**Altitude** is one of the most significant variables. At higher elevations, temperatures are cooler, which means beans develop more slowly. Slower development allows more time for complex sugars to accumulate, producing coffees with higher acidity and more layered flavor. Most specialty arabica is grown between 1,000–2,000 meters. However, altitude is not a simple guarantee of quality — it interacts with variety, climate, and processing.

### Major Regions and Their Flavor Signatures

You don't need to memorize these, but recognizing them helps you choose beans and predict what you're working with.

**Ethiopia** — Coffee's homeland. Ethiopian coffees are often the most complex and delicate. Flavor profiles range from intensely floral (jasmine, bergamot) to intensely fruity (blueberry, wine-like red fruit). The Yirgacheffe and Sidama regions are famous for this. Heirloom varieties grown here are genetically diverse — part of why Ethiopian coffees taste so different from each other.

**Colombia** — One of the world's most recognizable coffee origins. Balanced flavor: mild, caramel, nutty, with soft fruit notes. Often described as approachable — not aggressive in any one direction. Good choice for a first experimental roast.

**Guatemala** — Rich, full-bodied. Chocolate, spice, mild smokiness. Gets more complex at medium and medium-dark roasts. Antigua and Huehuetenango are well-known regions.

**Brazil** — The world's largest coffee producer. Brazilian coffees are characteristically low in acidity, heavy in body, nutty and chocolatey. They tolerate a wide range of roast levels without becoming unpleasant. Very forgiving for beginners.

**Kenya** — Distinctive. Bold, bright, wine-like berry acidity. Often described as almost "juicy." Kenya AA (a size grade for large beans) is a benchmark for brightness and complexity. Not for the faint of heart — the acidity is assertive.

**Indonesia (Sumatra, Sulawesi, Java)** — Earthy, full-bodied, low acidity, herbal. Distinctively different from African and Central American coffees due to both the wet-hull processing method and the local varieties. Sumatran coffees in particular have a characteristic "funky" earthiness.

## Processing Methods: The Game-Changer

Here is the most important concept in this session: **processing method can matter as much as origin.**

After harvest, coffee cherries must have the fruit removed and the seeds dried before they can be exported. *How* this happens leaves a permanent imprint on the flavor of the bean. The three main methods are washed, natural, and honey.

### Washed (Wet Process)

The most common method in Central America, East Africa, and Colombia.

1. The cherry skin and most of the fruit pulp are removed by machine immediately after harvest.
2. The beans (still in their parchment) are fermented in water tanks for 12–72 hours to break down the remaining mucilage.
3. Washed, then dried on raised beds in the sun.

**Flavor result:** Clean, bright, acidic. The intrinsic character of the bean — its variety and origin — comes through most clearly in a washed coffee, because the fruit isn't adding flavors of its own. Washed coffees tend to have prominent acidity and are often described as "transparent."

### Natural (Dry Process)

The oldest processing method — and still common in Ethiopia and Brazil.

1. Whole cherries are spread out to dry in the sun, often on raised beds, for 3–6 weeks.
2. The fruit dries around the seed. Sugars and fruit flavors infuse the bean during this time.
3. Once dry, the dried fruit husk is removed by machine.

**Flavor result:** Fruity, sweet, wine-like. Natural coffees often have berry, tropical fruit, or fermented notes because the fruit's sugars have been absorbed into the bean. Body is fuller. Acidity may be lower. The trade-off: natural coffees require more careful drying management; inconsistently dried naturals can have fermentation defects that make them taste funky or sour in an unpleasant way.

### Honey Process

Pioneered in Costa Rica; now common throughout Central America.

1. The cherry skin is removed (like washed), but the sticky mucilage layer is left on the bean during drying.
2. The amount of mucilage retained determines variations: white, yellow, red, and black honey — the darker the designation, the more mucilage and the more fruit influence.

**Flavor result:** A middle ground. Honey-processed coffees tend to have more sweetness and body than washed, but more clarity and cleanliness than natural. Often described as having a "rounded" profile.

## Choosing Your First Green Coffee

For your first 3–5 roasts as a beginner, consider these principles:

1. **Start with arabica.** Washed colombian or a light natural Ethiopian are classic starting points.
2. **Buy a sample pack.** Most specialty green bean suppliers offer sample packs of 4–6 origins in small quantities (4–8 oz each). This lets you experiment without committing to a pound of something you might not love.
3. **Buy fresh crop.** Look for a harvest date or "crop year." Avoid coffee more than 12 months from harvest.
4. **Read the listing carefully.** Good suppliers note origin (country, region, sometimes farm), altitude, processing method, variety (if known), and suggested roast level.

**Recommended first-buy suppliers:** Sweet Maria's (sweetmarias.com), Coffee Bean Corral (coffeebeancorral.com), Burman Coffee Traders (burmanscoffee.com).

## Why This Matters

When you roast, you are not creating flavors from nothing. You are *revealing* and *transforming* what is already in the green coffee. A naturally-processed Ethiopian with berry notes will respond differently to heat than a washed Guatemalan with chocolate notes — not just in flavor, but in how the beans look, sound, and behave during the roast. Understanding your raw material makes you a better roaster.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Coffee grows in the "coffee belt" around the equator; altitude, climate, and soil (terroir) shape flavor.
- Major regions have characteristic flavor profiles: Ethiopia (floral/fruity), Colombia (balanced), Guatemala (chocolate/spice), Brazil (nutty/low acidity), Kenya (bold berry acidity), Indonesia (earthy/full body).
- Washed processing yields clean, bright, acidic cups; natural processing yields fruity, sweet, wine-like cups; honey processing bridges the two.
- Processing method is often as important as origin in determining the flavor of a green coffee.
- Specialty suppliers provide detailed origin info — learn to read it before you buy.
