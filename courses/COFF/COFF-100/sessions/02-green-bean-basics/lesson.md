# Session 02: The Coffee Cherry — Green Bean Basics
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to identify the two main coffee species, describe the anatomy of a coffee cherry, and explain what a green coffee bean is and why it matters to the home roaster.

---

## Opening

Here's something most coffee drinkers don't know: coffee is a fruit. The "bean" you roast is actually the seed inside a small, red (or yellow) cherry that grows on a flowering shrub. There are two seeds per cherry, lying flat against each other — and when you peel back the layers of that cherry, you find a pale, greenish seed that smells like grass and tastes like almost nothing.

That unremarkable-looking seed is what you're going to transform. Understanding what it is — physically, genetically, and chemically — is the first step to understanding what roasting does to it.

---

## The Coffee Plant and Its Fruit

Coffee comes from the genus *Coffea*, a tropical shrub that grows in a band around the equator called the **coffee belt** (roughly 25° north to 30° south of the equator). The plant produces white jasmine-scented flowers, which develop into **coffee cherries** — small, round fruits that ripen over 6–9 months, typically turning red or yellow when ready to harvest.

Inside each cherry:
- An outer skin called the **exocarp**
- A layer of sweet pulp called the **mesocarp**
- A sticky, sugary layer called the **mucilage**
- A papery inner skin called the **parchment** (or endocarp)
- A thin silver skin (the **silverskin** or chaff — you'll see this come off during roasting)
- The **seeds** — two per cherry, flat-side together. These are the coffee beans.

When only one seed develops (the other doesn't fertilize), you get a rounder bean called a **peaberry** — prized by some for its even roasting properties.

---

## The Two Main Species

Most coffee in the world comes from two species of the *Coffea* genus. Understanding the difference between them matters for home roasters because they roast differently and taste completely different.

**Arabica (Coffea arabica)**
- Accounts for approximately 60% of world coffee production.
- Grown at higher altitudes — typically 1,000–2,000 meters above sea level.
- Has lower caffeine content than robusta.
- Slower-growing, more sensitive to disease and climate.
- Flavor profile: complex, fruity, floral, sweet, with higher acidity. The widest range of interesting cup profiles comes from arabica.
- Descended from plants first cultivated in Ethiopia and Yemen.

**Robusta (Coffea canephora)**
- Accounts for approximately 40% of world coffee production.
- Grows at lower altitudes; more disease-resistant and higher-yielding than arabica.
- Higher caffeine content (roughly double that of arabica).
- Flavor profile: earthy, woody, bitter, less fruity. Lower acidity.
- Widely used in espresso blends (for body and crema) and instant coffee.

For home roasting beginners, **arabica single-origin coffees are the recommended starting point.** Their flavor profiles are cleaner and more expressive, which makes it much easier to taste what your roasting decisions are actually doing.

A quick rule of thumb: if you see "100% arabica" on specialty coffee packaging, it's a marker of quality intent. If a coffee doesn't specify, it may contain robusta.

## What Is a Green Coffee Bean?

A **green coffee bean** is a raw, unroasted coffee seed that has been processed, dried, milled, and sorted. It is not ready to brew — grinding and brewing green coffee produces something bitter, grassy, and unpleasant.

What green coffee *does* contain is everything that roasting will transform:
- **Sugars** — sucrose and other reducing sugars that will caramelize and participate in the Maillard reaction.
- **Amino acids** — proteins that react with sugars under heat to create aromatic compounds.
- **Chlorogenic acids** — a family of antioxidant compounds that contribute to bitterness and acidity when roasted.
- **Lipids** — oils that carry fat-soluble aromatics and emerge as surface oils in dark roasts.
- **Water** — 8–12% moisture content that must be driven off during roasting.

Green coffee beans can be stored for up to a year in proper conditions (cool, dark, dry) without significant degradation. This is dramatically longer than roasted coffee, which starts declining in quality within weeks. Home roasters typically buy green coffee in quantities of 1–5 pounds and roast small batches as needed.

---

## What to Look for in Green Coffee

When you receive green coffee, here's how to evaluate it before roasting:

- **Color:** Should be consistently pale green to blue-green. Yellow or brown patches can indicate age or defects.
- **Smell:** Grassy, hay-like, slightly vegetable. Not musty or sour — those suggest moisture damage.
- **Size and uniformity:** Beans of similar size roast more evenly. Large variance in size is a quality indicator to watch.
- **Defects:** Pick through a small batch and look for obviously black beans (over-fermented), shells (hollow beans), stones, or severely undersized beans. A few defects are normal; many suggest lower-grade green coffee.

---

## Why This Matters

Every decision you make as a roaster starts with the green bean. You cannot roast complexity into a defective or stale bean — you can only reveal what's already there. Choosing good green coffee is at least as important as good roasting technique.

Specialty coffee suppliers publish detailed information about their green coffee: the country, region, farm, altitude, processing method, variety, and often suggested roast level. Learning to read and use that information is part of becoming a thoughtful home roaster — and Sessions 02 and 03 together give you the vocabulary to do it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Coffee is a fruit; the "bean" is the seed inside a coffee cherry.
- The two main species are arabica (complex, fruity, preferred for specialty coffee) and robusta (earthy, bitter, higher caffeine, used in blends and instant coffee).
- Green coffee contains chemical precursors — sugars, amino acids, chlorogenic acids, lipids — that roasting will transform into flavor.
- Green coffee stores for up to a year; roasted coffee is best within 1–4 weeks.
- Quality green coffee from specialty suppliers comes with detailed origin information that helps you make better roasting decisions.
