# Session 04: The Roasting Process — Stages and Chemistry
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to describe the three stages of coffee roasting, explain the Maillard reaction and caramelization in plain terms, and identify what happens at each stage to the bean's color, smell, and temperature.

---

## Opening

You've seen bread brown in a toaster. You've watched a steak sear in a pan. You've smelled onions turn sweet after 20 minutes in butter. All of these involve the same underlying chemistry — and so does roasting coffee. The brown colors, the complex aromas, the depth of flavor that makes something more than the sum of its raw ingredients: these come from heat-triggered chemical reactions that are consistent, predictable, and learnable.

Understanding what is happening inside the bean as you roast doesn't require a chemistry degree. It requires knowing three things: what happens in each stage, what signals tell you where you are, and what can go wrong if you rush or stall.

---

## A Green Bean, Ready to Transform

Take a moment to think about what you learned in Sessions 02 and 03. A green coffee bean contains:
- Water (8–12%)
- Sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose)
- Amino acids (proteins)
- Chlorogenic acids (a family of antioxidants)
- Lipids (oils)
- Carbon dioxide, bound in the bean's structure

None of this tastes like roasted coffee. The flavor potential is there — but it needs heat to activate it. Roasting provides that heat in a controlled sequence over roughly 8–20 minutes depending on the method and equipment.

---

## The Three Stages of Roasting

### Stage 1: The Drying Phase

**Duration:** Approximately 30–45% of total roast time.
**Temperature:** Bean temperature climbs from ambient to roughly 300°F (150°C) during this stage.
**What's happening:** The primary goal is to drive off the bean's moisture. Green beans contain 8–12% water, and that water must evaporate before the flavor-creating reactions can begin. The bean is acting like a sponge that has to be wrung out before it can absorb heat effectively.

During drying, the bean changes color from green to pale yellow, and the aroma shifts from raw grain to something like baking bread or grain toasting. No significant flavor is being created yet — but everything depends on this stage.

- **Too short:** The exterior starts to heat while the interior is still wet. This causes scorching — the outside burns before the inside can develop, producing a harsh, grassy, sour result.
- **Too long (losing momentum):** The roast "bakes" — developing slowly and flatly, producing a papery, dull cup. This is called a **baked roast**, one of the most common defects.

**What you observe:** Color shift from green to yellow-gold. A grassy or hay-like aroma. Beans feel lighter as moisture escapes.

---

### Stage 2: The Browning Phase (Maillard and Caramelization)

**Duration:** Approximately 35–45% of total roast time.
**Temperature:** Approximately 300°F (150°C) up to the onset of first crack at roughly 385–400°F (196–204°C).
**What's happening:** This is where coffee flavor is born.

Two chemical families do the work:

**The Maillard reaction** — Named after French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, who first described it in 1912. This is a non-enzymatic chemical reaction between **amino acids** and **reducing sugars** that begins around 280°F (140°C). It is the same reaction responsible for the flavors of seared meat, freshly baked bread, and dark beer. In coffee, it produces hundreds of new aromatic compounds — caramel, nuts, chocolate, toast, and the distinctive "roasted coffee" aroma. The rate and duration of the Maillard phase directly shape the sweetness and complexity of the final cup.

**Caramelization** — Separate from the Maillard reaction, caramelization is the thermal decomposition of sugars under heat. It contributes sweetness and color. Excessive caramelization can create burnt, acrid flavors and destroys the dissolved sugars that contribute to sweetness — so this phase requires balance.

The bean color shifts from yellow through tan to progressively deeper shades of brown. Aroma becomes increasingly complex — bread, then toast, then nutty, then chocolatey. The roast is still **endothermic** (absorbing heat from the environment) during this phase.

**What you observe:** Progressive darkening. Increasingly rich, complex aroma. Slight expansion of bean size as moisture fully escapes.

---

### Stage 3: The Development Phase

**Duration:** Approximately 15–25% of total roast time.
**Temperature:** First crack (roughly 385–400°F / 196–204°C) through your chosen end-of-roast temperature.
**What's happening:** Development begins with an event called **first crack.**

**First crack** is one of the most important moments in roasting. As the bean's internal temperature rises, water vapor and CO₂ pressure builds inside the bean's structure. At a critical point, this pressure becomes too great and the bean's cell walls fracture — audibly. You will hear a series of popping sounds, somewhat like popcorn but lighter and more crisp. This is an **exothermic** reaction: the beans are now generating heat themselves, not just absorbing it.

First crack marks several important events simultaneously:
- The bean's cell structure opens up, allowing gases to escape and oils to migrate toward the surface.
- Caramelization peaks.
- The aromatic compounds responsible for sweetness and acidity form rapidly.
- The roast level is now determined by when you stop.

After first crack completes, you enter the development phase. This is a time-sensitive window. Roasting decisions here are measured in seconds and minutes:

- **End roast soon after first crack ends:** Light roast. High acidity, fruity or floral notes, origin character preserved.
- **Extend roast 1–3 minutes past first crack:** Medium roast. Balanced sweetness and body, caramel notes, lower acidity.
- **Continue until second crack:** Dark roast. Lower acidity, fuller body, bittersweet, roast flavors dominate over origin character.

**Second crack** occurs around 220°C (428°F). It sounds different from first crack — faster, hissing, and almost continuous. This is **pyrolysis**: the breakdown of the bean's cellulose and lignin structure. Surface oils emerge visibly. Specialty roasters usually stop before second crack to preserve the bean's origin character. Roasting well past second crack produces charcoal and bitter notes.

**What you observe:** Audible popping at first crack. Beans expand noticeably. Color deepens rapidly. Aroma becomes intensely "roasty." At second crack: beans look dark brown to black, surface oils visible.

## Flavor Diagnostics

The good news about understanding these stages is that when a roast goes wrong, the flavor tells you where it went wrong:

| What It Tastes Like | What Went Wrong |
|--------------------|----------------|
| Sour, grassy, harsh | Underdeveloped — drying or development phase too short |
| Flat, papery, dull | Baked — drying phase too slow; roast lost momentum |
| Thin, aggressively acidic | Maillard phase too abbreviated |
| Bitter, ashy, empty | Overdeveloped — past second crack, or development too long |
| Sweet, balanced, complex | All three phases managed well |

---

## Why This Matters

You don't need to be a chemist to apply this. What you need is to understand the sequence — three stages, each with a role — and to recognize the signals that tell you where you are: color, aroma, and sound. First crack is your most reliable landmark. The stages before it are about building toward that moment. The stage after it is where you make your creative decisions as a roaster.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Roasting has three stages: drying, browning (Maillard + caramelization), and development.
- The Maillard reaction creates most of coffee's complex flavor by reacting amino acids and sugars under heat.
- First crack is the pivotal exothermic event — it signals the start of the development phase and is the primary landmark for determining roast level.
- Development time after first crack determines roast level: shorter = lighter; longer = darker.
- Second crack signals pyrolysis — structural breakdown of the bean; specialty roasters typically stop before this point.
- Flavor defects are diagnostic: sour/grassy means underdeveloped; flat/papery means baked; bitter/ashy means overdeveloped.
