# Session 07: Tasting and Evaluating Your Coffee
**Course:** COFF-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to conduct a basic cupping session, use the SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel to describe what you taste, and connect tasting observations to specific roasting decisions.

---

## Opening

You have roasted your first batch. It has rested for at least 48 hours. Now comes the part that most beginner home roasters skip — and that separates the ones who improve quickly from the ones who plateau.

They skip systematic tasting. They brew the coffee, think "yeah, that's pretty good" or "hmm, that's a bit off," and move on without recording anything or connecting what they taste to what they did. Then they roast the next batch the same way and get the same result.

Systematic tasting is the difference between roasting and *learning to roast*. This session gives you the tools.

---

## What Is Cupping?

**Cupping** is the standardized method the specialty coffee industry uses to evaluate coffee. It removes brewing equipment variables — no espresso machine, no filter type, no pour rate — so that the coffee itself is what you're evaluating.

The Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) maintains the official cupping protocol. Here is a simplified version suitable for home use:

**What you need:**
- ~8g of coffee per cup (coarse grind)
- A grinder (if you have one)
- Small cups, bowls, or mugs (5–8 oz each)
- A kettle (water at approximately 93°C / 200°F)
- Two spoons (one for breaking the crust, one for tasting)
- Your roasting log and a notes page
- A timer

**The protocol:**
1. Grind ~8g of coffee coarsely for each sample.
2. Smell the dry grounds. Note the aroma.
3. Pour approximately 150mL of hot water (~93°C) directly over the grounds.
4. Start your timer.
5. At 4 minutes: break the crust (the floating layer of grounds) by pressing through it gently with a spoon. Lean in and inhale deeply — this is the peak aroma moment.
6. Skim the remaining floating grounds from the surface.
7. Wait for the coffee to cool to approximately 70°C (158°F) — about 5–7 minutes from your pour. Begin tasting.
8. Use a spoon to take a sip. **Slurp loudly** — this aerates the coffee and distributes it across all your taste receptors simultaneously.
9. Continue tasting as the coffee cools. Flavors shift noticeably as temperature drops.
10. Record observations at each stage.

## The SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel

The **Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel** is a visual tool developed by the SCA and World Coffee Research (WCR) to give coffee drinkers a shared vocabulary for describing flavor. It was last updated in 2016 and is grounded in the WCR Sensory Lexicon.

The wheel works from the center outward:
- **Center (innermost ring):** Broad flavor families — fruity, sweet, nutty/cocoa, spicy, roasted, green/vegetative, sour/fermented, floral
- **Middle ring:** More specific subcategories — within "fruity," for example: berry, dried fruit, citrus fruit, other fruit
- **Outer ring:** Specific descriptors — blueberry, raisin, lemon, peach

**How to use it:**
1. Start at the center. What is the broadest category of what you taste? "Fruity? Nutty? Roasted?"
2. Move outward. Within "fruity," is it more like berry or citrus?
3. Move to the outer ring for specific vocabulary. "Blueberry? Raspberry? Lemon?"
4. You don't have to reach the outer ring every time. "Berry-like" is a perfectly useful tasting note.

The flavor wheel is vocabulary, not a checklist. You don't need to identify every descriptor. Identify the ones that are present, in the order of their intensity.

**Get the wheel:** Download a free copy from sca.coffee or search "SCA Flavor Wheel PDF."

## What to Evaluate

When tasting your roasted coffee, evaluate these six attributes:

**1. Aroma**
Smell the dry grounds, the wet crust moment, and the brewed cup. What do you smell? Aroma often predicts flavor.

**2. Acidity**
The bright, clean, "lift" quality associated with fruit acids in coffee. Good acidity is like the brightness of a lemon or apple — not harshness. Sourness is different from acidity: sourness is a roast defect; acidity is a desirable attribute (in moderate amounts). How bright or flat is this coffee?

**3. Sweetness**
Does the coffee have a sense of sugar or caramel? Or is it absent or hollow? Sweetness is most noticeable in medium roasts; it diminishes in very light and very dark roasts.

**4. Body**
The physical sensation of weight in the mouth. A heavy, thick coffee has "full body" (like whole milk). A thin, watery coffee has "light body" (like water). Neither is automatically better — it depends on the origin and your preference.

**5. Aftertaste (Finish)**
What remains after you swallow? A pleasant finish lingers for a few seconds with positive flavor. A negative finish: bitterness, harshness, or emptiness.

**6. Balance**
Does everything work together? Can you identify one attribute that is overwhelming the others in an unpleasant way?

## Connecting Taste to Your Roast

This is the skill you are building. Here is the feedback map from the research:

| What It Tastes Like | Likely Roast Cause |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| Sour, grassy, harsh | Underdeveloped — development time too short |
| Flat, papery, dull | Baked — drying phase too slow; roast lost momentum |
| Thin, aggressively acidic | Maillard phase too brief |
| Bitter, ashy, harsh | Overdeveloped or roasted past second crack |
| Sweet, balanced, complex | Roast executed well — replicate it |

When you taste your first batch:
1. What is the dominant flavor attribute?
2. Is there a problem? If so, which defect category does it match?
3. What one adjustment would address it?
4. Write the adjustment in your log as a plan for your next roast.

## Cupping Multiple Coffees

For the most useful evaluation, cup 2–3 coffees side by side. This could be:
- Your Session 06 roast vs. a commercial specialty coffee you respect
- Two batches of the same bean at different roast levels
- Two different origins roasted to the same level

Side-by-side comparison is dramatically more useful than tasting one coffee in isolation — it calibrates your palate and makes differences visible that you'd miss alone.

**Cupping fatigue** is real: after 5–6 cups, your palate loses sensitivity. Limit sessions to 3–6 samples.

---

## Why This Matters

Roasting without tasting is like writing without reading what you wrote. The feedback loop — roast, taste, adjust — is the mechanism of improvement. There's no other way to get better. Your first cupping session will be imperfect. You might only taste "coffee." That's exactly where everyone starts. The flavor vocabulary builds over time, with practice.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Cupping is the standardized evaluation method: ~8g of coarsely ground coffee, 150mL of hot water, 4-minute steep, break the crust, slurp at ~70°C.
- Evaluate six attributes: aroma, acidity, sweetness, body, aftertaste, and balance.
- The SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel provides shared vocabulary from general (fruity) to specific (blueberry). Use it as vocabulary, not a checklist.
- Connect tasting observations to roasting decisions using the flavor defect map.
- Cup multiple coffees side by side — comparison is far more useful than isolation.
- Record everything: tasting notes without connection to roast logs are half as useful.
