## Core Concepts

Tasting is the feedback loop of home roasting. Without a systematic way to evaluate your roasted coffee, you cannot connect your roasting decisions to your results. **Cupping** is the standard method used by the specialty coffee industry to evaluate coffee consistently — and it is fully accessible to home roasters. [sca.coffee]

### What Is Cupping?

Cupping is a standardized brew method designed to isolate the flavor of the coffee itself, removing variables like brewing equipment. The SCA cupping protocol is the industry standard:

1. Grind 8.25g of coffee to a coarse, uniform grind.
2. Place in a 5–6 oz (150 mL) cupping bowl or small cup.
3. Add hot water (approximately 93°C / 200°F) to fill.
4. Wait 4 minutes. A crust of grounds will float on the surface.
5. "Break the crust" by pushing it gently with a spoon and inhaling — this is when aroma is most intense.
6. Skim the remaining grounds from the surface.
7. When the liquid has cooled to ~70°C (158°F), begin tasting. Slurp loudly — this aerates the coffee and spreads it across your palate.
8. Continue tasting as it cools. Flavors shift significantly at different temperatures.

[sca.coffee / achillescoffeeroasters.com]

### The SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel

Originally published in 1995, significantly revised in 2016 in partnership with World Coffee Research (WCR), the **Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel** is the reference tool for describing coffee flavor. The revision was grounded in the World Coffee Research Sensory Lexicon — developed by professional sensory panelists, scientists, and coffee buyers.

The wheel works from the center outward — general categories at the center (fruity, nutty, sweet, floral) become increasingly specific at the outer edge (blueberry, hazelnut, brown sugar, jasmine). When evaluating a coffee:
1. Start at the center. What broad family of flavors do you detect?
2. Move outward. Which specific descriptor fits best?
3. Use the wheel as vocabulary, not a checklist.

[sca.coffee]

### Building a Tasting Protocol for Home Roasters

For a home roaster, a simplified cupping practice:

1. **Brew consistently:** Use the same method (cupping or pour-over) for every evaluation batch.
2. **Wait the right amount of time:** Taste at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-roast. Note how flavors evolve.
3. **Evaluate these attributes:**
   - **Aroma:** What do you smell dry and when water is added?
   - **Acidity:** Is there a bright, citrus-like quality? Or is the cup flat?
   - **Sweetness:** Caramel? Sugar? Or is it absent?
   - **Body:** Does the coffee feel thick (full body) or light and watery (thin body)?
   - **Aftertaste / Finish:** Does the flavor linger pleasantly or does it disappear quickly? Is there bitterness?
   - **Balance:** Does everything work together, or does one element dominate?

4. **Compare:** Cup 2–3 coffees at once for relative comparison. "Cupping fatigue" limits most people to 6 per session.
5. **Write it down:** Tasting notes without documentation are lost. Even simple notes ("too sour — probably underdeveloped; try 30 more seconds of development") are enormously useful.

### Connecting Taste to Roast Decisions

This is the core skill of home roasting:

| Flavor Problem | Likely Roast Cause | Adjustment |
|---------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Sour, grassy, harsh | Underdeveloped (short development time) | Extend post-first-crack time by 20–30 sec |
| Flat, papery, dull | Baked (slow roast, loss of momentum) | Increase heat earlier in roast; shorten total time |
| Thin, overly acidic | Maillard phase too short | Slow down temperature rise in browning phase |
| Bitter, ashy, harsh | Overdeveloped or past second crack | End roast earlier |
| Balanced, sweet, complex | Well-managed | Replicate it exactly |

### Storing Roasted Coffee

Proper storage prevents the primary enemies of coffee freshness: oxygen, moisture, heat, and light.

- **Airtight container** with a one-way CO₂ valve is ideal — allows degassing without oxygen ingress.
- Keep beans at room temperature, in a dark cupboard or cabinet. Refrigeration is not recommended — condensation as beans warm up accelerates staling.
- **Do not freeze unless planning long-term storage** (freezing can work if beans are sealed in a vacuum and thawed only once).
- Use roasted beans within 2–4 weeks for optimal flavor. Dark roasts go stale faster because surface oils oxidize.
- **Do not grind until brewing** — ground coffee goes stale in hours; whole beans last weeks.

## Key Definitions

- **Cupping:** The standardized industry method for evaluating coffee flavor; uses a consistent brew ratio and protocol.
- **Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel:** SCA/WCR tool for identifying and naming coffee flavor attributes; revised 2016.
- **Aroma:** The smell of coffee — both dry (before water) and wet (after brewing). Highly predictive of flavor.
- **Acidity:** The bright, crisp quality associated with fruit acids in coffee. Not the same as "sour" — acidity is desirable; sourness indicates a roast defect or extraction problem.
- **Body:** The perceived weight or thickness of coffee on the palate (light, medium, full).
- **Aftertaste:** The flavor that lingers after swallowing; can reveal defects not apparent on the palate.
- **Balance:** The overall harmony of acidity, sweetness, body, and bitterness.
- **Flavor wheel:** A visual vocabulary tool for naming flavors from general (fruity) to specific (blueberry).
- **Cupping fatigue:** Sensory overload from evaluating too many coffees — limits sessions to 3–6 samples for most people.
- **Degassing:** Post-roast CO₂ release from beans; affects extraction and flavor if brewed too soon.
- **Staling:** Flavor degradation from oxygen exposure after roasting.

## Process / Mechanics

Cupping protocol (simplified for home use):
1. Grind: ~8g coffee per cup, coarse grind.
2. Add ~150 mL water at 93°C (200°F).
3. Wait 4 minutes.
4. Break crust; inhale.
5. Skim remaining grounds.
6. Taste at ~70°C, then at room temperature.
7. Record: aroma, acidity, sweetness, body, aftertaste, overall impression.
8. Compare roast log to tasting notes. Identify one adjustment for next roast.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Tasting immediately after roasting — CO₂ dominates; flavors are muted. Wait 48 hours minimum.
- Not tasting comparatively — tasting one coffee in isolation makes it hard to know what "balanced" means. Cup at least two at once.
- Describing everything as "good" or "strong" — specificity is the goal. Use the flavor wheel.
- Not recording tasting notes — the roast log and the tasting notes must be connected to be useful.
- Grinding too early — pre-grinding a tasting batch hours before evaluation lets it stale.

## Hands-On Elements

- Conduct your first cupping session: roast two identical batches of the same green coffee, end one 30 seconds earlier than the other. Cup them side-by-side at 48 hours. Document every difference you notice.
- Download a copy of the SCA Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel (available free from sca.coffee). Keep it next to your cupping setup.
- Design a simple tasting log template: date, roast ID, aroma notes, acidity (1–5), sweetness (1–5), body (1–5), aftertaste, overall score, and what you'd change next time.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- **Subjectivity vs. standardization:** The SCA cupping protocol creates consistency, but flavor perception is individual. Some practitioners argue for more emphasis on personal vocabulary and less on universal descriptors.
- **When to drink:** Specialty roasters disagree on optimal degassing time — some say 4 days for pour-over, others say 10+ days. The ideal window depends on the roast level and brewing method.
- **Grind freshness:** Industry consensus is to grind immediately before brewing; some argue a short rest (30 minutes to 1 hour) after grinding improves certain pour-over methods.
