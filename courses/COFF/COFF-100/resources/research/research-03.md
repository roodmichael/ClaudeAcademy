## Core Concepts

Roasting is a heat-transformation process. A green coffee bean contains water, sugars, amino acids, chlorogenic acids, lipids, and volatile compounds. Heat rearranges all of these into the aromatics, colors, and flavors of roasted coffee. Two main chemical families are responsible for most of the flavor: **Maillard reaction products** and **caramelization products**. [sucafina.com, torque.coffee]

### The Three Stages of Roasting

**Stage 1 — Drying Phase (approximately 30–45% of total roast time)**
- Temperature range: roughly 375–420°F (190–215°C) in the drum or air stream.
- Green beans contain 8–12% residual moisture. This moisture must evaporate before flavor-creating chemistry can begin.
- Beans transition from green to pale yellow.
- No flavor is created in this stage, but everything depends on it: too short and the exterior scorches while the interior remains raw (producing sour, grassy notes); too long and the roast "bakes" — losing momentum and resulting in flat, papery flavor.
- This stage is endothermic: the bean absorbs heat from the environment.

**Stage 2 — Browning / Maillard Phase (approximately 35–45% of total roast time)**
- Temperature range: approximately 300°F (150°C) up to first crack at around 385–400°F (196–204°C).
- The **Maillard reaction** begins: amino acids and reducing sugars interact to form hundreds of new aromatic compounds. Color shifts from yellow through tan to rich brown. Nutty, caramel, chocolate, and bread aromas emerge.
- **Caramelization** occurs simultaneously: sugars break down under heat, contributing sweetness and additional color.
- The rate of temperature rise (called **Rate of Rise** or **RoR**) during this phase directly controls acidity and sweetness development. A well-managed Maillard phase yields balanced, complex flavors.
- This stage is also still endothermic.

**Stage 3 — Development Phase (approximately 15–25% of total roast time)**
- Begins at **first crack** — the audible, exothermic moment when steam and CO₂ build enough pressure to rupture the bean's cell walls.
- First crack sounds like popcorn popping. It is the most important landmark in home roasting — you can hear and sometimes smell when it begins.
- After first crack, the development phase determines the final roast level. Light roasts end close to first crack; medium roasts extend development; dark roasts push toward or past **second crack**.
- **Second crack** (approximately 220°C / 428°F) is a more rapid, hissing/crackling sound — pyrolysis (the breakdown of the bean's cellulose structure). Surface oils emerge. Origin character diminishes in favor of roast character (smoky, bitter, dark chocolate, charcoal notes).
- Specialty roasters generally stop before or just at second crack to preserve the bean's origin flavors.

[torque.coffee, sucafina.com]

### Roast Levels

Roast level is determined by when you stop the roast in relation to these phases:

| Roast Level | Stop Point | Flavor Character |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Light | Shortly after first crack | High acidity, fruity/floral, preserves origin character |
| Medium-Light | 1–2 min after first crack | Balanced acidity and sweetness, some caramel |
| Medium | Well after first crack, before second | Balanced body and sweetness, lower acidity |
| Medium-Dark | Onset of second crack | Full body, lower acidity, bittersweet chocolate |
| Dark | Well into second crack | Low acidity, oily, bitter, roast flavors dominant |

[perfectdailygrind.com, torque.coffee]

### Flavor Diagnostics

If a roast goes wrong, the flavor tells you why:
- **Sour / grassy:** Underdeveloped — drying or development phase too short.
- **Flat / papery:** Baked — drying phase too slow, roast lost momentum.
- **Thin / sharp:** Abbreviated Maillard phase.
- **Ashy / bitter:** Development phase too long.
- **Sweet, balanced, complex:** All three phases managed well.

[torque.coffee]

### Degassing

After roasting, beans release CO₂ for 12–72 hours (sometimes longer for light roasts). This is called **degassing** or **off-gassing**. Brewing immediately after roasting produces flat, gassy coffee. Waiting 1–3 days minimum is standard; many specialty roasters recommend 5–10 days for filter coffee, 7–14 days for espresso. [perfectdailygrind.com]

### The Roasting Log

Recording time, temperature, and observations for every batch is the foundation of improvement. Key variables to track:
- Start temperature
- Time to first crack
- Total roast time
- Final bean temperature (if measurable)
- Batch weight before/after (weight loss = water evaporated = proxy for roast level)

## Key Definitions

- **Maillard reaction:** A non-enzymatic chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars under heat; responsible for browning and most coffee flavor compounds. Begins around 280°F (140°C).
- **Caramelization:** Thermal decomposition of sugars; contributes sweetness, color, and caramel flavor notes. Occurs alongside the Maillard reaction.
- **First crack:** The audible, exothermic fracturing of bean cell walls, releasing steam and CO₂. The defining landmark of the roast.
- **Second crack:** Pyrolytic breakdown of cellulose and lignin. Signals dark roast territory. Surface oils emerge.
- **Pyrolysis:** Heat-induced chemical breakdown of organic material — in roasting, this refers to the structural breakdown that begins at second crack.
- **Rate of Rise (RoR):** How fast the bean temperature is increasing at any given moment; a key control variable for professional roasters.
- **Degassing (off-gassing):** The release of CO₂ from freshly roasted beans over 1–72+ hours.
- **Development phase:** The period from first crack to end of roast; determines final flavor balance and roast level.
- **Exothermic:** Releases heat (first crack onward — beans generate heat themselves).
- **Endothermic:** Absorbs heat (drying and early browning phases — beans draw energy from the roaster).

## Process / Mechanics

1. Preheat roasting equipment to target start temperature.
2. Add green coffee (respect batch size limits).
3. Drying phase: monitor color shift from green → yellow → tan; ensure continuous bean movement.
4. Browning phase: observe color deepen to brown; track time.
5. First crack: listen carefully. Note the time. Begin tracking development time.
6. Stop roast at target development time / color / temperature.
7. Cool immediately and quickly — beans continue cooking from retained heat if not cooled fast.
8. Degas in a breathable or vented container for minimum 12 hours; ideally 1–3 days.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Not recognizing first crack, or confusing it with equipment noise. Solution: practice active listening; first crack is loud and distinct.
- Ending the roast before first crack finishes — produces underdeveloped, sour coffee.
- Roasting too fast (excessive heat) — scorches bean exterior while interior remains raw.
- Failing to cool quickly enough — retained heat continues the roast after removal.
- Brewing immediately after roasting — CO₂ causes flat, gassy results.
- No roast log — makes it impossible to replicate or improve results.

## Hands-On Elements

- Time your first roast in 30-second intervals: note the color and aroma at each checkpoint.
- Listen specifically for first crack. Record the exact minute it begins. Record when it ends.
- After your first batch, weigh the green beans and the roasted beans. Calculate weight loss percentage (typically 12–20%; higher = darker roast).
- Wait 48 hours after roasting, then brew and taste. Compare to brewing at 24 hours and at 72 hours.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- **Optimal development time:** Practitioners disagree on the ideal development time as a percentage of total roast. Many specialty roasters target 20–25%; some prefer shorter for delicate light roasts. No universal answer.
- **Rate of Rise management:** Advanced roasters actively adjust heat to create a declining Rate of Rise through the roast. This is beyond beginner scope but worth knowing exists.
- **Dark roast quality:** Some argue dark roasting destroys origin character and masks poor-quality beans. Others consider it a legitimate craft. This is a genuine debate in the specialty community.
