## Core Concepts

Home roasting does not require expensive equipment to start. The main variable is how much control you want over the roast. Every method has tradeoffs between cost, control, consistency, batch size, and smoke management. [coffeebeancorral.com, roastar.com]

### Method 1: Stovetop Skillet or Wok

The most primitive and lowest-cost method — uses a pan you already own.

- **How it works:** Heat the pan over medium-high heat. Add green beans. Stir constantly with a wooden spoon or whisk to prevent scorching. Listen for first crack. Remove from heat at target roast level. Transfer to a colander for cooling.
- **Pros:** Zero equipment cost. Develops an intuitive feel for roasting quickly.
- **Cons:** Very inconsistent results. Produces heavy smoke. Hard to achieve even roasts. Steep learning curve. Not recommended as a long-term method.
- **Batch size:** Typically 4–6 oz (100–170g) at a time.
- **Best for:** Complete beginners who want to experience roasting before any investment.

[homesteadingfamily.com]

### Method 2: Electric Popcorn Popper (Air Popper)

The most commonly recommended beginner method in the home roasting community.

- **How it works:** Hot air enters from side vents, swirling beans and chaff upward. The same convection principle as an air roaster, but in a $20–$40 appliance.
- **Critical selection note:** You must use a side-venting popper, not one with a screen at the bottom. Side-venting poppers keep beans agitated and chaff moving out. The Poppery II is a historically recommended model (rated 1,200 watts); availability varies.
- **Pros:** Inexpensive. Fast roasts (8–12 minutes). Easy to see and hear what's happening. Good entry point before committing to a dedicated roaster.
- **Cons:** Small batch size (typically 3–4 oz / 85–115g). Not built for repeated roasting; motor may burn out with heavy use. Limited heat control.
- **Batch size:** 3–4 oz (85–115g).
- **Best for:** First 10–20 roasts; learning what first crack sounds like and how roast color develops.

[library.sweetmarias.com, coffeebeancorral.com]

### Method 3: Dedicated Home Coffee Roaster (Air / Fluid Bed)

Purpose-built machines using hot air to roast beans.

- **Examples:** Fresh Roast SR540, Fresh Roast SR800, Sandbox Smart R1.
- **How it works:** Beans are suspended and tumbled in a column of hot air inside a glass chamber. Most models allow airflow and temperature adjustment.
- **Pros:** Designed for repeated use. Chaff collection built in. Visible roasting chamber. Better control than a popcorn popper. Clear first-crack audio.
- **Cons:** Still limited batch size. Higher cost ($150–$350). Produces smoke — ventilation required.
- **Batch size:** Typically 4–8 oz (115–225g).
- **Best for:** Hobbyists who have confirmed they enjoy roasting and want more consistency and control.

[coffeebeancorral.com, roastar.com]

### Method 4: Drum Roaster

Tumbles beans in a rotating drum over a heat source (gas or electric).

- **Examples:** Behmor 2000AB Plus, Hottop, Aillio Bullet (prosumer).
- **How it works:** Beans tumble in a perforated drum. Heat is applied from below or around the drum. Slower heat transfer than air roasters.
- **Pros:** Larger batch sizes. More even roasting. More control over the roast curve. Fuller body in resulting cup.
- **Cons:** Significantly more expensive ($300–$2,500+). Longer roast times (14–20 minutes). Steeper learning curve. Heavier machine.
- **Batch size:** 8 oz–1 lb (225g–450g) for home models.
- **Best for:** Serious enthusiasts who want commercial-adjacent control and larger batches.

[roastar.com]

### Method 5: Oven Roasting

Spread green beans on a sheet pan; roast at high heat.

- **Pros:** No additional equipment.
- **Cons:** Very uneven roasting (hot spots). Hard to hear first crack through the oven. Poor heat control. Produces significant smoke. Generally not recommended.

### Essential Supporting Equipment (All Methods)

- **Kitchen scale:** Weigh green beans before and after roasting. Weight loss is a proxy for roast level (12–15% = light/medium; 16–20% = medium-dark/dark). Highest priority purchase for any method.
- **Timer:** Essential for tracking roast stages and first crack timing.
- **Colander or cooling tray:** Transfer roasted beans immediately for rapid cooling. Stirring during cooling stops residual heat from continuing the roast.
- **Storage containers:** Airtight containers with one-way CO₂ valves (like mason jars with valve lids) are ideal for degassing and storing roasted beans.
- **Roasting log (paper or digital):** Track every roast — method, batch weight, start time, first crack time, end time, color assessment, and tasting notes.
- **Thermometer (optional):** Not always accessible in simpler setups, but useful for stovetop and oven methods.
- **Coffee color card (optional):** Physical reference cards for assessing roast degree by bean color.

[roastar.com, perfectdailygrind.com]

### Smoke Management

All home roasting methods produce smoke — some more than others. Essential precautions:
- Roast near an open window or use a range hood fan.
- Some home roasters roast outdoors (adjust roast times — ambient cold air extends drying phase).
- Never leave a roast unattended.
- Clean equipment after every session — chaff and oil buildup is a fire hazard and affects flavor.

## Key Definitions

- **Air roaster (fluid bed roaster):** Uses convection (moving hot air) to roast beans; fast, even, highlights brightness.
- **Drum roaster:** Tumbles beans in a rotating drum; slower, fuller body, more control.
- **Chaff:** The thin silver skin that separates from coffee beans during roasting; must be collected or vented.
- **Batch size:** Weight of green beans loaded into the roaster; staying within equipment limits is essential for even roasting.
- **Cooling tray / colander:** Used to rapidly cool beans after roasting; prevents residual heat from continuing the roast.
- **One-way valve bag/lid:** Allows CO₂ to escape from freshly roasted beans without letting oxygen in.
- **Roast log:** A record of every roast — the home roaster's most important tool for improvement.

## Process / Mechanics

For all methods:
1. Preheat equipment.
2. Load green beans at correct batch weight.
3. Apply heat; begin timing.
4. Monitor actively — color, aroma, sound.
5. Listen for first crack; record the time.
6. End roast at target point.
7. Transfer to cooling vessel immediately.
8. Stir or agitate during cooling.
9. Store in airtight container; allow 12–72 hours degassing.
10. Log everything.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Using a bottom-vent popcorn popper — beans sit on the screen and burn unevenly.
- Overloading the roaster — prevents proper bean movement and causes uneven roasting.
- Not preheating the roaster before adding beans.
- Roasting indoors without ventilation — the smoke alarm will alert you to this mistake.
- Skipping the cooling step — beans continue to roast in their own heat.
- Not cleaning after each session — chaff is flammable; oil residue creates off-flavors.

## Hands-On Elements

- Start with a popcorn popper or stovetop method for the first 3–5 roasts before investing in dedicated equipment.
- Weigh your green beans before every roast. Weigh the roasted beans immediately after cooling. Record the difference. Over time, you'll see how weight loss correlates to roast level and flavor.
- Keep a simple log on paper for your first 10 roasts: date, method, batch size, roast time, first crack minute, roast color (light/medium/dark), and tasting notes 48 hours later.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- **Air vs. drum for beginners:** Community opinion is split. Air roasters teach you to pay attention through transparency (you can see the beans); drum roasters teach you through sound and smell. Both are valid starting points above a certain budget.
- **Batch size and quality:** Smaller batches are easier to cool quickly and more forgiving of mistakes; larger batches roast more evenly once technique is established.
