# Glossary — COFF-100: Home Coffee Roasting Foundations

All terms appear in session lessons. Definitions are written in plain language. Terms defined in-lesson are noted with the session where they first appear.

---

**Acidity** (Session 07)
A desirable coffee attribute — the bright, clean, citrus-like quality associated with fruit acids. Not the same as "sour," which is a defect. High-acid coffees feel lively and crisp; low-acid coffees feel rounder and flatter. Most noticeable in lighter roasts and washed processing.

**Aftertaste** (Session 07)
The flavor that remains after swallowing coffee. A good aftertaste lingers pleasantly for several seconds; a negative aftertaste leaves bitterness, harshness, or emptiness.

**Alfred Peet** (Session 01)
Founder of Peet's Coffee (Berkeley, 1966); credited as the father of specialty coffee in the United States. He emphasized freshness and craftsmanship and mentored the founders of Starbucks.

**Amino acids** (Session 04)
Protein building blocks present in green coffee beans that react with reducing sugars during the Maillard reaction to create hundreds of aromatic flavor compounds.

**Arabica (Coffea arabica)** (Session 02)
The higher-quality of the two main coffee species. Accounts for approximately 60% of world production. Grown at 1,000–2,000m altitude. Lower caffeine than robusta. Flavor profile: fruity, floral, sweet, complex. Descended from plants cultivated in Ethiopia and Yemen.

**Aroma** (Session 07)
The smell of coffee — both dry (before water) and wet (after brewing). Aroma is highly predictive of flavor and is evaluated at multiple points during cupping: dry grounds, wet crust, and the brewed cup.

**Baked roast** (Session 04)
A roast defect caused by a drying phase that progresses too slowly or stalls, causing the roast to lose momentum. The beans develop without adequate energy, producing flat, papery, dull flavors with no sweetness or acidity.

**Balance** (Session 07)
The overall harmony of a coffee's flavor attributes — acidity, sweetness, body, and bitterness working together without any one element dominating unpleasantly.

**Batch size** (Session 04, 06)
The weight of green coffee loaded into the roasting equipment. Exceeding equipment batch size limits reduces airflow, prevents proper bean movement, and causes uneven roasting.

**Body** (Session 07)
The perceived weight or thickness of coffee on the palate. A full-bodied coffee feels thick and rich; a light-bodied coffee feels thin and watery. Neither is automatically better — depends on origin and preference.

**Caramelization** (Session 04)
The thermal decomposition of sugars under heat. Occurs simultaneously with the Maillard reaction during the browning phase. Contributes sweetness and color. Excessive caramelization creates bitter, acrid flavors.

**Chaff** (Session 06)
The thin silver skin (silverskin) that separates from coffee beans during roasting. It is light and flammable. Must be collected or vented during roasting and cleaned from equipment after each session.

**Chlorogenic acids** (Session 02)
A family of antioxidant compounds found in green coffee. They contribute to bitterness and acidity when transformed by roasting heat.

**Coffee belt** (Session 03)
The equatorial band — roughly 25° north to 30° south latitude — where climate, rainfall, and temperature conditions support coffee cultivation.

**Coffee Taster's Flavor Wheel** (Session 07)
A visual vocabulary tool developed by the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) and World Coffee Research (WCR), last updated in 2016. Organizes coffee flavor descriptors from general (center: fruity, nutty, roasted) to specific (outer ring: blueberry, hazelnut, smoke). Used during cupping to build shared vocabulary.

**Cupping** (Session 07)
The standardized industry method for evaluating coffee flavor. Involves brewing approximately 8g of coarsely ground coffee in 150mL of hot water, steeping 4 minutes, breaking the crust, and tasting systematically as the cup cools. Removes brewing equipment variables to isolate the coffee's flavor.

**Cupping fatigue** (Session 07)
Sensory overload from evaluating too many coffees in one session. Most people lose palate sensitivity after 5–6 samples. Recommended session limit: 3–6 coffees.

**Degassing (off-gassing)** (Session 03, 06)
The release of CO₂ from freshly roasted beans over 12–72 hours (sometimes longer). Brewing immediately after roasting produces flat, gassy results. Waiting 48 hours minimum before brewing is strongly recommended; 72 hours is ideal for many coffees.

**Development phase** (Session 04)
The third and final stage of roasting, beginning at first crack and ending when the roaster stops the roast. Determines final roast level. Duration and temperature during development set the balance between acidity, sweetness, body, and roast character.

**Development time** (Session 04, 05)
The amount of time from first crack start to the end of the roast. A key variable for determining roast level. Shorter = lighter; longer = darker.

**Endothermic** (Session 04)
Absorbs heat from the environment. The drying and browning phases of roasting are endothermic — the beans draw energy from the roaster's heat source.

**Exocarp** (Session 02)
The outer skin of the coffee cherry.

**Exothermic** (Session 04, 05)
Releases heat. First crack is an exothermic event — the beans generate heat themselves as cell walls fracture.

**First crack** (Session 04, 05)
The audible, exothermic fracturing of coffee bean cell walls that begins the development phase. Sounds like lighter, less forceful popcorn popping. Typically unfolds over 1–3 minutes. The primary timing landmark for determining roast level.

**First wave** (Session 01)
The era of mass-produced, commodity coffee: pre-ground, canned, prioritizing convenience over quality. Associated with brands like Folgers and Maxwell House.

**Green coffee** (Session 02)
Raw, unroasted coffee beans that have been processed, dried, milled, and sorted. Contains chemical precursors (sugars, amino acids, chlorogenic acids, lipids) that roasting transforms into flavor. Can be stored for up to one year in proper conditions.

**Honey process** (Session 03)
A coffee processing method in which the cherry skin is removed but the sticky mucilage layer is retained on the bean during drying. Produces a cup that is sweeter and fuller-bodied than washed, but cleaner than natural. Pioneered in Costa Rica. Variations: white, yellow, red, black honey.

**Maillard reaction** (Session 04)
A non-enzymatic chemical reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars that begins around 280°F (140°C). Named after French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard. Produces hundreds of aromatic compounds responsible for roasted coffee's complex flavors — caramel, nuts, chocolate, toast. The most important flavor-creating reaction in roasting.

**Mesocarp** (Session 02)
The sweet pulp layer inside the coffee cherry, between the outer skin (exocarp) and the mucilage.

**Mucilage** (Session 02, 03)
A sticky, sugary layer surrounding the parchment inside a coffee cherry. In honey processing, some or all of the mucilage is retained during drying to add sweetness and body to the cup.

**Natural process (dry process)** (Session 03)
A coffee processing method in which whole cherries dry in the sun with the fruit intact for 3–6 weeks. The fruit's sugars and flavors infuse the bean, producing fruity, sweet, wine-like cup profiles. Common in Ethiopia and Brazil.

**One-way valve bag/lid** (Session 06)
Storage container or lid that allows CO₂ to escape from freshly roasted beans without letting oxygen in. Ideal for degassing and storing roasted coffee while preventing oxidation.

**Origin** (Session 03)
The geographic source of a coffee — country, region, or specific farm. Origin influences flavor through altitude, climate, soil, and variety (terroir).

**Parchment (endocarp)** (Session 02)
A papery layer surrounding the coffee seed inside the cherry. Removed during milling before export.

**Peaberry** (Session 02)
A coffee bean formed when only one seed develops inside the cherry instead of two. The single seed grows rounder than a typical flat-sided bean. Not a defect — often prized for uniform roasting properties.

**Processing method** (Session 03)
How the coffee cherry is handled after harvest to extract and prepare the bean for export: washed, natural, or honey are the three main methods. Processing method significantly affects the flavor of the green coffee.

**Pyrolysis** (Session 04, 05)
Heat-induced chemical breakdown of organic material. In coffee roasting, pyrolysis refers to the structural breakdown of cellulose and lignin that begins at second crack (~220°C / 428°F). This strips away origin character and produces roast-dominant flavors.

**Qahveh khaneh** (Session 01)
Early Arabic coffeehouses that served as social, intellectual, and commercial gathering places. Nicknamed "Schools of the Wise." Emerged on the Arabian Peninsula in the 15th century.

**Rate of Rise (RoR)** (Session 04, Research-03)
How quickly bean temperature is increasing at any given moment during a roast. A key variable for experienced roasters managing flavor development. Not directly accessible in most beginner setups.

**Robusta (Coffea canephora)** (Session 02)
The second major coffee species. Accounts for approximately 40% of world production. Grows at lower altitudes, more disease-resistant, higher yield. Higher caffeine than arabica. Flavor: earthy, woody, bitter, less fruity. Used in espresso blends and instant coffee.

**Roast log** (Session 06, 08)
A record of every roast — including pre-roast intent, in-roast observations, post-roast measurements, tasting notes, and adjustment plans. The home roaster's most important tool for systematic improvement.

**Sample pack** (Session 03)
A collection of small quantities of different green coffees from a specialty supplier. Allows beginners to explore multiple origins before committing to larger quantities.

**Second crack** (Session 04, 05)
An audible event occurring around 220°C (428°F) signaling pyrolysis — the structural breakdown of the bean's cellulose and lignin. Sounds faster and more hissing than first crack. Surface oils emerge. Specialty roasters typically stop before this point.

**Silverskin** (Session 02, 06)
The thin inner skin of the coffee seed that becomes chaff during roasting. See "Chaff."

**Single origin** (Session 03)
Coffee sourced from one defined location — a country, region, or specific farm. Allows flavor characteristics specific to that origin to be clearly expressed and evaluated.

**Specialty coffee** (Session 01)
Coffee scored 80 or above on the SCA's 100-point cupping scale; refers to the premium segment of the market emphasizing origin transparency, quality processing, and freshness. Sometimes used loosely in marketing.

**Staling** (Session 07)
Flavor degradation of roasted coffee from oxygen exposure. Begins immediately after roasting; significant loss of quality after 2–4 weeks in whole-bean form, hours in pre-ground form.

**Terroir** (Session 03)
Borrowed from wine: the combination of environmental factors — soil, altitude, climate, rainfall — that give a coffee its place-based flavor character. The reason coffees from different origins taste different even when roasted identically.

**Third wave** (Session 01)
The current era of specialty coffee culture: treating coffee like wine — emphasizing specific origins, processing methods, farm relationships, and roast transparency. Emerging from the 2000s onward.

**Washed process (wet process)** (Session 03)
A coffee processing method in which cherry skin and most pulp are removed immediately after harvest, and the beans are fermented in water before drying. Produces clean, bright, high-acidity cups that clearly express the bean's intrinsic character. Common in Central America, East Africa, and Colombia.

**Weight loss percentage** (Session 06)
(Green weight − Roasted weight) ÷ Green weight × 100. A proxy for roast level: 12–15% typically indicates a light roast; 15–17% medium; 17–20%+ medium-dark to dark.
