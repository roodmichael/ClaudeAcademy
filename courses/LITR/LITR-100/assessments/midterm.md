# Midterm Assessment — LITR-100
**Covers:** Sessions 01–04

## Overview

The midterm asks you to write a short analytical essay (500–700 words) responding to
a specific prompt drawn from the Age of the Gods material. Sessions 01–04 have covered
the Kojiki's origins, the cosmogony, the Ama-no-Iwato episode, and the kuni-yuzuri.
You now have enough context to make a sustained argument about how a specific mythological
episode functions as both narrative and political argument.

This is a low-stakes essay — its purpose is to give you practice with the analytical
skills you will need for the final project, and to let you and your tutor identify any
gaps in your understanding before the course concludes.

---

## The Task

**Choose one of the following prompts and write a focused analytical essay of 500–700 words.**

---

**Prompt A: The Ama-no-Iwato**

The Ama-no-Iwato (the Heavenly Rock Cave episode) is often described as the "heart" of
the Kojiki's mythological section. In your essay, analyze what this episode accomplishes
both as narrative and as political argument. What makes it structurally satisfying as a
story? What specific political work does it perform for the Yamato court that commissioned
the text? Use specific details from the text (not just summary) to support your claims.

---

**Prompt B: The Kuni-yuzuri as Political Charter**

The kuni-yuzuri (land cession) episode is not simply a myth about a peaceful transfer of
divine authority. Scholars have argued it is a political charter — a narrative encoding
the historical relationship between the Yamato court and the Izumo region. Do you agree?
In your essay, analyze the episode as a political document. What does it claim? Who does
it benefit? What does it ask the reader (or listener) to accept? And does acknowledging
its political function change how you read it as a story?

---

**Prompt C: Mythological Explanation as Political Argument**

The Kojiki consistently explains how things came to be the way they are — why the moon
and sun don't share the sky, why humans die, why Izumo's chief deity lives in a great
shrine rather than ruling the land. Choose two of these "explanation episodes" (you may
use any from Sessions 01–04) and analyze how they function. What do they explain at the
surface? What do they legitimate at the political level? What does this pattern of
embedded explanation tell you about how mythological texts work?

---

## What to Submit

Write your essay and share it with the Tutor. Include:

1. Which prompt you chose (A, B, or C)
2. Your essay (500–700 words)
3. One or two brief quotations or paraphrases from the Chamberlain text that you are
   analyzing — these should be specific passages, not summaries

You do not need footnotes or a bibliography for this essay. You do need specific textual
evidence — general claims without textual grounding will be the most common weakness.

---

## Evaluation Criteria

- **Thesis clarity**: Does the essay make a specific claim that goes beyond summary?
- **Textual evidence**: Does the essay use specific moments from the text to support its
  claims, rather than just retelling the story?
- **Political/ideological analysis**: Does the essay engage with the text's function as
  a political argument, not just its narrative content?
- **Analytical precision**: Does the essay distinguish between what the text says and what
  it does?

---

## Tutor Scoring Guide

**Strong response**: Makes a clear claim in the first paragraph and sustains it across
the essay. Uses specific textual moments (not just general narrative summary) as evidence.
Engages with the political dimension — not just "what happens" but "what this episode is
doing in the context of the 712 CE court." Makes at least one analytical observation that
shows the student is thinking for themselves rather than reproducing lesson content.

**Common weaknesses to watch for**:
- **Summary instead of analysis**: The student retells the episode without making an
  argument about it. Prompt: "You've described what happens well. Now: what is the
  episode *for*? What would be missing if this episode weren't in the text?"
- **Missing the political dimension**: The student analyzes the narrative well but
  treats it as pure mythology without political function. Prompt: "This is compiled in
  712 CE for Empress Genmei. How does that context change how you read this episode?"
- **Vague thesis**: "The episode is important because it shows how myths work" — prompt
  for specificity: important *how*? Shows *what* about how myths work?

**Constructive feedback approach**: Always start with what the student did well. Identify
the one analytical move they are closest to making but haven't fully made. Ask a Socratic
question to push them there rather than supplying the answer.
