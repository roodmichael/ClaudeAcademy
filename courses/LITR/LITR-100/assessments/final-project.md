# Final Project — LITR-100
**Covers:** The full course arc (Sessions 01–08)

## Overview

The final project asks you to write a comparative or argumentative essay (800–1,200 words)
that synthesizes the full scope of the course. You have now read the Kojiki closely,
encountered its major episodes and figures, analyzed its political foundations, studied its
embedded poetry, and traced its complex reception history.

The final essay asks you to bring these layers together — to write as a reader who can
hold the text, the translator, the commentators, and the consequences simultaneously.

---

## The Task

**Choose one of the following prompts and write a focused essay of 800–1,200 words.**

---

**Prompt A: The Layers of the Text**

Chamberlain himself is a layer of the Kojiki you are reading — his Victorian prose, his
Latin euphemisms, his footnotes, his assumptions. Norinaga is another layer. Yasumaro
is another. And beneath all of them are the poets who composed the songs that Yasumaro
embedded in his chronicle.

Write an essay arguing: when a modern reader sits down with Chamberlain's 1882 translation
of the Kojiki, what text are they actually reading? Whose choices, assumptions, and
interpretations are present on the page? Use specific examples from across the course
to illustrate your argument. Your essay should have a clear position, not just a catalogue
of layers.

---

**Prompt B: Poetry and Prose — Two Voices in One Text**

The Kojiki is written in two distinct registers: prose narrative and embedded poetry.
This course has argued that the poems are not ornamental — they are the text's oldest
layer and do work that the prose cannot.

Write an essay that tests this claim. Select two or three poems from across the text
(they can be from any section you have read) and analyze them alongside the prose
narrative they are embedded in. What specifically does each poem do that the surrounding
prose cannot? Is the relationship between poem and prose consistent across the text, or
does it vary? What does your analysis suggest about how the Kojiki was composed and what
it was for?

---

**Prompt C: The Uses and Misuses of a Myth**

The Kojiki has been read as sacred history, as political propaganda, as authentic
national tradition, as dangerous nationalism, and as literary art. It has been revered,
weaponized, banned, rehabilitated, and reinterpreted.

Write an essay arguing: what *should* a contemporary reader make of the Kojiki? Not
whether the myths are "true" — but what kind of reading does the text merit, and what
does it offer a reader who is neither Japanese nor invested in any of the ideological
uses it has been put to? Your argument should engage with at least two of the historical
moments in the Kojiki's reception (Norinaga, State Shinto, post-WWII) and should
be grounded in specific episodes from the text itself.

---

**Prompt D: A Comparative Essay (advanced option)**

Choose one major episode from the Kojiki and one episode from another founding or origin
text from another tradition that you know well (examples: the Genesis creation narrative,
an episode from the Iliad or Aeneid, a creation myth from another culture, a passage from
the Hebrew scriptures). Write a comparative essay analyzing what the two episodes share
and where they diverge in structure, purpose, and narrative strategy.

Your essay should not simply catalogue similarities and differences — it should make an
argument about what the comparison reveals about one or both texts. What does placing
the Kojiki next to this other text illuminate that reading it in isolation does not?

*(Note: This prompt is recommended for students with strong prior familiarity with the
comparison text.)*

---

## What to Submit

1. Which prompt you chose (A, B, C, or D)
2. Your essay (800–1,200 words)
3. At least three specific quotations or close paraphrases from the Chamberlain text —
   these should be moments you are analyzing, not just illustrating
4. One sentence stating your central argument (your thesis) — write this before sharing
   the essay, not after

---

## Evaluation Criteria

- **Thesis**: Does the essay make a specific, arguable claim that could be challenged
  or complicated?
- **Synthesis**: Does the essay draw on material from across the course arc, not just
  one or two sessions?
- **Textual grounding**: Does the essay use specific moments from the Kojiki as evidence,
  analyzed rather than summarized?
- **Intellectual honesty about complexity**: Does the essay acknowledge where the text
  resists easy interpretation, or where the student's position has limits?
- **Voice and commitment**: Does the essay feel like it is written by someone who has
  engaged genuinely with the material, or does it feel generic?

## Tutor Scoring Guide

**Strong response**: Has a clear thesis stated in or near the first paragraph. Uses
specific textual evidence that is analyzed (not just quoted). Shows movement: the essay
develops a position rather than restating the same point in different words. Demonstrates
awareness that the Kojiki is simultaneously a literary text, a political document, and a
reception history. The student has made at least one observation that goes beyond lesson
content — something they have genuinely noticed or argued for themselves.

**Common weaknesses to watch for**:
- **The survey essay**: Student visits multiple topics without sustaining an argument.
  Prompt: "You've covered a lot of ground. What is the one claim you most want me to
  believe after reading this? Let's make sure every paragraph is building toward that."
- **Quotation without analysis**: Student quotes the text but doesn't analyze it.
  Prompt: "You've shown me this passage. Now tell me: why does it matter for your argument?
  What specifically does it prove or complicate?"
- **Reception history without the text**: Student writes about Norinaga or State Shinto
  without connecting to specific Kojiki episodes. Prompt: "Which episode in the Kojiki
  would Norinaga have used to make this argument? What in the text makes his reading
  possible?"
- **Too much hedging**: Student qualifies every claim until nothing is asserted.
  Prompt: "I understand you see multiple sides. But what do you actually think? What
  is your position?"

**Final project tone**: Celebrate the completion of an 8-session literary course.
Acknowledge what the student has learned — both the content and the analytical skills.
The Kojiki is not easy reading, and engaging with it seriously is genuinely impressive.
