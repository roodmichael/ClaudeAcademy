## Exercise 1: The Rabbit of Inaba — Compassion as Character Establishment
**Type:** Reading + Analysis

Find and read the Rabbit of Inaba episode in your Chamberlain.

**What to record:**

1. What specifically do the eighty elder brothers do or fail to do that Ōkuninushi corrects?
   List the concrete actions Ōkuninushi takes to help the rabbit.

2. The rabbit's prophecy — that Ōkuninushi will win the princess — immediately follows his
   act of compassion. This is a common narrative structure: kindness to a small creature
   is rewarded with knowledge or power. Why do you think this pattern recurs across so
   many mythological traditions? What does it communicate about what makes a good ruler?

3. Note the names Chamberlain uses for the creature Ōkuninushi helps. His footnotes may
   discuss the translation challenge here (the Japanese word can mean crocodile, shark,
   or a large sea creature). Read any relevant footnote. What does the ambiguity suggest
   about the text's oral origins?

## Exercise 2: The Kuni-Yuzuri as Political Charter
**Type:** Analysis + Written reflection

Read the kuni-yuzuri section in your Chamberlain — from Amaterasu's decision that Ninigi
should rule, through the failed messengers, to Ōkuninushi's final statement of consent.

**Write a short analytical paragraph (6–10 sentences):**

The lesson argues that the kuni-yuzuri is a political charter — a myth that encodes the
historical relationship between the Yamato court and the Izumo region. Evaluate this claim.
What evidence in the text supports it? What are the limitations of this reading? Is it
possible to read the kuni-yuzuri as both a genuine myth of divine cosmology and as a
political document at the same time? How?

## Exercise 3: Compare Two Modes of Governance
**Type:** Thought experiment

The Kojiki presents two models of heavenly governance and two models of earthly governance.
In heaven: Amaterasu rules through divine authority and occasionally retreats in crisis.
On earth: Ōkuninushi rules through building, cultivation, and compassion.

**Reflect and answer in 3–5 sentences per question:**

1. What does the Kojiki seem to value about earthly governance, based on how it portrays
   Ōkuninushi's achievements?

2. When the heavenly gods demand the land cession, they do not argue that Ōkuninushi
   has been a bad ruler. They simply assert that their authority supersedes his. What
   does this say about the nature of the heavenly gods' legitimacy vs. Ōkuninushi's?

3. Ōkuninushi's request — a great shrine as grand as the palace of the heavenly gods —
   is granted. What does this suggest about the negotiation happening beneath the myth?
