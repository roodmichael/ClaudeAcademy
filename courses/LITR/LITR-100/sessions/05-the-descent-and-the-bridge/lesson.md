# Session 05: The Descent and the Bridge
**Course:** LITR-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain the Tenson Kōrin (Ninigi's descent) as the structural pivot of the Kojiki and analyze the Rejected Sister episode as the narrative hinge between divine immortality and human mortality.

---

## Opening

At some point in every mythological tradition, the gods stop being gods and start being
people — or at least, start being people's ancestors. The mythology ends and the legend
begins. The Kojiki handles this transition with a single, pivotal episode.

A princess's ugly older sister is sent away. And because she is sent away, every emperor
who ever lived will die.

That is the hinge. Let's look at it carefully.

---

## Ninigi: The Chosen Grandson

After Ōkuninushi's abdication, the earthly realm is ready. Amaterasu decides to send
her grandson **Ninigi-no-Mikoto** (his full name is considerably longer and more
elaborate — Chamberlain renders it in full) to rule the land.

Ninigi is not a god of great individual deeds or adventures. His narrative role is different
from Ōkuninushi's or Susanoo's. He is significant precisely because of who he is: the
bridge. He is divine by lineage (Amaterasu's grandson) and earthly by destination (he
descends to Kyushu and stays). His children will be heroes; his great-great-grandson will
be Emperor Jimmu. His function is connective.

Before descending, Ninigi receives the three imperial regalia from Amaterasu:
- The **Yata no Kagami** (the sacred mirror from the Ama-no-Iwato episode)
- The **Magatama beads** (the curved jewels from the same episode)
- The **Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi** (the sword found by Susanoo in Yamata no Orochi)

He also receives divine companions — the gods who will serve as ancestors of important
priestly clans and noble families. Their enumeration in the text is partly a genealogical
argument: these families can trace their lineage to the gods who descended with Ninigi.

Amaterasu gives him this charge: "This Reed-Plain-1500-Autumns-Fair-Rice-ear Land is the
region which my descendants shall be lord of. Do thou, my August Grandchild, proceed
thither and govern it." The land has already been prepared — Ōkuninushi ceded it. Ninigi
is going to a kingdom, not a wilderness.

---

## The Descent: Tenson Kōrin

The **Tenson Kōrin** ("Descent of the Heavenly Grandchild") is considered the climax of
the Kojiki. Ninigi descends through "the manifold-piled clouds" of heaven, standing on the
"Floating Bridge of Heaven" — the same celestial bridge from which Izanagi and Izanami
first looked down at the primordial ocean.

He descends to **Kushifurutake** (also called Takachiho), a peak in Kyushu. On landing,
he declares the land excellent and establishes a grand palace.

One brief scene marks his arrival. An earth-deity named **Sarutahiko** is standing at the
crossroads between heaven and earth, its light shining in all directions. The gods are
afraid to approach him. But Ame-no-Uzume — the same goddess who danced outside the rock
cave — descends, confronts him without fear, and demands to know who he is. He identifies
himself as a guide god for Ninigi. The encounter establishes that the earthly realm will
have guides, not obstacles.

### Why Kyushu?

The geography of Ninigi's descent matters. He lands in **Himuka** (modern Miyazaki), on
the southeastern coast of Kyushu. His descendants will rule from Kyushu, and eventually
Emperor Jimmu will lead a military campaign eastward to Yamato — the central region of
Honshu where the real Yamato court is located.

This geographic logic is politically significant: it traces the ruling dynasty's origin
to a specific, non-central location, and the story of their rise to power becomes a story
of eastward conquest from divine origins in Kyushu. The myth maps onto something that may
reflect a real historical migration or military expansion.

## The Rejected Sister: The Hinge of Mortality

Ninigi is offered a marriage alliance with a local god's daughters. The god presents both
daughters for Ninigi's consideration:
- **Konohanasakuya-hime** ("Blossom Princess" or "She Who Makes Trees Bloom"): beautiful
- **Iwanagahime** ("Princess Long-as-Rock"): ugly

Ninigi accepts Konohanasakuya-hime and sends Iwanagahime back to her father.

Her father is shamed and angry. He explains what Ninigi has forfeited:

> "I sent both my daughters together because, if you had retained Iwanaga-hime, the life
> of the children of the Heavenly Deity would have been as long as the enduring rock. But
> now that you have rejected her and kept only Konohanasakuya-hime, their lives will be
> like that of the blossom — they will fall and perish."

Ninigi chose beauty over permanence. He chose flowers over rock. And so the imperial
descendants — and by extension, all humans — are mortal.

---

## What This Episode Accomplishes

This is the Kojiki's answer to a fundamental human question: why do people die? And
specifically: why do the descendants of the gods — who are themselves immortal — have to die?

The answer is that mortality was not the plan. It was a mistake. A preference for beauty
over endurance, made in a single moment of ordinary human choosing. The emperor was
supposed to live as long as rock; he will instead bloom and fall like flowers.

Note what the story does *not* say. It does not say mortality is a punishment. It does not
say it is a curse. It says it is a consequence — an unintended result of a particular
choice, with the logic explained after the fact.

This is how the Kojiki handles theodicy (the explanation of why bad things are built into
the world): not through divine decree or punishment for sin, but through the accumulation
of narrative consequences. Death came about because Izanami ate the food of the underworld.
Human mortality came about because Ninigi preferred flowers to rock. Each is embedded in
a specific dramatic moment with a specific human-feeling motivation.

## Ninigi's Sons and the Bridge to Jimmu

Ninigi's marriage produces children, and the text continues through two more generations
before reaching Emperor Jimmu. These generations have their own mythological episodes —
most notably, the sea god's palace narrative, in which one of Ninigi's sons retrieves a
lost fishhook from the sea god's realm and marries the sea god's daughter.

These episodes are worth reading for their own charm, but their structural function is
transitional: each generation is slightly more human than the last. By the time we reach
Jimmu, the text is prepared to call him the first emperor — the first figure who will
engage in historically-patterned conquest, establish a court, and begin the reign-by-reign
chronology of the Middle Volume.

---

## Why This Matters

The Tenson Kōrin and the Rejected Sister are the last episodes of the Age of the Gods
before the story becomes — nominally at least — history. They do two essential things:

1. **They explain why imperial rule is legitimate**: The emperor is the direct descendant
   of a deity sent from heaven by Amaterasu herself. The three regalia he carries are the
   physical evidence of that transmission.

2. **They explain why even emperors die**: Not as punishment or failure, but as the
   unintended consequence of a choice made at the moment of their divine origin. This
   makes imperial mortality poignant and human, rather than awkward or theologically
   embarrassing.

The Kojiki is doing careful work here. A dynasty that claims divine descent must explain
why its members are mortal. The Rejected Sister story does exactly that — and makes it
beautiful in the process.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Ninigi's function in the narrative is primarily connective: he bridges the divine and
  human ages as the grandson of Amaterasu who establishes the imperial line in Kyushu.
- He receives the three imperial regalia (mirror, jewels, sword) as tokens of legitimate
  divine authority before descending.
- The Rejected Sister (Iwanagahime) episode explains why the descendants of the gods are
  mortal: Ninigi chose beauty (flowers) over permanence (rock), and the consequence is
  that the imperial line blooms and falls rather than enduring as stone.
- The Kojiki handles mortality not as punishment but as the accumulated consequence of
  a specific dramatic choice — a characteristic of how the text explains why the world
  is the way it is.
