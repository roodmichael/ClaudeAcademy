# Session 01 Exercises

Complete these before taking the session quiz.

---

## Exercise 1: Read Yasumaro's Preface
**Type:** Reading + Analysis

Open your Chamberlain translation to the preface — the text written by Ō no Yasumaro
himself before the myths begin. Read it in full. It is not long.

**What to observe and record:**

Write down answers to these three questions:

1. In Yasumaro's own words (as rendered by Chamberlain), what problem was the Kojiki
   compiled to solve? Quote at least one line that captures the political purpose.

2. Yasumaro describes the oral traditions he is transcribing as having become "confused."
   What does this suggest about the political situation before the Kojiki was written?

3. Yasumaro praises the imperial lineage extravagantly in the preface. How does this
   compare to how you would expect a neutral historian to write? What does the tone of
   the preface tell you about what kind of document this is?

## Exercise 2: A First Encounter with the Footnotes
**Type:** Analysis

Flip to any point in the Upper Volume of your Chamberlain translation — within the first
twenty sections is fine. Find three footnotes and identify which type each belongs to:

- Philological note (explains a word, etymology, or variant reading)
- Comparative mythology note (draws a parallel to another world mythology)
- Commentary note (reports what Norinaga or another Japanese scholar said)
- Editorial confession (Chamberlain admits uncertainty or difficulty)

**What to record:**

For each of the three footnotes you selected:
- Write the footnote number and briefly summarize what it says
- Identify its type from the list above
- Note whether it changes your understanding of the passage it annotates, and how

## Exercise 3: The Kojiki vs. The Nihon Shoki — A First Comparison
**Type:** Thought experiment

You now know that the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki are parallel chronicles compiled within
eight years of each other, for different audiences.

Consider this: the Nihon Shoki is written in pure Classical Chinese, the international
prestige language of East Asia. The Kojiki is written in a mixed Chinese-Japanese style
that preserves archaic Japanese phonetics.

**Reflect and write a short paragraph (5–8 sentences) on:**

If you were a 7th-century Japanese court official, which text would you use when meeting
with a Chinese diplomat, and which would you use for a domestic ceremony in which the
emperor's divine origins were being affirmed? Why might a ruling court need *both* types
of text rather than just one?

There is no single right answer. This exercise is about reasoning from the political context
you've learned.
