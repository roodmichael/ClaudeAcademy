## Exercise 1: Trace the Genealogical Chain
**Type:** Reading + Analysis

Without using any reference other than your Chamberlain translation, trace the genealogical
chain from Amaterasu to Emperor Jimmu, generation by generation.

**What to record:**

Write out the chain as a simple list: Amaterasu → [child] → [grandchild] → ... → Jimmu.

Then answer these questions:

1. How many generations separate Amaterasu from Jimmu? What does this number suggest
   about how the text manages the transition from divine to human?

2. At which generation does the shift from "deity" to "emperor" seem to occur? Is there
   a clear line, or does it blur?

3. The Kojiki gives most of these intermediate figures elaborate names that describe
   their divine nature. Look up one of these names in Chamberlain's footnotes.
   What does the name mean, and how does the meaning connect to that figure's position
   in the genealogical chain?

## Exercise 2: Divine Guidance — Tracing the Mediation
**Type:** Reading + Analysis

In the Upper Volume, the gods act directly: Amaterasu is literally present; Izanagi
physically wrestles thunder-gods. In the Jimmu sections, divine guidance becomes more
mediated.

**Find three moments of divine guidance in the Jimmu narrative and for each, identify:**

1. The form of divine communication (direct voice, dream, bird, descending object, oracle)
2. The source of the communication (Amaterasu, unnamed kami, a specific deity)
3. What Jimmu is told or shown to do

Then write a sentence about the pattern you observe: is divine communication becoming
more or less direct as the text approaches human history?

## Exercise 3: Genealogy as Argument — A Modern Parallel
**Type:** Thought experiment + Written reflection

The Kojiki's Middle Volume genealogical structure argues for legitimacy through continuity:
an unbroken chain of succession from divine origins to the present.

**Write a short paragraph (5–8 sentences):**

Think of a modern institution — a company, a church, a government, a family — that claims
legitimacy partly through the argument of long, unbroken continuity. How does that institution
tell its history? Does the claim of continuity depend on some simplification or selective
telling? Compare this to what the Kojiki does with its genealogies.

You are not being asked to be cynical about all origin claims. You are being asked to
recognize the rhetorical structure: continuity as legitimation.
