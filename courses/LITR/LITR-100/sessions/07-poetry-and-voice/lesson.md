# Session 07: Poetry and Voice
**Course:** LITR-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to identify the major waka forms in the Kojiki, explain what the poems do that the surrounding prose cannot, and analyze a specific poem for its emotional and political function.

---

## Opening

The Kojiki contains approximately 112 poems. They are the text's oldest layer.

That claim is worth pausing on. The prose narrative you have been reading — the mythological
frame, the divine genealogies, the political charters — is organized and edited by Yasumaro.
It reflects 712 CE priorities. But many of the poems embedded in the prose are almost
certainly older: fragments from oral traditions, songs sung at court or at shrines, love
poems and war songs that existed before anyone decided to compile a national chronicle.

Yasumaro's prose is the frame. The poems are the jewels set in it.

When you encounter a poem in the Kojiki, you are encountering a different layer of the
text, doing a different kind of work.

---

## What Is Waka?

**Waka** (和歌, "Japanese poem") is the broad term for classical Japanese poetry. In
the Kojiki, the poems are called *uta* (songs), and they have not yet settled into the
fixed forms that will dominate later Japanese literature. The Kojiki's poems use several
structures, all built from the 5-7 syllable rhythm that is fundamental to Japanese
prosody:

**Katauta** (5-7-7): A "half-poem," nineteen syllables. Sometimes it is a question
inviting a response; sometimes it stands alone.

**Sedōka** (5-7-7 / 5-7-7): Two katauta joined. Used primarily for dialogues — one
character's voice, then another's, the rhythm echoing between them.

**Chōka** (5-7-5-7-…-7-7): A "long poem" of variable length, always ending with an
extra 7-syllable phrase. Used for extended lyric moments — the emotional intensity runs
long.

**Tanka** (5-7-5-7-7): 31 syllables. Later becomes the dominant waka form; in the
Kojiki it is one form among several. Much Japanese poetry for the next thousand years
will be tanka.

The 5-7 pattern feels natural to Japanese ears in a way that is difficult to convey in
English. The alternating rhythm creates a sense of breath and resolution — a light phrase
followed by a heavier one, over and over.

## What the Poems Do That Prose Cannot

This is the session's central question. The short answer is: they voice experience at
its most concentrated.

The Kojiki's prose is primarily narrative and genealogical: this happened, then that
happened; this deity was born, this descendant married that one. The prose tells the story.
But at moments of extreme emotional intensity — grief, longing, triumph, terror, love —
the narrative pauses and a poem speaks.

Think of it as the moment in an opera when the character stops speaking and starts singing.
The prose has brought you to the emotional situation; the poem is the emotion itself.

### Five functions the poems serve

**1. Emotional intensification at crisis moments.** When Yamato Takeru (a later hero in
the Kojiki) is dying on a mountain and composes his final songs — three poems about his
homeland, the white clouds, the cranes flying over the mountains — the prose could say
"he was overcome by grief." The poems do not summarize grief; they enact it. Each poem
is a breath getting shorter.

**2. Cultural memory and oral preservation.** The poems were almost certainly older than
the prose frame. They were sung, memorized, and transmitted before anyone wrote them down.
The prose narrative was built around them — not the other way around. When Yasumaro
embedded a poem in his chronicle, he was preserving an ancient song by giving it a
dramatic context.

**3. Legitimation through performance.** A poem attributed to a god or emperor is an act
of authority. By giving Susanoo the first love poem in the text, the Kojiki gives the
storm god a voice of unexpected tenderness — which is part of how the text redeems him
after the cave episode. When Emperor Jimmu sings a victory poem after his eastern campaign,
the recitation is itself a royal act.

**4. Dialogue and relationship.** The sedōka form — two units, call and response —
enacts relationships in real time. A sedōka between two characters is not a description
of their exchange; it is the exchange. The reader experiences the dynamic between the
speakers through the rhythm of the response.

**5. Linking the mythological past to the living present.** Poems are often followed by
phrases like "therefore this song is sung to this day," or "therefore people say." The poem
is a living artifact — not a quotation from a dead past but a voice that can be reactivated
by recitation in the present.

## Susanoo's First Poem: A Close Look

The first waka attributed to a named character in the entire Kojiki tradition is spoken
by Susanoo, after he arrives in Izumo and prepares to marry Kushinadahime. He has just
slain the eight-headed serpent. He is about to build a palace. He looks around at the
clouds and sings.

In Chamberlain's translation:

> "Many clouds arise. / On all sides a manifold fence / Of clouds is forming to receive
> my new-wed wife: / Oh! that manifold cloud-fence!"

The Japanese form is a chōka — extended, celebratory. The word Chamberlain renders as
"fence" (or "wall") is *kakitsu*, which Norinaga read as carrying an additional layer of
meaning: the clouds surrounding and protecting, enclosing, making a space.

What this poem does:
- It is a love poem. Susanoo, the figure of cosmic violence and destructive grief, is
  building a home for his wife out of clouds.
- It transforms the storm god. The same deity who threw a skinned horse into a weaving
  hall is now composing something tender and protective.
- It establishes Izumo as a place of poetic origin — which matters for the reception
  history of Izumo's religious identity.

Chamberlain has a substantial footnote on this poem discussing Norinaga's interpretation
and the philological questions around the word *kakitsu*. This is a good example of a
footnote that genuinely enhances the poem.

## Makurakotoba: Pillows and Prefixes

One feature of early waka that confuses translators and readers alike: **makurakotoba**
(枕詞, "pillow words"). These are conventional epithets that appear before specific nouns
or place names, contributing emotional tone, cadence, and associations that are essentially
untranslatable.

For example, the phrase "nubatama no" (meaning something like "the jewel-like night") is
a conventional makurakotoba that appears before words relating to darkness, night, or black
hair. It contributes a sense of rich, lustrous darkness — but its meaning is more sonic
and associative than semantic.

When Chamberlain encounters makurakotoba, he sometimes translates them literally (which
produces strange effects in English), sometimes explains them in footnotes, and sometimes
simply omits them. Modern translations like Heldt's handle them more gracefully.

When you read a phrase in a poem that seems oddly specific or over-long, you may be looking
at a makurakotoba. Check the footnote.

## The War Songs (Kume-uta)

A different register entirely: the Kojiki also preserves **kume-uta** — martial songs
associated with Emperor Jimmu's campaigns. These are aggressive, rhythmically forceful,
and use imagery of pounding, slicing, and breaking.

They almost certainly originated as actual military songs in an earlier oral context —
chants that real armies might have used before or during battle. The Kojiki frames them as
historical quotation: "therefore the Kume sang this song."

The contrast between Susanoo's love poem and the kume-uta illustrates the range of the
Kojiki's poetic register. The same form — waka, built from 5-7 rhythms — can carry the
most tender affection and the most violent exultation.

---

## Why This Matters

The poems in the Kojiki are the oldest surviving Japanese literature. They predate the
great poetry anthologies by decades. Some of them may predate writing in Japan by
centuries. When you read them, you are reading the most direct surviving voice of the
ancient culture the Kojiki was compiled to preserve.

The prose narrative is a 712 CE political document. The poems are something older.
Yasumaro knew this — it is why he took such care to preserve them, writing them out
phonetically in man'yōgana even when the surrounding prose used Classical Chinese. He
was preserving a voice he knew could not be replaced.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Kojiki contains approximately 112 waka poems in several forms: katauta, sedōka,
  chōka, and tanka.
- The poems are likely older than the prose frame — embedded artifacts from an earlier
  oral tradition that Yasumaro preserved by writing them phonetically.
- The poems do what the prose cannot: enact emotion rather than narrate it, give voice
  to divine and royal authority, preserve cultural memory across time, and link the
  mythological past to the living present.
- Susanoo's first poem (the cloud-fence love song) exemplifies how a poem can transform
  a character — showing dimensions the prose narrative cannot accommodate.
- Makurakotoba ("pillow words") are conventional epithets that carry sonic and associative
  meaning untranslatable into English; Chamberlain's footnotes often explain them.
