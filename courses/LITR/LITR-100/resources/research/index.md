## Key Themes

- **Political legitimation is the text's foundation**: The Kojiki was compiled to justify Yamato imperial authority. Every major myth has a political payoff. Reading the myths without this context produces misunderstanding.
- **The text has layers**: What Yasumaro wrote in 712, what Norinaga interpreted in 1798, and what Chamberlain translated in 1882 are three distinct events. All three are in the student's hands.
- **Poetry is not ornament**: The approximately 112 poems in the Kojiki are the oldest stratum of the text, embedded artifacts from oral tradition. They do emotional and political work the prose cannot.
- **The Kojiki was nearly forgotten**: Its status as "Japan's oldest and most important text" is a Meiji-era construction. For most of Japanese history, scholars read the Nihon Shoki instead.
- **The danger of reading myths literally**: State Shinto's weaponization of the Kojiki is a major historical event that any serious study of the text must reckon with.
