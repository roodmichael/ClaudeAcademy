## Core Concepts

The *Kojiki* (古事記, "Records of Ancient Matters") is Japan's oldest surviving literary work,
presented to Empress Genmei on the 28th day of the 1st month of 712 CE. It was compiled by
court scholar Ō no Yasumaro from two sources: (1) earlier written genealogical records and
(2) the memorized oral traditions of a court attendant named Hieda no Are.

The Kojiki spans three books (*maki*):
- **Kamitsumaki** (Upper Volume): Creation mythology and the Age of the Gods
- **Nakatsumaki** (Middle Volume): Emperor Jimmu through Emperor Ōjin (the 15th emperor)
- **Shimotsumaki** (Lower Volume): The 16th through 33rd emperors; more historical, less mythological

The text was written using Chinese characters in two distinct ways: the prose sections use
Classical Chinese, while the poems (uta) are written phonetically in man'yōgana — Chinese
characters used as sound symbols to capture Japanese syllables.

### Political Context

The Kojiki did not emerge in a neutral atmosphere. It served explicit political purposes for
the ruling Yamato court:

1. **Imperial legitimation**: By connecting the reigning emperors directly to Amaterasu, the
   sun goddess, the text established divine right to rule.
2. **Clan integration**: Rival noble families (uji) were incorporated into the official
   genealogy — giving them a stake in the imperial narrative while subordinating them to the
   Yamato line.
3. **Forgery suppression**: By the 670s CE, many clan documents were circulating with
   fabricated or conflicting genealogies. Emperor Tenmu (r. 673–686) commissioned the project
   partly to establish a single authoritative account before forgeries became entrenched.
4. **Response to Chinese influence**: Contact with China had introduced the Japanese court
   to Chinese historiography. The Kojiki created an origin narrative parallel in prestige to
   Chinese dynastic chronicles.

Emperor Tenmu commissioned the work but died before completion. Empress Genmei (r. 707–715)
revived it and ordered Yasumaro to complete the transcription from Hieda no Are's memorized
materials. Yasumaro added a preface explaining this history — a preface that is itself a
political document justifying the work's authority.

### The Kojiki vs. The Nihon Shoki

Eight years after the Kojiki, the *Nihon Shoki* (720 CE) was compiled — a second, longer
chronicle. The two texts differ significantly:
- The *Kojiki* is inward-looking, written in a mixed Chinese-Japanese style, concerned with
  the ruling family's divine origins.
- The *Nihon Shoki* is written in pure Classical Chinese, designed partly for foreign
  (especially Chinese) audiences, and often presents variant myths with multiple versions.
- Where the *Kojiki* presents a single authoritative account, the *Nihon Shoki* sometimes
  contradicts it, offering competing versions of the same stories.

This means the Kojiki is not Japan's only mythological record — it is the *earliest*,
and the one most concerned with a specifically Japanese audience and identity.

### Historical Reliability

Modern scholars treat the Kojiki's "historical" sections (the human emperors) with significant
skepticism. The traditional founding date for Japan (660 BCE for Emperor Jimmu) is considered
exaggerated — likely invented to give Japan an antiquity comparable to China and Korea. Only
the final ~250 years of the chronicle can be cross-referenced with contemporary external
sources. Tsuda Sōkichi's landmark 1913 study argued the text is "a document created to
legitimize the rule of the imperial line" rather than history in the modern sense.

## Key Definitions

- **Kojiki** (古事記): "Records of Ancient Matters" — Japan's oldest extant literary work,
  compiled 712 CE
- **Nihon Shoki** (日本書紀): "Chronicles of Japan," compiled 720 CE — a longer, parallel
  chronicle serving different rhetorical goals
- **Ō no Yasumaro**: Court scholar who wrote down and edited the Kojiki on Empress Genmei's
  orders
- **Hieda no Are**: Court attendant who had memorized oral traditions and genealogical records;
  the primary source Yasumaro transcribed
- **Uji**: Noble clans; their genealogical integration into the Kojiki gave them a stake in the
  imperial narrative
- **Man'yōgana**: The system of using Chinese characters phonetically to write Japanese sounds;
  used for the poems in the Kojiki
- **Kamitsumaki / Nakatsumaki / Shimotsumaki**: The three volumes (Upper, Middle, Lower) of
  the Kojiki
- **Yamato**: The powerful clan that formed the imperial line; the Kojiki is largely their
  official mythology
- **Kunitsukami**: Gods of the earth (as opposed to the heavenly kami, the amatsukami)
- **Amatsukami**: Heavenly deities, ancestors of the imperial line

## Process / Mechanics

How the Kojiki came to be:
1. c. 620 CE: Prince Shōtoku and Soga no Umako begin historical documentation; destroyed
   in the Isshi Incident of 645
2. c. 673–686 CE: Emperor Tenmu orders review of clan documents and commissions Hieda no Are
   to memorize the correct genealogies
3. 711 CE: Empress Genmei orders Yasumaro to transcribe Are's memorized materials (18th day,
   9th month)
4. 712 CE: Yasumaro presents the completed work (28th day, 1st month)
5. 720 CE: The *Nihon Shoki* is compiled by a different team, partially correcting and
   supplementing the Kojiki

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Treating it as objective history**: The Kojiki is a political document first. Its
  "historical" sections are largely legendary; its mythological sections served ideological
  purposes.
- **Conflating the Kojiki with the Nihon Shoki**: They are different texts with different
  goals. Scholars often distinguish them carefully.
- **Assuming it was always important**: The Kojiki was largely forgotten during the Heian
  and Kamakura periods. It was the *Nihon Shoki* that scholars read. The Kojiki's elevation
  to foundational status is a relatively recent development (Edo period onward).
- **Ignoring the preface**: Yasumaro's preface is itself a primary source about the text's
  political purpose. Chamberlain translated it fully, and it repays careful reading.

## Hands-On Elements

- Read Yasumaro's preface in Chamberlain's translation — compare what he claims about the
  text's origins to what modern scholars say about those claims.
- Compare two myths from the Kojiki with their counterparts in the Nihon Shoki to observe
  how the "same" story can be told differently for different political purposes.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Was Hieda no Are male or female? (The text's pronouns are ambiguous; this has been
  a lively scholarly dispute.)
- How much of the "oral tradition" Yasumaro transcribed was genuinely ancient vs. shaped
  in the 7th-century political moment?
- How much should we read the Kojiki through the lens of Motoori Norinaga's 18th-century
  interpretation, which itself reshaped how the text was understood?

## Connections to Adjacent Topics

- Shinto as a religion did not exist in a formalized sense at 712 CE — the Kojiki is partly
  a document in the *creation* of formal Shinto, not merely its documentation.
- The political use of mythology to legitimize ruling dynasties is cross-cultural; the
  Kojiki parallels Roman genealogical myths (Troy → Rome), Norse royal genealogies tracing
  to Odin, and the genealogical arguments in Genesis.
- Michael's interest in early Christian writings is a useful bridge: just as patristic writers
  compiled and argued from scriptural authority to legitimize certain interpretations of
  Christianity, Yasumaro compiled and arranged oral traditions to legitimize certain claims
  about imperial rule.
