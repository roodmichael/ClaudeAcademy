# Glossary — LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100: The Kojiki

All key terms from the course, alphabetized, with plain-language definitions.

---

**Ama-no-Iwato** (天岩戸): "Heavenly Rock Cave" — the cave where Amaterasu hides after
Susanoo's rampage, plunging the world into darkness. The episode in which the gods lure
her out is one of the Kojiki's most analyzed narratives.

**Ama-no-Uzume**: Goddess of festivity, dawn, and revelry. Her ecstatic dance outside
the Ama-no-Iwato — making the gods laugh so hard that Amaterasu becomes curious — is what
lures Amaterasu out of the cave.

**Ame-no-Tajikarao**: The powerful god who pulls Amaterasu fully out of the Ama-no-Iwato
once she has cracked the door open.

**Amaterasu Ōmikami** (天照大神): "Great Divinity Illuminating Heaven" — the sun goddess,
ruler of Takamagahara (the High Plain of Heaven), and the divine ancestor of the imperial
line. Born when Izanagi washed his left eye.

**Arahitogami** (現人神): "A living kami" — the Meiji-era State Shinto doctrine that the
emperor was literally divine. Renounced by Emperor Hirohito in his January 1946 Humanity
Declaration.

**Ashihara no Nakatsukuni** (葦原中国): "Central Land of Reed Plains" — the earthly realm
developed by Ōkuninushi; the land that Amaterasu demands be ceded for Ninigi's descent.

**Asiatic Society of Japan**: A scholarly society founded in 1872 for Western residents
and scholars in Japan. Chamberlain first presented his Kojiki translation here in 1882.

**Etiology**: A narrative explaining the origin of something — a natural phenomenon, a
social practice, or a cosmic fact. The Kojiki contains many etiological episodes: why humans
die, why the sun and moon don't share the sky, why a particular shrine exists.

**Hieda no Are**: The court attendant (gender debated by scholars) who memorized the
genealogical records and oral traditions that Ō no Yasumaro later transcribed into the
Kojiki. An oral repository of ancient Japanese tradition.

**Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843): Motoori Norinaga's spiritual successor; he never met
Norinaga. Developed kokugaku into a popular, exclusivist religious movement; his version
was more explicitly nationalistic than Norinaga's.

**Izanagi** (伊邪那岐): "He-Who-Invites" — the male half of the primordial creative couple.
With Izanami, he creates the Japanese islands. After Izanami's death, he descends to the
underworld, fails to retrieve her, and then purifies himself — in which act the Three
Precious Children are born.

**Izanami** (伊邪那美): "She-Who-Invites" — the female half of the primordial creative couple.
She dies giving birth to the fire god; her rotting body in the underworld establishes the
cosmological logic of death and pollution in Shinto.

**Izumo Taisha**: The Izumo Grand Shrine — one of Japan's most important Shinto sites.
Built as the condition of Ōkuninushi's land cession; dedicated to Ōkuninushi.

**Jimmu** (神武): The legendary first emperor of Japan, traditionally dated to 660 BCE
(a construction modern scholars reject). His narrative in the Middle Volume traces his
divine genealogy (five generations from Amaterasu) and his eastward military campaign
from Kyushu to Yamato.

**Kami** (神): Divine spirits or supernatural forces. The Kojiki describes approximately
8 million kami — a number meaning "infinite" rather than a literal count.

**Kamitsumaki** (上巻): "Upper Volume" — the first of the Kojiki's three books; covers
the Age of the Gods from creation through Ninigi's descent.

**Kami no michi** (神の道): "Way of the Kami" — Motoori Norinaga's reconstructed form
of Shinto, grounded in Kojiki analysis; claims to represent Japan's original, pre-Buddhist
and pre-Confucian religious nature.

**Katauta** (片歌): "Half-poem" — a 5-7-7 syllable waka form (19 syllables total);
sometimes stands alone, sometimes invites a response from another speaker.

**Keichū** (1640–1701): Buddhist monk; pioneer of historical linguistic analysis of
ancient Japanese texts; established the philological methods that Norinaga later applied
to the Kojiki.

**Kojiki** (古事記): "Records of Ancient Matters" — Japan's oldest surviving literary
work, compiled by Ō no Yasumaro and presented to Empress Genmei in 712 CE.

**Kojiki-den**: Motoori Norinaga's 44-volume philological commentary on the Kojiki,
completed in 1798 after 34 years of work. One of the most intensive scholarly engagements
with a single text in world literary history.

**Kokugaku** (国学): "Native studies" or "national learning" — an Edo-period intellectual
movement seeking authentic Japanese identity in ancient texts, in opposition to Chinese-
influenced Confucian and Buddhist learning.

**Kotoamatsukami**: "Separate Heavenly Deities" — the five solitary kami who appear
at the very opening of the Kojiki, before even the paired divine generations, and then
"hide their persons."

**Kume-uta**: Martial songs in the Kojiki associated with Emperor Jimmu's campaigns;
aggressive in tone, probably originating as actual battle chants in earlier oral tradition.

**Kuni-yuzuri** (国譲り): "Land cession" — the episode in which Ōkuninushi peacefully
cedes the earthly realm to Amaterasu's heavenly line, in exchange for the construction
of the Izumo Grand Shrine.

**Kuniizukuri** (国作り): "Building of the land" — Ōkuninushi's culture-hero work:
establishing agriculture, medicine, sake brewing, and other civilizational goods in the
earthly realm.

**Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi** (草薙の剣): "Grass-Cutting Sword" — one of the three imperial
regalia; found by Susanoo in the tail of the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi.

**Makurakotoba** (枕詞): "Pillow words" — conventional epithets in waka poetry attached
to specific nouns or place names; carry sonic and associative meaning that is largely
untranslatable into English.

**Man'yōgana**: The system of using Chinese characters phonetically to represent Japanese
sounds; used in the Kojiki for the poems (uta) and in sections requiring archaic Japanese
phonetic preservation.

**Mihashira no uzu no mikoto** (三貴子): "Three Precious Children" — Amaterasu,
Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo; the three greatest kami born from Izanagi's purification.

**Misogi** (禊): Ritual purification by water; one of Shinto's most important practices.
Its origin story in the Kojiki is Izanagi's purification after returning from the underworld.

**Mono no aware** (もののあわれ): "The pathos of things" — Motoori Norinaga's concept
of sensitivity to beauty and transience; he argued it was the fundamental quality of
Japanese literary sensibility.

**Motoori Norinaga** (1730–1801): The foremost kokugaku scholar; physician from Matsusaka;
author of the 44-volume Kojiki-den (1798); developer of mono no aware and kami no michi.

**Nakatsumaki** (中巻): "Middle Volume" — the second of the Kojiki's three books; covers
Emperor Jimmu through Emperor Ōjin; predominantly genealogical.

**Nihon Shoki** (日本書紀): "Chronicles of Japan" — a second chronicle compiled in 720 CE;
written in pure Classical Chinese for international audiences; often presents variant
versions of the same myths as the Kojiki.

**Nihonjinron** (日本人論): "Theories of Japaneseness" — post-WWII cultural discourse
arguing for Japanese cultural distinctiveness; the attenuated, non-racial descendant of
kokugaku's cultural essentialism.

**Ninigi-no-Mikoto** (邇邇芸命): Amaterasu's grandson; the chosen emissary sent to rule
the earthly realm; his descent (Tenson Kōrin) is the structural pivot of the Kojiki.

**Ō no Yasumaro**: The court scholar who compiled and edited the Kojiki, presenting it
to Empress Genmei in 712 CE. His preface is a political document explaining the text's
purpose.

**Omoikane**: "Thought-Combining" — the god of wisdom and deliberation; chairs the divine
council outside the Ama-no-Iwato, devising the strategy to lure Amaterasu out.

**Ōkuninushi** (大国主): "Great Land Master" — a descendant of Susanoo; culture hero who
builds the earthly realm (kuniizukuri) and then peacefully cedes it in the kuni-yuzuri.

**Orientalism**: Edward Said's term for the Western scholarly tendency to construct Asia
as exotic, static, and less developed — the framework through which Chamberlain and most
Victorian scholars approached Japanese subjects.

**Sedōka** (旋頭歌): "Whirling-head poem" — a 5-7-7 / 5-7-7 waka structure (38 syllables),
used in the Kojiki primarily for dialogues between characters.

**Shimotsumaki** (下巻): "Lower Volume" — the third of the Kojiki's three books; covers
the 16th through 33rd emperors; the most historically plausible section.

**State Shinto**: The Meiji government's official ideology using Shinto — including the
doctrine of imperial divinity grounded in the Kojiki's divine genealogy — as national
religion and political legitimation. Disestablished in 1945.

**Sukunabikona**: Ōkuninushi's small but wise companion in building the earthly realm;
a deity who arrives in a boat and contributes to the kuniizukuri project.

**Susanoo-no-Mikoto** (素戔嗚尊): The storm god; born when Izanagi washed his nose.
A morally complex figure: he causes the Ama-no-Iwato crisis through violence, is exiled,
then becomes a hero who slays the eight-headed serpent and composes the first waka.

**Takamagahara** (高天原): "High Plain of Heaven" — the divine realm ruled by Amaterasu;
the origin point of the heavenly lineage.

**Tanka** (短歌): "Short poem" — 5-7-5-7-7, 31 syllables total; one of several waka forms
in the Kojiki; later becomes the dominant form of classical Japanese poetry.

**Tenson Kōrin** (天孫降臨): "Descent of the Heavenly Grandchild" — Ninigi-no-Mikoto's
descent from heaven to Mount Takachiho in Kyushu; considered the climax of the Kojiki
and the pivot between the divine age and the proto-historical age.

**Tsuda Sōkichi** (1873–1961): Modern Japanese historian who argued in 1913 that the
Kojiki was political literature rather than history; suppressed during the militarist
period; rehabilitated after 1945.

**Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto** (月読命): The moon god; born when Izanagi washed his right eye;
assigned to rule the night. Notably absent from most of the Kojiki after his initial
introduction — this lacuna has fascinated scholars.

**Uji**: Noble clans; the rival genealogical factions whose forged documents were one
of the problems the Kojiki was compiled to resolve.

**Uta**: "Song" or "poem" — the general term for the embedded poems in the Kojiki;
related to but broader than waka.

**Waka** (和歌): "Japanese poem" — classical Japanese poetry in several forms based on
5-7 syllable rhythms. The Kojiki contains the oldest recorded examples of waka.

**Yamata no Orochi** (八岐大蛇): The eight-headed serpent of Izumo, slain by Susanoo.
Inside one of its tails is the sword Kusanagi, which becomes one of the three imperial
regalia.

**Yamato**: The powerful clan that formed the imperial line and gave their name to the
central region of Honshu. The Kojiki is primarily their mythology and their political
legitimation document.

**Yata no Kagami** (八咫鏡): "Eight-Span Mirror" — one of the three imperial regalia;
forged by the smith god and hung outside the Ama-no-Iwato to lure Amaterasu out;
Amaterasu's reflected image in this mirror transfixes her long enough for the gods to
pull her out.

**Yomi** (黄泉): The underworld — a dark, polluted realm below the earth. Izanagi's
failed attempt to retrieve Izanami from Yomi establishes the cosmological logic of death
and ritual pollution in Shinto.
