## Core Concepts

### The Kojiki's Long Obscurity

Counterintuitively, the Kojiki was largely forgotten for over a thousand years after its
compilation. During the Heian period (794–1185) and into the medieval era, it was the
*Nihon Shoki* — with its Chinese-style historical prose — that Japanese scholars read and
commented upon. The Kojiki was considered difficult, linguistically opaque, and somewhat
peripheral to official court learning.

By the late Kamakura period (1185–1333), few people had access to even the complete text.
It was first printed as the *Kan'ei Kojiki* in 1644, reaching a broader audience for the
first time in nearly a millennium. A second printing with marginal annotations appeared
in 1687.

### The Kokugaku Movement (Native Studies)

In the Edo period (1603–1868), a group of Japanese scholars began pushing back against the
dominance of Chinese Confucian and Buddhist frameworks in Japanese intellectual life.
They called their approach **kokugaku** (国学, "native studies" or "national learning") and
sought to recover a distinctly Japanese identity encoded in ancient texts.

Key figures in the progression:
1. **Keichū** (1640–1701): Pioneered historical linguistic analysis of the Man'yōshū,
   establishing the philological methods later scholars would use
2. **Kada no Azumamaro** (1669–1736): Called for the formal study of ancient Japanese
   literature; helped institutionalize kokugaku as a scholarly program
3. **Kamo no Mabuchi** (1697–1769): Focused on the Man'yōshū; developed a concept of
   ancient Japanese simplicity and directness as superior to Chinese sophistication;
   became Norinaga's teacher (they met only once, famously, in 1763)
4. **Motoori Norinaga** (1730–1801): The movement's apex; turned to the Kojiki as the
   primary source of authentic Japanese identity; wrote the definitive commentary

### Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801)

Norinaga was a physician from Matsusaka who spent 34 years (1764–1798) producing the
**Kojiki-den** — a 44-volume philological commentary on the Kojiki. It remains one of the
most intensive scholarly engagements with a single text in world literary history.

**His philological method**: Norinaga treated the Kojiki as a *makoto no fumi* ("True Book")
and approached it with unprecedented rigor. He analyzed ancient grammar, vocabulary,
and phonetics to reconstruct meanings obscured over centuries. His reliance on historical
linguistics (drawing on Keichū's work with the Man'yōshū) gave him tools to read the
archaic Japanese of the text more accurately than any previous scholar.

**His ideological argument**: Norinaga argued that the Kojiki, precisely because it
*did not* employ Chinese stylistic conventions, was more authentically Japanese than the
*Nihon Shoki*. He claimed it revealed "Japan in its pristine, ideal state" — before Chinese
and Buddhist influences contaminated Japanese thought and sensibility.

He introduced (or radically developed) two key concepts:

1. **Mono no aware** (もののあわれ, "the pathos of things"): A sensitivity to the beauty
   and transience of the natural and human world. He applied this concept first to the
   *Tale of Genji* but argued it was the fundamental quality of Japanese literary
   sensibility — and that it could be recovered from ancient texts like the Kojiki.

2. **Kami no michi** ("Way of the Kami"): A reconstructed form of Shinto grounded in
   Kojiki analysis. He claimed this was Japan's original, authentic religion — not
   the Buddhism-influenced or Confucianism-influenced forms dominant in his day.

**The political edge**: Norinaga's scholarship contained a nationalistic implication he
did not entirely develop himself. By arguing that the Japanese emperor was the unique
descendant of Amaterasu — a metaphysical link between people and the divine — he provided
theological tools that later ideologues would weaponize.

### After Norinaga: The Kojiki's Politicization

Norinaga died in 1801. His student **Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843, though he never met
Norinaga in person) claimed his spiritual legacy and developed kokugaku into a popular
religious movement. Hirata's version was more explicitly exclusivist and spiritualist.

In the **Meiji period** (1868–1912), after the Meiji Restoration ended the shogunate and
restored imperial rule, kokugaku's ideas became state ideology. **State Shinto** used the
Kojiki's divine genealogy to assert that the emperor was literally divine (arahitogami, "a
living kami"), that the Japanese people were uniquely descended from the gods, and that
Japan was therefore superior to other nations.

This ideology contributed directly to the expansionism and militarism of the early 20th
century. The Kojiki was taught in schools as literal history.

**After 1945**: Following Japan's defeat in World War II, State Shinto was abolished by
Allied occupation authorities. The emperor publicly renounced divinity. Scholars like
**Tsuda Sōkichi** (whose 1913 work was suppressed during the war) were rehabilitated;
his argument — that the Kojiki is political literature rather than history or scripture —
became the new scholarly consensus.

Contemporary scholarship treats the Kojiki as a rich literary and mythological document,
a primary source for early Japanese history, and a cautionary example of how ancient texts
can be weaponized for modern political purposes.

### What Does "Myth" Mean Here?

The word "myth" requires careful handling when applied to the Kojiki. In popular usage,
"myth" means "a false story." In scholarship, it means something different: a narrative
that a community uses to explain its origins, values, and cosmic order. Myths are not
simply believed or disbelieved — they are *performed*, *inhabited*, and *argued over*.

The Kojiki's cosmological narratives were:
- Politically functional in 712 CE (imperial legitimation)
- Liturgically active (Shinto rituals drew on them)
- Editorially shaped by Yasumaro and his 8th-century assumptions
- Reinterpreted by Norinaga in the 18th century
- Appropriated by the Meiji state in the 19th–20th centuries
- Critiqued and partially rehabilitated by post-WWII scholarship

Understanding the Kojiki means holding all of these layers simultaneously — not deciding
whether to "believe" the myths, but asking what they were doing at each historical moment.

## Key Definitions

- **Kokugaku** (国学): "Native studies" — an Edo-period intellectual movement seeking
  authentic Japanese identity in ancient texts, in opposition to Chinese-influenced learning
- **Motoori Norinaga** (1730–1801): The foremost kokugaku scholar; author of the 44-volume
  Kojiki-den; developed the concepts of mono no aware and kami no michi
- **Kojiki-den**: Norinaga's 44-volume commentary on the Kojiki, completed 1798 after 34
  years of work
- **Mono no aware** (もののあわれ): "The pathos of things" — sensitivity to beauty, transience,
  and emotional resonance; Norinaga's central concept of Japanese literary sensibility
- **Kami no michi**: "Way of the kami" — Norinaga's reconstructed "original" Shinto,
  grounded in Kojiki analysis
- **State Shinto**: The Meiji-era government's use of Shinto as national ideology, including
  the doctrine of imperial divinity; abolished in 1945
- **Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843): Norinaga's spiritual successor; developed kokugaku
  into a popular exclusivist religious movement
- **Tsuda Sōkichi** (1873–1961): Modern Japanese historian who argued the Kojiki was
  political literature, not history; suppressed during militarist period, rehabilitated
  post-WWII
- **Arahitogami**: "A living kami" — the doctrine that the emperor was literally divine;
  a Meiji-era State Shinto claim; renounced by Emperor Hirohito in 1946
- **Nihonjinron**: Post-WWII "theories of Japaneseness" — a softer descendant of kokugaku's
  cultural essentialism, appearing in business books, cultural studies, and soft power discourse

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Thinking the Kojiki has always been Japan's "national scripture"**: It was largely
  ignored for a millennium. Its elevation to foundational status is an Edo-Meiji construction.
- **Conflating Norinaga's interpretation with the text itself**: Norinaga shaped how
  the Kojiki is read, including how Chamberlain read it. His commentary is brilliant but
  ideologically loaded.
- **Treating the militarist appropriation as the "natural" reading**: The Kojiki contains
  the raw material for many readings. The fascist reading was one powerful one, but it was
  not inevitable.
- **Applying the word "myth" dismissively**: Calling the Kojiki "just myth" misses how
  myths function. The question isn't whether Izanagi literally stirred the ocean; it's
  what work that story did for the people who told and retold it.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Is Norinaga a great philologist who happened to have nationalist implications, or is the
  nationalism inseparable from the philology? Scholars disagree.
- Can the Kojiki be read as "pure literature" stripped of its political and religious
  functions? Post-structuralist critics say this is impossible — texts are always embedded
  in power structures.
- The relationship between kokugaku and modern Japanese cultural identity (*Nihonjinron*)
  is contested: is it the same stream of thought, or has it been substantially transformed?

## Connections to Adjacent Topics

- The parallel to Norinaga in Western intellectual history is often drawn to the German
  Romantic philologists (Grimm brothers, Herder) who collected folk tales and legends as
  expressions of national "folk soul" — a project with similar nationalist implications.
- For Michael's interest in patristics: Norinaga's effort to recover an authentic
  pre-contaminated Japanese religion from ancient texts has a structural parallel in
  patristic debates about which texts represented "original" Christianity vs. later
  "corruptions." The politics of canonization operate similarly in both contexts.
- The post-WWII rehabilitation of the Kojiki as literature rather than scripture has a
  parallel in post-Enlightenment Biblical hermeneutics — reading sacred texts historically
  and literarily without requiring literal belief.
