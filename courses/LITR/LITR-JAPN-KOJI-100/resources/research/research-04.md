## Core Concepts

### What Is Waka?

**Waka** (和歌, "Japanese poem") is a broad term for classical Japanese poetry. The Kojiki
is the earliest recorded source of waka. The poems in the text are written in Old Japanese
using man'yōgana — the same phonetic system used for the Kojiki's prose poems, making them
the oldest stratum of Japanese literary culture accessible to modern readers.

The Kojiki contains approximately 112 poems (the Nihon Shoki contains even more). They are
not decorative additions — they are structurally integral to the narrative.

### Forms Found in the Kojiki

By the time of the Kojiki, waka had not yet settled into fixed forms. The early poems use
several structures:

- **Katauta** (5-7-7): A "half-poem," sometimes a question seeking a response
- **Sedōka** (5-7-7-5-7-7): Two katauta joined; used for dialogues between characters
- **Chōka** (5-7-5-7-…-7-7): A "long poem" with repeating 5-7 phrases; used for extended
  lyric moments
- **Tanka** (5-7-5-7-7): A 31-syllable "short poem"; later becomes the dominant waka form,
  but in the Kojiki it is one form among several

The 5-7 syllable pattern that underlies all these forms is not arbitrary. Japanese phonology
naturally groups syllables in alternating light-heavy patterns; the 5-7 rhythm creates
what sounds, to Japanese ears, like a natural breath and resolution.

### What the Poems Do That Prose Cannot

This is the pedagogically central question for Session 07.

In the Kojiki, prose tells the story. Poems do something else: they voice emotional and
spiritual experience at moments too intense for narrative prose to contain. Key functions:

1. **Emotional intensification**: When a character (god or emperor) is in a moment of
   extreme grief, longing, triumph, or awe, the narrative typically shifts into verse.
   The poem is not a summary of what they feel — it is the feeling itself. Prose describes;
   verse enacts.

2. **Cultural memory and oral preservation**: The poems were almost certainly composed
   before the Kojiki was written — they are embedded artifacts from an older oral tradition.
   The prose narrative is a later frame built around them. This means the poems are, in a
   sense, more ancient than the surrounding text.

3. **Legitimation through performance**: A poem attributed to an emperor or deity is an
   act of royal and divine authority. By associating poems with specific divine or imperial
   figures, the Kojiki gives those figures a living voice. Reciting the poem is, in a
   sense, re-activating that authority in the present.

4. **Dialogue structure (sedōka)**: Some poems are exchanges between two characters — a
   call-and-response form that enacts relationship dynamics (love, rivalry, submission,
   challenge) in a way prose summary cannot.

5. **Breaking the fourth wall of narrative time**: Poems are often framed with formulas
   like "therefore people say to this day" — linking the mythological past to the present
   moment of the reader. The poem serves as a living link between the time of the gods
   and the time of reading.

### Specific Examples

**Susanoo's first waka**: After arriving in Izumo and preparing to marry Kushinadahime,
Susanoo composes what is traditionally considered the first waka in the Kojiki. In
Chamberlain's translation it begins: "Many clouds arise. / On all sides a manifold fence /
Of clouds is forming to receive my new-wed wife: / Oh! that manifold cloud-fence!" This
poem (a chōka) expresses wonder, enclosure, protection, and erotic anticipation simultaneously.
It is spoken by the storm god as a love poem — a surprising register for a figure we have
just seen as a violent force.

**Ōkuninushi's longing songs**: Several poems attribute to Ōkuninushi express grief and
longing. Their emotional directness — a god who misses his wife — has made them perennial
favorites in the Japanese tradition.

**War songs (kume-uta)**: Songs associated with Emperor Jimmu's eastern campaign use
aggressive, martial imagery. These songs likely originated as actual battle songs in
earlier oral tradition; the Kojiki frames them as historical quotation from the founding
imperial moment.

### How to Read Chamberlain's Poem Translations

Chamberlain translated the poems earnestly but without strong aesthetic sense for their
Japanese prosody. He preserves the content but not the cadence. Modern translations
(Philippi, Heldt) are significantly better as poems. When studying the verse in the Kojiki,
reading two translations side-by-side is especially valuable.

A key challenge: Japanese poetry depends heavily on **makurakotoba** ("pillow words") —
conventional epithets applied to specific nouns or places. They contribute emotional tone
and resonance that often doesn't survive translation. Chamberlain sometimes explains them
in footnotes.

## Key Definitions

- **Waka** (和歌): "Japanese poem" — classical Japanese poetry; in the Kojiki, the earliest
  recorded stratum of this tradition
- **Uta**: A more general term for "song" or "poem" used in the Kojiki; the poems in the
  text are sometimes called uta
- **Tanka** (短歌): "Short poem" — 5-7-5-7-7, 31 syllables; eventually becomes the dominant
  waka form
- **Chōka** (長歌): "Long poem" — extended 5-7 pattern; used for significant lyric moments
  in the Kojiki
- **Sedōka** (旋頭歌): "Whirling-head poem" — 5-7-7-5-7-7; used for dialogues
- **Katauta** (片歌): "Half poem" — 5-7-7; a single unit that may invite a response
- **Man'yōgana**: Phonetic use of Chinese characters to represent Japanese sounds; the
  script of the Kojiki's poems
- **Makurakotoba** ("pillow words"): Conventional epithets or modifiers attached to
  specific nouns in Japanese poetry; carry emotional and associative resonance
- **Mono no aware**: "The pathos of things" — Motoori Norinaga's concept of poignant
  sensitivity to transience; he argued the Kojiki's poems embody this quality

## Process / Mechanics

Approaching the poems when reading:
1. Identify what narrative moment the poem interrupts — what is the character doing and
   feeling just before the poem begins?
2. Read the poem aloud. Even in English translation, the pausing rhythm differs from prose.
3. Ask: what does this poem say that the prose around it does not? What does it add?
4. Look at the poem's speaker — god, emperor, or common person? The social position of
   the speaker shapes what the poem means.
5. Check Chamberlain's footnotes for that poem — he often explains specific imagery or
   a word's etymology.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Treating the poems as ornamental**: They are not decorations on the prose narrative.
  They are often its oldest layer.
- **Skipping poems because the translation seems awkward**: Chamberlain's poem translations
  are sometimes clunky. Use a footnote or a second translation rather than skipping.
- **Missing the political dimension of poetry attribution**: When a poem is "spoken by"
  Emperor Jimmu, this is a political act. The poem authorizes Jimmu; Jimmu's authority
  authorizes the poem.
- **Expecting modern lyric poetry conventions**: The poems in the Kojiki are deeply
  embedded in specific dramatic moments. They are less like a lyric in isolation and
  more like an aria in an opera — they need the surrounding drama to mean fully.

  What is the relationship being enacted?

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Were the poems in the Kojiki composed specifically for the text, or are they embedded
  artifacts from earlier oral tradition? Most scholars believe the latter, but the exact
  relationship between poem, oral context, and written frame is still debated.
- How should we understand the waka in the Kojiki in relation to the Man'yōshū (completed
  c. 759 CE), the first great imperial poetry anthology? The two texts are nearly
  contemporary; their poems share imagery, forms, and some authors.
- To what extent does Motoori Norinaga's concept of mono no aware — which he derived
  largely from the Tale of Genji and Man'yōshū — retroactively shape how we read the
  emotional registers of Kojiki poetry?

## Connections to Adjacent Topics

- The relationship of poem to prose narrative in the Kojiki is analogous to the psalms
  embedded in Hebrew narrative texts (e.g., Exodus 15, Judges 5) — ancient songs
  given a prose frame that recontextualizes and legitimizes them. This is a bridge
  that may resonate for Michael given his interest in early scriptural literature.
- The function of poetry as emotional intensification at moments of crisis appears across
  world literary traditions: Greek tragedy with its choral odes, the Song of Songs embedded
  in Hebrew scripture, the laments in the Mahabharata.
