# LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100: The Kojiki — Reading Japan's Oldest Chronicle

## Course Description

The *Kojiki* ("Records of Ancient Matters," 712 CE) is Japan's oldest surviving literary
work — a text of creation myths, divine genealogies, heroic narratives, and embedded poems
that forms the bedrock of Shinto mythology and the ideological foundation of the Japanese
imperial line. In this course you will read it closely, in Basil Hall Chamberlain's 1882
translation, learning to navigate its Victorian prose and extensive scholarly footnotes.
More importantly, you will learn to ask the right questions: not "did this really happen?"
but "what is this narrative doing, who told it, and why?"

## Prerequisites

None. No prior knowledge of Japanese history, mythology, or language is required.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

- Explain the Kojiki's political origins and describe the roles of Ō no Yasumaro, Hieda no
  Are, and Empress Genmei in its compilation
- Identify the major deities of the Kojiki's cosmological narrative and trace the genealogical
  chain from primordial creation to Emperor Jimmu
- Read Chamberlain's 1882 translation productively — understanding its strengths, its
  Victorian biases, and how to use its footnotes
- Analyze key mythological episodes (the Ama-no-Iwato, the kuni-yuzuri, Tenson Kōrin) for
  their political and theological function, not just their narrative content
- Distinguish the waka poems embedded in the text from the surrounding prose and explain
  what the poems do that prose cannot
- Trace the Kojiki's reception history from Motoori Norinaga's 18th-century commentary
  through State Shinto's weaponization and post-WWII scholarly rehabilitation
- Apply the scholarly concept of "myth" to explain how ancient narratives function in
  political and religious contexts

## Required Materials

- *The Kojiki: Ancient Japanese Chronicles*, Basil Hall Chamberlain translation (your
  physical book — any edition of the Chamberlain translation is appropriate)
- The Wikisource edition of the text is available free at:
  https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)
  (useful for searching and cross-referencing)

## Recommended (Not Required)

- Gustav Heldt (trans.), *The Kojiki: An Account of Ancient Matters* (Columbia University
  Press, 2014) — a modern translation worth consulting alongside Chamberlain for difficult
  passages and the poetry

## Assessment Plan

- **Session quizzes**: After each session, 4–6 questions mixing recall and application
- **Midterm**: After Session 04 — a short analytical essay (500–700 words) responding to
  a specific prompt drawn from the Age of the Gods
- **Final project**: A comparative or argumentative essay (800–1200 words) synthesizing
  the full course arc — myth, politics, poetry, and reception

## A Note on Pacing

Each session pairs a chunk of the Kojiki text with the analytical framework needed to read
it well. Before each session, read the assigned sections in your Chamberlain translation —
even if parts are confusing. The lesson will address the confusion directly. Plan for one
to two hours of reading per session, plus one to two hours for the lesson and exercises.
If a myth or concept isn't clicking, bring it to the tutor as a specific question. The
Kojiki rewards slow, curious reading; it does not reward rushing. If you find yourself
bored by the genealogies, ask what the genealogy is *doing* — the answer is almost always
politically interesting.
