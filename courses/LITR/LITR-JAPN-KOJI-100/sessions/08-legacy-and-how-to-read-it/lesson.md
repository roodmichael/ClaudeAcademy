# Session 08: Legacy and How to Read It
**Course:** LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to trace the Kojiki's reception history from its long obscurity through Norinaga's commentary, State Shinto's appropriation, and post-WWII rehabilitation — and apply the scholarly concept of "myth" to explain how the Kojiki has functioned at different historical moments.

---

## Opening

In 1945, the Emperor of Japan broadcast a recorded message over the radio — the first time
most Japanese people had ever heard his voice. In that message, he announced Japan's
surrender and renounced his own divinity.

The divinity he renounced had been established, in part, by the Kojiki.

The story of how an 8th-century collection of myths about divine origins came to be used
as the theological foundation for a modern militarist state — and then had to be publicly
disclaimed — is not a footnote to this course. It is one of its central subjects. The
Kojiki is not just an interesting ancient text. It is a text with consequences.

---

## The Long Obscurity

We covered this briefly in Session 01, but it is worth dwelling on. The Kojiki was compiled
in 712 CE. It was largely ignored for the next nine hundred years.

During the Heian period (794–1185), the text that mattered was the *Nihon Shoki* — the
parallel chronicle written in pure Classical Chinese. Court scholars read the Nihon Shoki.
The Kojiki, with its awkward mixed script and archaic Japanese phonetics, was considered
difficult and peripheral.

The Kojiki was not even printed until 1644, nearly a millennium after its composition.

This matters for how we read it. The Kojiki did not naturally evolve into Japan's founding
text. It was *constructed* as such, through a deliberate intellectual movement, by scholars
who had a specific ideological agenda.

---

## Kokugaku: The Recovery of "Native" Japan

In the Edo period (1603–1868), Japan was under the governance of the Tokugawa shogunate.
The intellectual culture of the period was dominated by Neo-Confucian thought and Buddhist
learning — both heavily Chinese in origin. A group of scholars began pushing back.

**Kokugaku** (国学, "native studies" or "national learning") was an intellectual movement
devoted to recovering authentic Japanese identity from ancient Japanese texts — before they
were, in the kokugaku scholars' view, contaminated by Chinese influence.

The progression of key figures:

**Keichū** (1640–1701): A Buddhist monk who applied rigorous historical linguistics to
the Man'yōshū (the great 8th-century poetry anthology). He demonstrated that ancient
Japanese could be read systematically — that its grammar, vocabulary, and phonetics could
be recovered through careful analysis.

**Kada no Azumamaro** (1669–1736): Institutionalized kokugaku as a scholarly program.

**Kamo no Mabuchi** (1697–1769): Focused on the Man'yōshū; developed a concept of ancient
Japanese simplicity and "masculine directness" as superior to Chinese sophistication.

**Motoori Norinaga** (1730–1801): The movement's apex. He turned from the Man'yōshū to
the Kojiki itself — the most difficult and least-read of the ancient texts — and spent
34 years on it.

---

## Motoori Norinaga and the Kojiki-den

Motoori Norinaga was a physician from Matsusaka. He met Kamo no Mabuchi only once —
famously, in 1763 — but that meeting redirected his scholarly life toward the Kojiki.

He produced the **Kojiki-den** — a 44-volume philological commentary on the entire Kojiki,
completed in 1798. It is one of the most intensive scholarly engagements with a single
text in world intellectual history.

### His Philological Method

Norinaga treated the Kojiki as a *makoto no fumi* ("True Book") and approached it with
a kind of fundamentalist confidence: the text says what it says, and modern readers have
been prevented from understanding it by layers of Chinese intellectual contamination.

His method balanced rigorous historical linguistics (analyzing ancient grammar, the history
of phonetic changes in Japanese, the meaning of archaic vocabulary) with an intuitive
literary sense for emotional register and dramatic intention. He was, by any measure, an
extraordinary scholar.

### His Ideological Claims

Two of Norinaga's central ideas had enormous downstream consequences:

**Mono no aware** (もののあわれ, "the pathos of things"): Norinaga developed this concept
primarily from the *Tale of Genji* and the Man'yōshū, then argued it was the fundamental
quality of Japanese literary sensibility — a sensitivity to beauty, transience, and
emotional resonance that he claimed was distinctively Japanese and superior to the moral
and didactic emphasis of Chinese literature.

**Kami no michi** ("Way of the Kami"): Norinaga constructed a form of Shinto grounded
entirely in Kojiki analysis. He argued that the Kojiki revealed Japan's original religious
nature — a universe governed by kami whose operations are beyond human understanding. He
explicitly rejected Confucian moralizing and Buddhist metaphysics as foreign impositions.

His most pointed argument: the *Nihon Shoki*, written in Chinese, was contaminated by the
Chinese intellectual framework of its compilers. The Kojiki, precisely because its mixed
script preserved archaic Japanese phonetics, was more authentically Japanese.

**The political implication** Norinaga did not fully develop himself but made possible:
if the Kojiki reveals Japan's true nature, and the Kojiki establishes the emperor as the
literal descendant of Amaterasu, then the emperor's authority is not political but cosmic.

## State Shinto and the Meiji Weaponization

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, which ended the shogunate and restored direct imperial
rule, kokugaku's ideas became government ideology.

**State Shinto** was the apparatus through which this happened:
- Shinto was separated from Buddhism and established as Japan's national religion
- The emperor was declared to be *arahitogami* — a living kami, literally divine
- The Kojiki's divine genealogy was taught in schools as literal history
- The founding date of 660 BCE was treated as established fact
- Japan's imperial mission was given a cosmic justification: the divine land ruled by
  the divine emperor was naturally and properly the center of Asia

Scholars who questioned this were suppressed. **Tsuda Sōkichi's** 1913 study — which
argued the Kojiki was political literature, not history — was banned. Its author was
prosecuted.

This ideology contributed directly to the expansionism, colonialism, and militarism of
the early 20th century. The Kojiki was part of the ideological infrastructure of a war
that killed tens of millions of people.

---

## After 1945: Rehabilitation and Reassessment

Japan's defeat in World War II forced a comprehensive reassessment. Allied occupation
authorities required the formal disestablishment of State Shinto in December 1945.
Emperor Hirohito's *Humanity Declaration* of January 1946 renounced his divinity.

Tsuda's work was rehabilitated. His framework — the Kojiki as political literature, not
history — became the new scholarly consensus.

Since 1945, several important scholarly positions have emerged:

**The Kojiki as literary text**: Scholars read it for its narrative artistry, its poetic
traditions, its insights into early Japanese culture — without requiring it to be either
history or scripture.

**The Kojiki as source material for religious history**: Historians of Shinto use it as
evidence for early religious practices and beliefs, while carefully distinguishing the
8th-century compilation from any "original" pre-historic Shinto.

**The Kojiki as political document**: Following Tsuda's lead, scholars analyze the text
for what it reveals about 8th-century court politics — Yasumaro's choices, the myths
included vs. excluded, the genealogical arguments embedded in seemingly neutral narrative.

**Nihonjinron ("theories of Japaneseness")**: Post-WWII, the explicitly racial claims of
State Shinto were replaced by a subtler cultural essentialism — the idea that Japan and
the Japanese have uniquely distinctive characteristics rooted in ancient traditions.
This discourse, visible in business books, cultural studies, and popular media, is the
attenuated descendant of kokugaku.

## What "Myth" Means

The word "myth" requires one more careful look here, in the context of everything you
have learned about the Kojiki.

In popular usage, "myth" means "a false story." This is not what scholars mean.

A myth is a narrative that a community uses to explain its origins, values, and cosmic
order. Myths are not simply believed or disbelieved — they are:
- **Performed**: rituals reenact them
- **Inhabited**: cultural identity is organized around them
- **Argued over**: different factions interpret them differently
- **Weaponized**: political actors use them for purposes beyond explanation
- **Critiqued**: scholars and reformers challenge their authority

The Kojiki is a myth in this sense. The question is not whether Izanagi stirred the ocean
with a spear. The question is: what has this narrative done in history? Who has used it
and for what? What happens when a community decides to treat it as literal fact, or when
it decides to read it as literature?

You have now read enough of the text to hold those questions with some sophistication.

## How to Keep Reading

Now that you have completed this course, you have the tools to read the Kojiki on your
own terms. A few recommendations:

1. **Reread the sections that confused you** — with the full context of the course, earlier
   episodes will make much more sense than they did on first pass.

2. **Read the poems again**, knowing what you now know about what they are doing. They will
   feel different from early in the course.

3. **Read Norinaga at least once** — his *Kojiki-den* is not available in full English
   translation, but selections appear in several anthologies of Japanese intellectual
   history. Reading his commentary on even one passage will show you what sophisticated
   native scholarship looks like.

4. **Compare Chamberlain and Heldt on a passage you know well** — the comparison will
   illuminate both translators' choices.

5. **Ask what you notice now that you didn't notice before** — in a text this dense and
   layered, a second reading is always a different book.

## Key Takeaways

- The Kojiki was largely ignored for nearly a millennium after its composition; its
  elevation to foundational status is a construction of the Edo period and Meiji era.
- Motoori Norinaga's 44-volume Kojiki-den (1798) is one of the most intensive scholarly
  engagements with a single text in world literary history; his concepts of mono no aware
  and kami no michi shaped how Japan read itself for 150 years.
- State Shinto weaponized the Kojiki's divine genealogy to justify the doctrine of imperial
  divinity and, ultimately, Japanese imperial expansionism.
- Post-WWII scholarship, following Tsuda Sōkichi, rehabilitated the Kojiki as political
  literature and literary text, while disavowing its use as historical or scriptural authority.
- "Myth" in the scholarly sense means a community's narrative of origins and values —
  not a false story, but a story with consequences, which is more interesting.

## Congratulations

You have read Japan's oldest chronicle. You have moved from Izanagi stirring the primordial
ocean to the post-WWII reckoning with what the text had been made to mean. You have read
poems that are over a thousand years old and asked what they were doing there, in that
dramatic moment, sung by that god or emperor.

This is what literary study is for: not to consume stories passively, but to read with
your full intelligence engaged — asking who, to whom, when, why, and at what cost.

The Kojiki rewards that kind of reading. So does every great text.
