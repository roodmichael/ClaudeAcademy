# Session 01: What Is the Kojiki?
**Course:** LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain where the Kojiki came from, why it was written, and how to read Basil Hall Chamberlain's 1882 translation productively.

---

## Opening

Here is a strange fact: Japan's oldest surviving literary work — a text studied in schools,
quoted in politics, cited in religious ceremonies, and treated as something like a national
scripture — was almost entirely ignored for a thousand years after it was written.

For most of Japanese history, scholars didn't read the Kojiki. They read a different text,
the *Nihon Shoki*, compiled just eight years later. The Kojiki sat in relative obscurity
through the entire Heian and medieval periods. It wasn't even printed until 1644 — nine
hundred and thirty-two years after its composition.

What you hold in your hands is a text that has been compiled, forgotten, rediscovered,
reinterpreted, weaponized, denounced, and rehabilitated. Learning to read it means learning
to hold all of that history simultaneously.

So: before we read a single myth, let's ask who wrote it, for whom, and why.

---

## A Text Born from Political Crisis

The Kojiki did not emerge from a neutral moment. Japan in the late 7th century CE was
navigating multiple crises at once:

**The problem of legitimacy.** The ruling Yamato clan had consolidated power over the
Japanese islands, but their authority rested on increasingly contested claims. Rival noble
families — called **uji** — were circulating their own genealogical documents, many of them
fabricated or inflated, each asserting the family's ancient, divine origins. By the 670s
CE, the documentary landscape was, in the words of one modern scholar, "a mess of forgeries."

**The problem of China.** Japan had spent the 7th century absorbing Chinese court culture
at an extraordinary rate — its administrative system, its legal codes, its writing system,
its Buddhism. China had an impressive tradition of official historical chronicles stretching
back centuries. If Japan was to take its place among civilized nations, it needed a founding
narrative of comparable prestige.

**The problem of oral tradition.** Whatever genuine accounts existed of Japan's divine
origins and imperial genealogy were circulating orally. Oral traditions can be altered,
corrupted, or simply lost. Emperor Tenmu (r. 673–686 CE) recognized the danger and took
action: he commissioned a court attendant named **Hieda no Are** to memorize the correct
versions of the genealogical records and oral traditions.

Tenmu died before anything was written down. The project stalled.

---

## Ō no Yasumaro and the Commission

In 711 CE, Empress Genmei (r. 707–715 CE) revived the project. She ordered a court
scholar named **Ō no Yasumaro** to transcribe what Hieda no Are had memorized. On the
28th day of the 1st month of 712 CE, Yasumaro presented the completed work.

Yasumaro is not simply a scribe. His preface — which you should read before anything else
in Chamberlain's translation — is itself a political document. In it, Yasumaro explains the
text's purpose with remarkable clarity: to preserve the correct traditions, to expose the
existing forgeries, and to establish an authoritative account of the imperial lineage. He
is simultaneously a literary craftsman and a political agent.

The text he produced has two main goals:
1. **Reinforce that the emperor descended from the gods**, specifically from Amaterasu,
   the sun goddess.
2. **Determine the proper rank of the noble families** by giving each an established place
   in the divine genealogy.

This is not history in the modern sense. It is **mythologized politics** — an act of
statecraft wrapped in narrative.

---

## The Three Books

The Kojiki is organized into three volumes:

**Kamitsumaki (Upper Volume)**: The Age of the Gods. Primordial creation, Izanagi and
Izanami, Amaterasu and Susanoo, Ōkuninushi, and Ninigi's descent. This is the mythological
heart of the text — the material most studied and most quoted.

**Nakatsumaki (Middle Volume)**: The semi-historical period. Emperor Jimmu (the legendary
first emperor) through Emperor Ōjin (the 15th). The prose shifts from pure myth toward
legend — still largely unverifiable, but structured as sequential reigns.

**Shimotsumaki (Lower Volume)**: The later emperors (16th through 33rd). The material
becomes increasingly sparse and less mythological. Cross-referencing with Chinese and
Korean chronicles becomes possible for the final 250 years or so.

The further you read into the Kojiki, the less mythological and more political it becomes.
The further back you go, the more the political function is embedded in cosmic narrative.

---

## The Kojiki's Companion: The Nihon Shoki

Eight years after the Kojiki, a second chronicle was compiled: the **Nihon Shoki** (720
CE). This is not a sequel — it's a parallel document with different goals.

The Nihon Shoki is written in pure Classical Chinese, the international prestige language
of East Asia. It was designed to be readable and impressive to Chinese and Korean audiences.
It often presents the same myths as the Kojiki, but in more polished prose — and sometimes
with multiple variant versions of the same story.

The Kojiki is **inward-looking**: written in a mixed Chinese-Japanese style that preserves
archaic Japanese phonetics, aimed at a domestic audience, and focused on establishing a
distinctly Japanese divine origin.

When you encounter scholars debating what a Kojiki passage "really means," they often
compare it with the Nihon Shoki version. For this course, we stay with the Kojiki — but
knowing the Nihon Shoki exists helps you understand what the Kojiki is and is not.

---

## Your Translator: Basil Hall Chamberlain

The book you are holding is Basil Hall Chamberlain's 1882 translation — the first rendering
of the Kojiki into any European language. Chamberlain (1850–1935) was a British scholar
who lived in Japan for nearly three decades and taught Japanese language at Tokyo Imperial
University. He was the foremost British Japanologist of his era.

His translation is extraordinary by any measure: meticulous, literally faithful, and
accompanied by hundreds of scholarly footnotes drawing on native Japanese commentaries
(especially the 44-volume commentary of Motoori Norinaga). But it is also a document of
its time, and you need to know its limitations:

**The Latin problem.** The Kojiki contains explicit sexual and scatological content.
Chamberlain rendered these passages in Latin, leaving them untranslated for his Victorian
audience. When you encounter Latin in Chamberlain, that is the text being too explicit for
1882 England. The original is more visceral.

**The orientalist lens.** Chamberlain approached Japanese culture within the evolutionary
framework typical of Victorian scholarship. He described early Japanese as "a race who had
long emerged from the savage state, and had attained to a high level of barbaric skill."
This framing — positioning non-Western cultures on a developmental ladder toward European
civilization — is historically important but intellectually mistaken. Recognize it when you
see it.

**Victorian prose.** His sentences are long and formal. The translation can feel remote.
If a passage is genuinely confusing, the 2014 Heldt translation is available in most
university libraries and offers a modern prose alternative.

### How to Read Chamberlain's Footnotes

The footnotes are one of the translation's most valuable features. They fall into several
distinct types:

- **Philological notes**: What a Japanese word or phrase means, its etymology, variant
  manuscript readings. These are usually gold. Read them when they appear.
- **Comparative mythology notes**: Chamberlain's parallels to Greek, Norse, or other
  world myths. Suggestive, not proven — treat them as invitations, not conclusions.
- **Commentary notes**: Summaries of what Norinaga or other Japanese scholars said about
  a passage. Valuable, but remember Norinaga has his own ideological agenda.
- **Editorial confessions**: Places where Chamberlain admits uncertainty. Treasure these —
  intellectual honesty about difficulty is a sign of a serious scholar.

## Why This Matters

Here is what will happen over the next eight sessions: you are going to learn to read a
text in layers. The myth on the surface. The politics embedded in the myth. The translator's
assumptions embedded in the translation. The commentator's ideology embedded in the footnotes.
And beneath all of that, the very ancient voices — the poets who sang love songs and war
songs that eventually found their way, through layers of transcription and interpretation,
into your hands.

This is what literary study does. It doesn't just ask "what happens in this story." It asks
"who told this story, to whom, when, and to what end." The Kojiki is an extraordinarily
rich subject for this kind of reading, because almost every answer to those questions is
contested and interesting.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Kojiki (712 CE) was compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from the memorized traditions of
  Hieda no Are, at the order of Empress Genmei, for the explicit purpose of establishing
  imperial legitimacy and suppressing forged genealogies.
- It is organized in three books: mythological (Upper), semi-historical (Middle), and
  proto-historical (Lower).
- The Nihon Shoki (720 CE) is a parallel, not a sequel — different goals, different
  audience, different style.
- Chamberlain's 1882 translation is scholarly and literally faithful, but Victorian in its
  biases (orientalist framing, Latin euphemism) and prose style.
- The footnotes are worth reading selectively; they fall into distinct types with different
  levels of authority.
