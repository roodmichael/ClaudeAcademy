# Session 02: The Age of the Gods, Part I
**Course:** LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to describe the Kojiki's cosmogony, identify the major figures of the first divine generations, and analyze the Izanagi and Izanami narrative as both myth and political argument.

---

## Opening

Before you were born, seven pairs of gods appeared and disappeared in the Plain of High
Heaven. Each pair was more active than the last. The world was not yet solid — the Kojiki
describes it as "resembling floating oil and drifting like a jellyfish." Then, from the
eight generations, a divine couple descended to the Floating Bridge of Heaven, stirred
the ocean with a jeweled spear, and let the brine drip. The drops fell and hardened into
the first island.

No single creator. No divine plan announced in advance. Instead: a carefully ordered
procession of divine generations, each one preparing the way for the next, until the
moment of active creation is finally possible.

This is a different kind of cosmogony than most Western readers have encountered. It is
worth pausing to ask: what kind of universe does this opening describe? And what does it
imply about the nature of the gods who inhabit it?

---

## The Seven Generations of Heavenly Gods

The Kojiki opens not with Izanagi and Izanami, but with a procession. Five solitary deities
appear in the **High Plain of Heaven** (Takamagahara) and then immediately "hide their
persons" — they exist, briefly, and then withdraw. They are called the **Separate Heavenly
Deities** (Kotoamatsukami). They have no narrative — only names and an existence.

Then come pairs of deities, two more generations. Then the **Seven Generations of the
Age of the Gods** proper — seven pairs of brother-and-sister deities who appear, exist,
and vanish. With each generation, the text seems to be counting down to something.

The final pair of this procession is **Izanagi** and **Izanami** — "He-Who-Invites" and
"She-Who-Invites." They are the eighth generation, the culmination of the countdown, the
first to actually *do* anything.

This structural choice matters. The Kojiki does not begin with creation — it begins with
a divine lineage. The world does not appear; it is *descended to* through a carefully
ordered divine genealogy. The genealogical form (A begat B, who begat C) that structures
the human sections of the Kojiki appears here at the very start of time, embedded in the
cosmogony itself. The universe is organized, from the beginning, like a noble family tree.

---

## The Floating Bridge of Heaven and the First Island

The Separate Heavenly Deities give Izanagi and Izanami a **jeweled spear** (the
Heavenly Jewel Spear, *Ame-no-nuboko*) and command them to "make, consolidate, and give
birth to this drifting land." The couple descend to the **Floating Bridge of Heaven**
(*Ame-no-ukihashi*) — a celestial bridge connecting the heavenly plain to the formless
below.

They thrust the spear downward and stir the brine. When they withdraw the spear, the
brine that drips from its tip congeals into an island: **Onogoro** ("Self-Solidifying").
They descend to this island and erect a great pillar.

This is creation not as divine fiat (God said "Let there be light") but as craft. The gods
are doing something — a physical, almost artisanal act. They are stirring, solidifying,
dripping. The universe is made rather than commanded.

---

## The First Marriage and the Lopsided Child

Izanagi and Izanami decide to marry. They walk around the central pillar — but they do
it wrong the first time. Izanami speaks first: "What a delightful and lovely youth!" Then
Izanagi responds.

The deities they consult tell them this is backwards: the male should speak first. They
do it again, correctly — Izanagi speaks first — and their union proceeds.

Their first attempt produces a child who is born without bones, the **Leech-Child** (Hiruko),
who is set adrift in a reed boat and plays no further role in the narrative. Their second
try produces an island called Foam-Island, also considered a failure.

Only the third — the properly ordered marriage — produces the real islands of Japan: Awaji,
Shikoku, Oki, Kyushu, the other islands of the archipelago. The Japanese islands are
literally the children of the divine couple.

### What is this episode doing?

The ritual ordering of the marriage (male speaks first) is almost certainly a legitimation
of actual ritual practice. The text is explaining *why* a certain ceremonial form is correct
by grounding it in the founding moment of creation. Getting it wrong produced a malformed
child; getting it right produced Japan. This is how mythological texts justify social and
ritual norms: not by argument, but by narrative. The "right" way is simply the way the
gods did it when it worked.

---

## Izanami's Death and the Descent into Yomi

The couple continue creating — gods of sea, wind, trees, mountains, plains, food. But
when Izanami gives birth to the fire god **Kagutchi**, she is burned so severely in the
process that she dies.

Izanagi's grief is overwhelming. He weeps over her body; from his tears, more deities
are born. Then, enraged, he kills Kagutchi. From the blood and body of the slain fire
god — eight separate body parts giving birth to eight more deities — creation continues
even through violence and death.

Izanagi then descends to **Yomi** ("the land of the dead") to retrieve his wife.

Izanami's voice comes from the darkness: she wants to return, but she has already eaten
the food of Yomi. She must consult with the rulers of the underworld. She tells him: "Do
not look at me."

Izanagi waits. Then, unable to bear the wait any longer, he breaks off a tooth from his
comb and lights it as a torch.

He looks.

What he sees is Izanami's rotting body, swarming with maggots and thunder-gods. He
turns and runs. She, shamed and furious, sends the thunder-gods and Yomi-women to pursue
him. He throws peaches at them to slow them down. At the entrance to the underworld, he
rolls a great boulder between them.

They exchange words through the boulder. She says she will kill a thousand people per day.
He says he will cause fifteen hundred to be born. And so death and birth are set in their
fixed relationship. Then they separate forever.

### What is this episode doing?

Multiple things at once. It establishes **Yomi** as a real cosmological location, explains
where death comes from, and creates the divine logic of why the dead are polluting. But
it also does something else: it explains why Izanagi has to purify himself after leaving
Yomi — and that purification is the act that produces the three most important deities in
the entire text.

---

## Purification and the Three Great Children

Izanagi emerges from the underworld and purifies himself in a river — washing off the
pollution of Yomi. The act of **misogi** (ritual purification by water) is among the most
important practices in Shinto, and this is its origin story.

As he washes, deities spring from the garments and bodily fluids he discards. But the most
important births come from the act of washing his face:

- Washing his **left eye**: **Amaterasu Ōmikami** — the Great Divinity Illuminating Heaven,
  the sun goddess, the supreme ruler of Takamagahara
- Washing his **right eye**: **Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto** — the moon god
- Washing his **nose**: **Susanoo-no-Mikoto** — the storm god

Izanagi is overwhelmed with joy. These are his greatest children, and he names them the
**Three Precious Children** (Mihashira no uzu no mikoto). He assigns them their realms:
Amaterasu rules heaven, Tsukuyomi rules the night, Susanoo rules the seas.

Then he removes his necklace and gives it to Amaterasu — symbolically investing her with
supreme authority.

### Why does this matter politically?

Because Amaterasu is the direct ancestor of the imperial line. Every subsequent emperor
traces lineage back through Amaterasu. The story of her birth — from the act of purification
that ended the Izanagi-Izanami narrative — makes her the culmination of the entire creation
sequence. The Kojiki has structured its opening cosmogony so that the divine progenitor
of the imperial family is not just *one* of the gods but the greatest of all of them,
born at the moment of the universe's most important cleansing.

---

## Why This Matters

You are reading the ideological foundation of a ruling dynasty. The myths are beautiful and
often genuinely strange — but beneath them is a political argument being made in narrative
form: the emperor is not just powerful, not just ancient, but *cosmologically prior* to
everything else. The universe was organized, from its very first moments, to produce the
imperial lineage.

Recognizing this doesn't make the myths less interesting. If anything, it makes them more
so: a narrative doing political work while simultaneously being a genuine attempt to
describe how the world came to be.

---

## Key Takeaways

- The Kojiki's cosmogony is a procession: seven generations of divine pairs counting down
  to the moment when Izanagi and Izanami can begin active creation.
- Creation is an artisanal act (stirring the brine with a jeweled spear), not a divine
  command. The universe is made, not spoken into existence.
- The first marriage establishes ritual order (male speaks first), and its initial failure
  explains why that order is correct — a pattern of myth legitimating social norms.
- Izanami's death and Izanagi's failed underworld rescue establishes the cosmological
  logic of death, pollution, and purification in Shinto.
- The Three Great Children (Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, Susanoo) are born from Izanagi's
  purification — the act that ends the Izanami story is the act that begins the next phase.
