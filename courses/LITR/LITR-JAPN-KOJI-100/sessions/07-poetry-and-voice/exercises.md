# Session 07 Exercises

Complete these before taking the session quiz.

---

## Exercise 1: Find a Poem, Map Its Moment
**Type:** Reading + Analysis

Find two poems in your Chamberlain — one from the Upper Volume (the Age of the Gods)
and one from the Middle Volume (the human age). They will be in verse form, usually
introduced by phrases like "he sang" or "therefore he said."

**For each poem, record:**

1. The name of the speaker (god, emperor, or unnamed character)
2. The dramatic situation: what is happening in the narrative at the moment the poem is
   sung? (2–3 sentences)
3. The emotional register of the poem: what feeling does it express?
4. What the poem says that the surrounding prose does not — what does it add or intensify?
5. If there is a footnote for the poem, briefly note what Chamberlain's footnote says.

## Exercise 2: Identify the Form
**Type:** Analysis

Using the definitions of waka forms from the lesson (katauta, sedōka, chōka, tanka),
attempt to identify the form of each poem you selected in Exercise 1.

Note: This requires counting syllables in Japanese, which you cannot do precisely in
English translation. Instead, use the structure as a guide:

- Is it two short units? It may be sedōka (two katauta joined) or two katauta in dialogue.
- Is it unusually long, with a flowing structure? It may be chōka.
- Is it a short, complete statement that fits in roughly five lines? It may be tanka.

Chamberlain's footnotes sometimes identify the form explicitly. Check them.

**What to record:**

For each poem: your best guess at the form, your reasoning, and whether Chamberlain
(or a footnote) confirms or complicates your reading.

## Exercise 3: Write a Short Essay — What Poems Do
**Type:** Written analysis (200–300 words)

This exercise is the warm-up for the midterm essay.

Write a short analytical passage responding to this prompt:

**"The waka poems in the Kojiki are not ornamental. They are the text's oldest layer and
its most direct voice. Discuss."**

Use the two poems you analyzed in Exercise 1 as evidence. You do not need to argue that
the poems are "better" than the prose. You need to argue (or complicate, or qualify) the
claim that the poems do something the prose cannot.

You may agree with the claim, disagree with it, or present a more nuanced position — but
you must support your position with specific textual evidence from the poems you read.
