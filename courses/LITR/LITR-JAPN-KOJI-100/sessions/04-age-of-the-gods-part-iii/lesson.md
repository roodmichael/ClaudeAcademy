# Session 04: The Age of the Gods, Part III
**Course:** LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to describe Ōkuninushi's narrative role as a culture hero, explain the kuni-yuzuri (land cession) as a political argument embedded in myth, and identify how this episode prepares the ground for Ninigi's descent.

---

## Opening

Here is a puzzle: why would the Kojiki spend so much time on a deity who ultimately loses?

Ōkuninushi rules the earthly realm. He builds it, cultivates it, establishes medicine and
agriculture. He is the master of a rich, prosperous land. And then the heavenly gods send
a messenger demanding he give it all up. He considers this — and says yes.

That scene, called the **kuni-yuzuri** (land cession), is one of the most politically
revealing episodes in the entire text. To understand it, you need to understand both why
it is there and what it was doing for the people who first told it.

---

## Ōkuninushi: Who He Is

**Ōkuninushi** ("Great Land Master") is a descendant of Susanoo — his precise genealogical
relationship varies across different tellings, but he is consistently of the Susanoo lineage,
not the Amaterasu lineage. This matters: he is a ruler of earthly origins, not a heavenly
one.

His story occupies a large portion of the Upper Volume and is itself a collection of earlier,
probably independent myths woven together. Unlike the stories of Izanagi and Amaterasu,
which have a single focused arc, Ōkuninushi's narrative is episodic — a series of trials,
adventures, and accomplishments.

### The Rabbit of Inaba

One of the Kojiki's most beloved episodes introduces Ōkuninushi through an act of
compassion. He is traveling to Inaba to court a princess. Along the way he encounters
a white rabbit lying skinned and agonized on the ground. His eighty elder brothers, who
traveled ahead of him, had mocked the rabbit's predicament. Ōkuninushi stops and helps it.

The rabbit tells him how it came to be there: it had been trying to cross the ocean and
tricked a crocodile (or, in some translations, a shark or seal) into forming a bridge with
its fellows, counting them as it ran across. The last crocodile realized the trick and
tore off the rabbit's skin in revenge.

Ōkuninushi bathes the rabbit in fresh water and has it roll in cattail pollen, soothing
its wounds. The rabbit, healed, prophesies: "You, great name, will win the princess."

This episode establishes Ōkuninushi's character before the political argument begins: he
is the one who stops, notices suffering, and acts. His eighty proud brothers did not.
The capacity for compassion that makes him a suitable culture hero — and, paradoxically,
a suitable candidate for the dignified abdication to come — is introduced here.

### Trials by Susanoo

To win the hand of Susanoo's daughter Suseribime, Ōkuninushi must pass a series of trials
in Susanoo's underworld palace. He is placed in a room full of snakes, then centipedes
and wasps, then forced to survive a fire. Each time, Suseribime provides him with a magical
scarf that drives off the creatures.

Susanoo sets one more trap: he falls asleep with Ōkuninushi combing his hair, then uses
this moment to show dominance — his hair is full of centipedes. Ōkuninushi uses berries
to simulate the sound of crushing them without disturbing Susanoo, then ties Susanoo's
hair to the palace rafters and escapes with Suseribime and the weapons hanging in the
palace.

Susanoo wakes and pursues them to the border of the underworld. There, instead of
killing them, he shouts advice to Ōkuninushi on how to defeat his eighty brothers.
He addresses him by a new, royal name: **Ōkuninushi**. The former antagonist has become
a sponsor.

### Kuniizukuri: Building the Land

With Susanoo's blessing and his own force of character, Ōkuninushi returns to the earthly
realm and defeats his eighty brothers. He consolidates his rule over **Ashihara no
Nakatsukuni** (the "Central Land of Reed Plains") — the earthly realm.

His great project is **kuniizukuri** ("building of the land"): with his companion
**Sukunabikona** (a small deity of great wisdom who arrives in a boat and becomes his
partner), Ōkuninushi develops agriculture, medicine, brewing techniques for sake and other
fermented products, and techniques for driving away disease. He is a culture hero in the
fullest sense — not a conqueror but a builder.

Sukunabikona eventually departs for other realms, leaving Ōkuninushi to complete the
work alone. It is at this moment — the realm built, the culture hero at the height of his
power — that the heavenly gods intervene.

## The Kuni-Yuzuri: The Land Cession

Amaterasu looks down from Takamagahara and sees that the earthly realm is fully developed.
She announces that it should be ruled by her grandson Ninigi. But there is an obstacle:
Ōkuninushi is already there.

Amaterasu sends a messenger to demand the cession. The first messenger is distracted and
never delivers the message. The second is also distracted. The third is killed. The fourth
(the god Takemikazuchi, a thunder deity, accompanied by Futsunushi) finally succeeds —
not through force, but through presence. He descends to the seashore with his sword
planted in the ocean, and sits on the sword's pommel.

Ōkuninushi asks for time to consult with his son Kotoshironushi, who is out fishing.
Kotoshironushi is called back; he strikes the boat to capsize it, capsizes it into a "green
fence" (disappearing into the sea), and approves the cession. Ōkuninushi's other son
Takeminakata challenges Takemikazuchi to a wrestling match and loses, fleeing to the shore
of Lake Suwa in Nagano, where he remains (in the real world, the Suwa Grand Shrine is
dedicated to him).

Ōkuninushi then consents:

> "These two children of mine have now both expressed their opinions, and there is nothing
> for me to do but obey. I will cede this central land of the reed plains in accordance
> with your commands."

His one request: that a great palace be built for him at Izumo, as grand as the palace of
the heavenly gods, and that his descendants be allowed to continue the divine rites. This
palace is the **Izumo Taisha** — the Izumo Grand Shrine, one of Japan's most important
Shinto sites to this day.

---

## What the Kuni-Yuzuri Is Doing

The land cession is not just a myth. It is a **political charter** — and scholars have
understood it as such since at least the 19th century.

The Izumo region, in what is now Shimane Prefecture, was a powerful semi-independent realm
in early Japanese history. Its chief deity (or patron deity) was Ōkuninushi, and the Izumo
Grand Shrine was among the most important religious sites on the islands. When the Yamato
court consolidated control over Japan, they had to account for Izumo's existing prestige
and power.

The kuni-yuzuri does this elegantly: it acknowledges Ōkuninushi's legitimate greatness
(the realm was genuinely built by him), stages a dignified abdication rather than a
conquest, and guarantees Izumo its most important ongoing role — the maintenance of the
grand shrine and the divine rites. Ōkuninushi does not lose; he is memorialized. The
Izumo clan is given a place in the imperial religious system rather than being erased from it.

This is how political mythology works. It does not say: "we conquered you." It says: "the
gods of heaven and earth agreed that heaven should govern the earth — and the earth's
greatest ruler saw this clearly and consented."

## The Path Is Now Clear

With Ōkuninushi's consent, the earthly realm is ready for Amaterasu's grandson. The next
session begins the bridge episode — Ninigi's descent from heaven to earth, the three
imperial regalia transferred to him, and the transition from the divine to the proto-human
age.

---

## Why This Matters

Ōkuninushi's story is the Kojiki's most sustained portrait of rulership as stewardship
rather than domination. He builds, heals, and cultivates before he governs. And when asked
to give up what he has built, he does so with wisdom and dignity, securing the best outcome
for his descendants.

This makes him one of the Kojiki's most compelling figures — and his story one of its most
politically sophisticated episodes. You are not reading a simple myth of divine conquest.
You are reading a careful negotiation, encoded in narrative, between two real historical
power centers: Yamato and Izumo.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Ōkuninushi is a culture hero descended from Susanoo; his story is episodic and introduces
  him through compassion (the Rabbit of Inaba) and trial (Susanoo's palace tests).
- His kuniizukuri (building of the land) establishes him as the developer of agriculture,
  medicine, and other civilizational goods.
- The kuni-yuzuri (land cession) is a political charter: it explains why Yamato rule
  over the Izumo region is legitimate, and it guarantees Izumo's ongoing religious role.
- Ōkuninushi's single condition — a great shrine — produces the Izumo Taisha, one of
  Japan's most important Shinto sites to this day.
- The kuni-yuzuri is not presented as conquest but as dignified abdication, which is
  itself politically important: it incorporates Izumo into the imperial system rather
  than eliminating it.
