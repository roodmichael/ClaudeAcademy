# Session 03: The Age of the Gods, Part II
**Course:** LITR-JAPN-KOJI-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to trace the narrative arc of the Ama-no-Iwato episode, explain what it accomplishes politically and cosmologically, and analyze Susanoo as a structurally complex figure.

---

## Opening

The sun goes out.

That is the situation in the Kojiki's most dramatic set piece. The sun goddess Amaterasu —
ruler of the High Plain of Heaven, supreme deity, divine ancestor of the imperial line —
seals herself inside a rock cave. Without her, there is no light anywhere. Evil spirits run
unchecked. The eight million gods convene in desperate council.

Everything that follows — the preparations, the dance, the laughter, the mirror, the
wrenching open of the door — is a structured answer to a single question: how do you get
a god to come out of hiding when you cannot force her?

This episode is often called the heart of the Kojiki's mythological section. Understanding
it is essential to understanding everything that comes after.

---

## The Problem of Susanoo

Before we get to the cave, we need to understand who caused it.

Izanagi assigned Susanoo to rule the seas. But Susanoo refuses. Instead he weeps without
ceasing, letting his beard grow down to his chest, causing trees to wither and mountains
to crumble with his grief. He mourns his dead mother, Izanami, and wants to go to Yomi.

Izanagi, furious, exiles him. Before leaving, Susanoo goes to say farewell to his sister
Amaterasu in Takamagahara. This visit is the triggering event for everything that follows.

Amaterasu is suspicious. She believes Susanoo is coming to take her realm. They have a
contest — each takes a possession of the other's and creates deities from it, trying to
prove who can generate the most powerful offspring. The contest is structurally a tie.

But Susanoo, having won (in his own assessment, at least), celebrates — catastrophically.

---

## The Rampage

What Susanoo does in Takamagahara is not simply vandalism. Each act strikes at something
specific and sacred:

**The rice paddies**: He breaks down the ridges between paddy fields, fills in the
irrigation ditches, and buries the harvest channels. Rice cultivation in Japan was a
divine enterprise — the emperor himself performed ritual rice planting. To sabotage
the paddies is to attack the sacred agricultural order.

**The sacred hall**: He defecates in the ceremonial hall where Amaterasu performs divine
rituals. This is not crude behavior — it is desecration.

**The weaving hall**: He flings a skinned piebald horse through the roof of the hall where
Amaterasu's attendants are weaving. The shock kills one of the weaving maidens. Chamberlain
renders the cause of her death in Latin; the original is more explicit and more violent.

Amaterasu's response to this — and this is important — is first to find excuses for him.
"He must have intended no harm," she says of the rice paddy destruction. But the horse
and the weaving maiden she cannot explain away.

---

## Amaterasu Hides: The Ama-no-Iwato

Amaterasu retreats into the **Ama-no-Iwato** (天岩戸) — the Heavenly Rock Cave — and
rolls the door shut. With her inside, both Heaven and Earth fall into darkness. The eight
million deities experience this as catastrophe.

Note what the text describes happening while the cave is sealed: evil spirits make noise
throughout the land, all manner of calamity arises. The withdrawal of divine light is not
just astronomical — it is moral and spiritual disruption.

The eight million gods convene on the Tranquil River of Heaven in desperate council, with
**Omoikane** ("Thought-Combining" or "Accumulating Thoughts"), the god of wisdom, presiding.
Their strategy is not to force the door open — that would fail. It is to make Amaterasu
*want* to come out.

---

## The Luring: Spectacle, Laughter, and Curiosity

The gods prepare an elaborate distraction:

- The smith god **Amenomahitotsu** forges a great mirror — the **Yata no Kagami** —
  and it is hung in a sacred tree outside the cave
- The **Magatama beads** (curved jewels) are also hung in the tree
- **Cocks** are gathered to crow
- A bonfire is lit

Then **Ame-no-Uzume** — a goddess of festivity, dawn, and revelry — begins to dance.
Her dance escalates from ritual movement to something ecstatic and physically uninhibited.
The other gods dissolve into roaring laughter.

Inside the cave, Amaterasu hears the laughter. This is her undoing. She cannot understand:
the world should be in darkness and terror. Why are the gods laughing? She cracks the door
slightly and asks. Ame-no-Uzume tells her: "We are rejoicing because there is a deity more
glorious than you."

Amaterasu looks out — and sees her own reflection in the Yata no Kagami mirror. She is
transfixed by what she sees (and, in some readings, confused: is this the more glorious
deity they mentioned?). While she stands there, the mighty god **Ame-no-Tajikarao** grasps
her hand and pulls her out. Another deity stretches a rope across the entrance, preventing
her return. Light is restored.

### The Political Subtext

The mirror in which Amaterasu sees herself becomes one of the **three imperial regalia**
of Japan — alongside the sword found in Yamata no Orochi's tail and the curved jewels
from this scene. These objects are transmitted from deity to emperor as the physical
tokens of imperial authority.

The Ama-no-Iwato episode thus produces the sacred objects that make the emperor legitimate.
It is not just a myth about the restoration of sunlight — it is the origin story of the
imperial regalia.

---

## What Happens to Susanoo

After Amaterasu emerges, the eight million gods try and punish Susanoo: they fine him
heavily (cutting his fingernails and toenails, pulling out his beard) and expel him from
heaven.

He descends to the province of Izumo — the region on the western coast of Honshu that
will become the center of the next major narrative arc. His descent from heaven is a
structural parallel to the later, more triumphant descent of Ninigi. But where Ninigi
descends as the chosen ruler of the earthly realm, Susanoo descends as an exile.

---

## Susanoo as a Complex Figure

It is tempting to read Susanoo as simply the villain of the Ama-no-Iwato episode and
leave it there. The Kojiki does not let you do this.

The same deity who destroyed the paddies, desecrated the sacred hall, and caused the
death of a weaving maiden will, in the following sections:
- Slay the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi and save a young woman
- Compose the first waka poem in the text — a love song of considerable beauty
- Discover and present to Amaterasu the sword that becomes one of the imperial regalia

This is not an inconsistency. The Kojiki's gods are not simple moral agents who are either
good or bad. Susanoo's violence and destructiveness are real, but so is his heroism. He is
the storm — unpredictable, capable of both destruction and fertility, finally driven into
the earthly realm where his wildness becomes useful.

This complexity matters for reading the text. The Kojiki does not have a devil. Its gods
include agents of chaos who also produce order.

---

## Why This Matters

The Ama-no-Iwato story is one of the most analyzed mythological episodes in Japan. It has
been staged as drama, painted on scrolls, invoked in political speeches, and interpreted
by scholars for over a thousand years. What makes it so durable?

In part: it is structurally satisfying. Problem → consultation → solution → resolution.

In part: it captures something genuine about how communities respond to crisis — collective
deliberation, creative response, humor as a survival mechanism.

But mainly: it produces the objects that make imperial rule sacred. Every time the emperor
uses the mirror in a ceremony, the Ama-no-Iwato is present. The myth is not in the past —
it is the living charter for the present.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Susanoo's refusal to accept his realm and his rampage in Takamagahara trigger the
  Ama-no-Iwato episode — but the text shows him making excuses for his behavior, suggesting
  the situation is more morally complex than simple villainy.
- Amaterasu retreats into the rock cave, causing cosmic darkness; the gods must lure her
  out through spectacle, laughter, and curiosity — they cannot force her.
- The Ama-no-Iwato episode produces two of the three imperial regalia: the Yata no Kagami
  (mirror) and the Magatama beads (jewels).
- Susanoo is expelled from heaven and descends to Izumo — a complex figure, not a simple villain.
- The three imperial regalia (mirror, jewels, sword) are the physical tokens of legitimate
  imperial authority throughout Japanese history, including today.
