# PHIL-100: Classical Philosophy — Foundations

## Course Description

This course traces Western philosophy from its first stirrings in 6th-century Greece through the practical wisdom schools of the Roman world. You will read the actual words of Plato, Aristotle, Epictetus, and Epicurus — not summaries — and wrestle with the questions they opened: What is the world fundamentally made of? What is the difference between knowledge and belief? What makes a human life go well? How do we face death without fear? These questions were not resolved in antiquity. They are still live.

## Prerequisites

None. No prior philosophy background required.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

- Identify the core philosophical questions posed by the Pre-Socratics and explain why they matter
- Analyze the Socratic method and apply it to test a knowledge claim
- Explain Plato's Theory of Forms and the three Republic analogies (Cave, Sun, Divided Line)
- Describe Aristotle's account of eudaimonia and the role of virtue and habit in achieving it
- Distinguish the Stoic and Epicurean approaches to achieving tranquility and facing death
- Construct a structured philosophical argument using only primary texts from the course
- Read pre-modern philosophical prose accurately and respond critically in writing

## Assessment Plan

- **Session quizzes**: after each session, 4–6 questions covering reading and concepts; low-stakes, immediate feedback
- **Midterm**: after Session 05 — a 500–700 word analytical essay comparing two Platonic concepts or dialogues from Sessions 1–5
- **Final project**: Philosophical Argument Paper — a 900–1200 word structured argument defending one position in a genuine disagreement between two figures from the course (e.g., Plato vs. Aristotle on Forms; Stoics vs. Epicureans on how to face death). Primary texts only.

## A Note on Pacing

Each session involves two things: reading a passage from a primary text (plan 45–60 minutes), then working through the lesson and exercises (another 45–60 minutes). Philosophy rewards slow reading — you are not trying to finish the text, you are trying to understand a single argument. If a passage isn't clicking, re-read it once more before asking the Tutor; often the second read opens it up. The Tutor is available to work through any passage or argument with you at any time. When the midterm essay feels hard, that's normal — you're being asked to say something, not just report what you read.
