# Final Project — PHIL-100: Philosophical Argument Paper
**Covers:** Sessions 01–10

## Overview

You have now read primary texts from ten philosophical thinkers spanning eight centuries of ancient thought. Every one of them takes strong positions — positions that conflict with each other in interesting, productive ways. This final project asks you to enter that conversation directly.

You will write a structured philosophical argument defending one position in a genuine disagreement between two figures from the course. You are not writing a summary of both sides. You are taking a side and defending it — using only the texts you have read.

## The Task

Write a 900–1,200 word argumentative essay responding to **one** of the following prompts. You may also propose your own topic to the Tutor, provided it involves a genuine disagreement between two figures from the course and is confined to primary texts.

---

**Prompt A: Plato vs. Aristotle — Do the Forms Exist?**

Plato argues that genuine knowledge requires eternal, abstract Forms that exist independently of the physical world. Aristotle rejects this entirely, arguing that Forms are an unnecessary and explanatorily empty addition to ontology. Defend one position against the other. Your argument must engage with the strongest version of the opposing view — not a caricature — and explain why your chosen position handles the objection better.

*Key texts*: Republic Books VI–VII (Plato); Metaphysics Book I, Nicomachean Ethics Book I (Aristotle)

---

**Prompt B: Stoics vs. Epicureans — How Should We Face Death?**

The Stoics hold that death should be accepted as a natural part of the cosmic order, and that virtue — not the cessation of consciousness — is what ultimately matters. Epicurus argues that death is simply nothing to us: non-existence cannot harm us, and the fear of death is a rational mistake correctable by argument. Defend one school's approach as more philosophically coherent than the other.

*Key texts*: Epictetus, Enchiridion; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (Stoics); Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Principal Doctrines (Epicureans)

---

**Prompt C: Plato vs. Aristotle — What Makes a Life Go Well?**

Plato's Republic argues that the just life — reason governing spirit and appetite, oriented toward the Form of the Good — is the happiest life, regardless of external circumstances. Aristotle argues that eudaimonia requires not only virtue but also external goods: friends, health, a measure of prosperity. Defend one position against the other.

*Key texts*: Republic Books I, IV, IX (Plato); Nicomachean Ethics Books I, II, X (Aristotle)

---

**Prompt D: Plato's Socrates vs. Epicurus — The Examined Life vs. the Tranquil Life**

Socrates argues in the *Apology* that the unexamined life is not worth living — that philosophical inquiry into virtue is the highest human calling, even at the cost of social comfort or life itself. Epicurus argues that what matters is tranquility: reducing anxiety, satisfying natural desires, cultivating friendship, and avoiding the turbulence of political and philosophical ambition. Can both be right? Or does one view make a stronger case for the best human life?

*Key texts*: Plato, Apology; Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Principal Doctrines

---

## What to Submit

A 900–1,200 word essay with:

1. **A clear thesis in the opening paragraph**: state which position you are defending and why you think it is stronger
2. **Statement of the opposing view**: present the rival position in its strongest form — a version the rival thinker would recognize as their own
3. **Your argument**: at least two distinct reasons for your chosen position, grounded in specific passages from the primary texts
4. **Response to the strongest objection**: acknowledge the best argument against your view and explain why your position survives it
5. **Conclusion**: restate the thesis in light of the argument, noting any remaining uncertainty

**Source constraint**: Primary texts only. No secondary scholarship. Cite by text name and section or passage number (e.g., "Republic 444e," "Enchiridion §5," "Letter to Menoeceus 125").

Submit your essay text directly to the Tutor.

## Evaluation Criteria

- **Thesis clarity**: Is there a specific, arguable claim being defended?
- **Steel-manning**: Does the essay present the opposing view fairly and at its strongest?
- **Textual grounding**: Are arguments anchored in specific passages, accurately cited?
- **Argument quality**: Are the reasons given actually reasons — do they support the thesis rather than just asserting it?
- **Objection handling**: Does the essay acknowledge and respond to the best counterargument?
- **Prose**: Is the writing clear, precise, and organized?

## Tutor Scoring Guide

**Strong responses** will:
- Open with a thesis sentence that says something like "I will argue that Aristotle's critique of Forms succeeds on the grounds that..."
- Present the opposing view clearly enough that the rival thinker (or their defender) would say "yes, that is my position"
- Ground each major argument in a specific quoted or closely paraphrased passage with accurate location
- Anticipate and reply to the strongest counterargument — not a strawman
- Acknowledge genuine uncertainty while still defending a position

**Common weaknesses to watch for**:
- Both-sides essays that never take a position ("Plato says X. Aristotle says Y. Both have good points.")
- Presenting a weak version of the opposing view (strawmanning)
- Arguments that simply repeat the thesis without reasoning ("Aristotle is right that eudaimonia requires external goods because external goods are part of a good life")
- Secondary literature slipping in — if a student uses language or arguments not traceable to the primary texts, ask them to identify the source
- Missing the objection-handling section entirely

**Feedback guidance**:
- For a strong essay: affirm the thesis and strongest argument; pose one further question that would push the argument deeper
- For a thesis-less essay: ask the student to write the thesis in one sentence before revising; do not accept "I discuss both views" as a thesis
- For a strawman: identify the exact passage where the opposing view is misrepresented; direct to the relevant primary text
- For missing objection-handling: ask "what would [the other philosopher] say to this?" before accepting the essay as complete
