# Midterm Assessment — PHIL-100
**Covers:** Sessions 01–05

## Overview

You have completed the first half of PHIL-100, moving from the Pre-Socratics through Plato's political philosophy. This midterm asks you to step back from individual sessions and think across the Platonic corpus — to connect ideas, trace developments, and take a position.

This is a short analytical essay. The goal is not to summarize what you've read; it is to make an argument.

## The Task

Write a 500–700 word analytical essay responding to **one** of the following prompts. Choose the prompt you find most genuinely interesting — the quality of your thinking matters more than which prompt you select.

---

**Prompt A: Knowledge and the Soul**

In the *Meno*, Plato argues that learning is recollection — the soul remembering what it knew before birth. In the *Republic* (Books VI–VII), he argues that knowledge is of the Forms, accessible only through philosophical training. Are these two accounts of knowledge compatible? Do they say the same thing about what the soul knows and how it comes to know it, or is there a tension between them?

---

**Prompt B: Justice and Forms**

In *Republic* Books IV and VI–VII, Plato argues that justice in the soul is reason governing spirit and appetite, and that genuine knowledge requires ascent to the Form of the Good. Is there a necessary connection between these two claims — does Platonic justice *require* the Theory of Forms, or could it stand without it?

---

**Prompt C: Socrates' Consistency**

In the *Apology*, Socrates describes his philosophical mission as serving Athens by asking questions. In the *Meno*, he guides an uneducated boy to geometric knowledge. In the *Republic*, Plato argues that philosophers should govern. Are these three pictures of the philosopher's role consistent with each other? If there is a tension, what is it?

---

## What to Submit

A 500–700 word essay with:
- An opening sentence that states your thesis (what you are arguing, not just what you are discussing)
- At least two specific references to the primary texts (quotes or close paraphrases with dialogue locations)
- A clear argument — not a tour of what you've read, but a claim you are defending
- A concluding sentence that restates the thesis in light of the argument you've made

Submit your essay text directly to the Tutor.

## Evaluation Criteria

- **Thesis clarity**: Is there a clear, specific claim being argued?
- **Textual grounding**: Does the argument engage directly with specific passages from the primary texts?
- **Reasoning**: Is the argument coherent? Does each move follow from the last?
- **Nuance**: Does the essay acknowledge complexity — counterarguments, tensions, qualifications — rather than oversimplifying?
- **Prose**: Is the writing clear and precise? (Length requirements are guidelines, not goals — a tight 500-word essay is better than a padded 700-word one.)

## Tutor Scoring Guide

**Strong responses** will:
- Open with a specific thesis rather than "In this essay I will discuss..."
- Quote at least one specific line from the primary texts (Apology, Meno, Republic) with accurate attribution
- Identify a genuine philosophical tension and take a position on it — not just describe both sides
- Show awareness that the answer is not obvious — acknowledge the strongest counterargument

**Common weaknesses to watch for**:
- Summary without argument ("In the Meno, Socrates asks Meno about virtue..." for 500 words without a thesis)
- Vague claims ("Plato believed that knowledge is important") not supported by specific text
- Conflating the dialogues (treating the Republic's elaborate metaphysics as if it were already present in the Meno)
- Missing the connection to primary text — if a student cannot cite a specific passage, ask them to revisit the text

**Feedback guidance**:
- For a strong essay: affirm the thesis, note the strongest point, suggest one extension or question to sharpen
- For a weak essay: name the missing element (thesis? textual evidence? argument?) specifically, then direct to the relevant session and passage — do not rewrite for the student
- For a disorganized essay: ask the student to state their thesis in one sentence before revising
