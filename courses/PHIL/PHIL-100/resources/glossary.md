# Glossary — PHIL-100: Classical Philosophy — Foundations

All terms defined as they appear in the course. Terms are listed alphabetically. Page or passage references use standard Stephanus notation (Plato) or Bekker numbers (Aristotle).

---

**Akrasia** — Weakness of will; acting against one's own better judgment. A person who knows they should not eat the cake but eats it anyway exhibits akrasia. Aristotle uses this phenomenon to argue against Plato's intellectualist view that virtue is knowledge (if it were, akrasia would be impossible). (NE, Book VII)

**Anamnesis** — Recollection; the Platonic theory that learning is the recovery of knowledge the soul had before birth. Plato uses the slave-boy demonstration in the *Meno* to illustrate this: the boy arrives at correct geometric answers through guided questioning, suggesting innate knowledge. Also used in the *Phaedo* as an argument for the soul's pre-natal existence. (Meno 80d–86c; Phaedo 72e–78b)

**Apeiron** — "The Indefinite" or "the Boundless"; Anaximander's proposed archē. Unlike Thales' water, the apeiron is not any specific substance — it is a neutral, inexhaustible source from which opposites emerge. (Pre-Socratic fragments, DK12)

**Apologia** — Defense speech; the genre of Plato's *Apology*. The Greek word does not mean apology in the modern sense; it means a speech made in one's own defense before a court. (Apology, title)

**Aponia** — Freedom from physical pain; one of the two components of the highest Epicurean pleasure, alongside ataraxia. (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus)

**Aporia** — Impasse; productive puzzlement. The typical outcome of the Socratic elenchus — the interlocutor realizes they cannot define the virtue they claimed to know. Socrates treats aporia as progress: knowing you don't know is better than false certainty. (Meno 84a–c; Apology throughout)

**Archē** — First principle; the fundamental substance or cause that underlies all reality. The central question of Pre-Socratic philosophy: what is the archē? Thales: water. Anaximander: the apeiron. Heraclitus: fire (as symbol of logos-governed change). (Pre-Socratic fragments)

**Aretē** — Virtue or excellence; human goodness in the fullest sense. The central subject of Socratic ethics and Aristotelian ethics alike. In Plato, aretē is connected to knowledge; in Aristotle, to the development of hexis through habituation. (Meno 70a; NE throughout)

**Ataraxia** — Tranquility; freedom from mental anxiety. The highest Epicurean pleasure — a stable, quiet state of mind, not an intense sensory experience. Also used by the Stoics to describe the condition of the philosopher who has correctly separated what is up to them from what is not. (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus; Epictetus, Enchiridion)

**Cyclical Argument** — The first argument for soul immortality in the *Phaedo*: opposites generate from opposites (waking from sleeping, large from small); therefore life and death generate each other, and the soul persists through death to be reborn. (Phaedo 70c–72e)

**Dianoia** — "Thinking" or "reasoning"; the third level of cognition on Plato's Divided Line. Mathematical reasoning belongs here — it uses diagrams and makes hypotheses, but reasons toward Forms without questioning its starting assumptions. (Republic VI, 511d)

**Dichotomy of Control** — Epictetus's foundational distinction between what is "up to us" (our judgments, desires, and moral character) and what is "not up to us" (our bodies, reputation, wealth, other people's actions). The entire Stoic practical philosophy flows from getting this distinction right. (Enchiridion §1)

**Divided Line** — Plato's analogy dividing reality into four levels: images/shadows (eikasia), physical objects (pistis), mathematical objects (dianoia), and Forms (nous). Moving up the line represents increasing clarity, reality, and cognitive access. (Republic VI, 509d–511e)

**Doctrine of the Mean** — Aristotle's claim that every virtue is an intermediate state between two vices (excess and deficiency). The mean is not a fixed average but what is appropriate for the specific person in the specific situation, determined by phronesis. (NE, Book II, chapters 6–9)

**Doxa** — Opinion or belief; the cognitive state associated with the sensory world in Plato's system. Contrasted with epistēmē (genuine knowledge). The objects of doxa are physical things, which are changeable; the objects of epistēmē are Forms, which are eternal. (Republic V–VI; Parmenides used it earlier for the deceptive world of appearances)

**Eidos / Idea** — Form; Plato's term for the eternal, abstract, perfect entities that are the proper objects of knowledge. (Note: not "idea" in the casual modern sense — a Form is not a thought in someone's mind but an independently existing abstract entity.) (Republic VI–VII; Phaedo throughout)

**Eikasia** — Image-thinking; imagination; the lowest level of cognition on the Divided Line. The mental state associated with looking at shadows, reflections, and images rather than the things themselves. (Republic VI, 511d)

**Elenchus** — Refutation; Socrates' method of cross-examination. He asks someone to define a concept, tests the definition with hypothetical cases, exposes contradictions, and drives the interlocutor to aporia. (Apology; Meno; early Platonic dialogues generally)

**Epistēmē** — Genuine knowledge; stable understanding that grasps not just that something is true but why it is true. For Plato, epistēmē is only possible about eternal, unchanging things (Forms). Contrasted with ortho doxa (true belief). (Meno 97e–98a; Republic V–VII)

**Eudaimonia** — Happiness, flourishing, or living-well; the highest human good for Aristotle. Not a feeling or a passive state but an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, sustained over a complete life. The term combines eu (well) and daimon (spirit/divinity). (NE, Book I, chapters 7–12)

**Examined Life** — A life in which one's values, commitments, and beliefs are subjected to rational scrutiny. Socrates' central ethical ideal, stated in the *Apology*: "the life which is unexamined is not worth living." (Apology 38a)

**Final Argument (Phaedo)** — The fourth argument for soul immortality: the soul has life essentially, as fire has heat essentially; since it cannot admit death, it is indestructible. (Phaedo 102b–107b)

**Form of the Good** — The highest of Plato's Forms; the source of being and intelligibility for all other Forms. Analogous to the sun in the visible realm — it provides the "light" by which all Forms are knowable. (Republic VI, 507b–509d)

**Four Causes** — Aristotle's framework for explaining things: (1) material cause (what it's made of); (2) formal cause (its structure or essence); (3) efficient cause (what produced it); (4) final cause (what it's for — its telos). (Metaphysics I–II; Physics II)

**Function Argument** — Aristotle's argument that the good for humans is excellent performance of our distinctive function (rational activity), derived by analogy with tools and craftsmen. (NE I.7, 1097b–1098a)

**Hexis** — Stable disposition or state of character; the category under which Aristotle places virtue. A hexis is built through habituation — repeated right action — and results in a tendency to feel and act in the appropriate way. (NE II.5–6)

**Indifferents (Adiaphora)** — The Stoic category for everything outside virtue: health, wealth, reputation, and so on. These are neither genuinely good nor genuinely bad; they can be "preferred" or "dispreferred" but do not determine one's fundamental wellbeing. (Epictetus, Enchiridion §1–2; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations)

**Kosmos** — The ordered natural world; the universe conceived as intelligible and self-regulating. Pre-Socratic thinkers assumed the world had this structure; their inquiry was into what principle governed it. (Pre-Socratic fragments)

**Logos** — Rational principle; the governing law of reality for Heraclitus. More broadly: reasoned speech, argument, or account. Later adopted by Aristotle (rationality as the defining function of humans) and Stoics (the rational principle pervading the cosmos). (Heraclitus, DK22B1; Aristotle, NE I.7; Stoics generally)

**Meno's Paradox** — The puzzle that inquiry is impossible: you either already know what you're looking for (no need to look) or you don't (you couldn't recognize it). Plato's response: the theory of recollection. (Meno 80d)

**Nous** — Pure intellect; the highest cognitive faculty in Plato's Divided Line, by which Forms are directly grasped. Also used by Aristotle for the capacity for first principles. (Republic VI, 511d; Aristotle, NE X.7)

**Ortho Doxa** — Correct or true belief; a state that can guide action successfully but lacks the stability of genuine knowledge. Unlike epistēmē, true belief can be "talked away" — it is not grounded in understanding why the belief is true. (Meno 97b–98b)

**Phronesis** — Practical wisdom; the intellectual virtue that enables a person to perceive what virtue requires in particular circumstances. Cannot be reduced to rules; requires experience and emotional maturity. The master virtue that coordinates all others. (NE VI.5–7)

**Pistis** — Belief; the cognitive state associated with physical objects on Plato's Divided Line. Higher than image-thinking (eikasia) but lower than mathematical reasoning (dianoia) and pure intellect (nous). (Republic VI, 511d)

**Prohairesis** — Moral purpose or will; the faculty of judging impressions and choosing responses. For Epictetus, prohairesis is the core of what is "up to us" — it cannot be taken away by any external force. (Epictetus, Enchiridion §1–2; Discourses)

**Recollection** — See *Anamnesis*.

**Socratic Ignorance** — Knowing that one does not know; the second-order awareness that Socrates claims as his only wisdom. The Oracle said no one was wiser than Socrates; he interprets this to mean that the gods alone are truly wise, and the best a human can do is know the limits of their knowledge. (Apology 21d)

**Soul (Platonic)** — In Plato, an immortal, non-physical entity that animates the body and exists before birth and after death. It has three parts in the Republic (reason, spirit, appetite); in the Phaedo it is simple and akin to the eternal Forms. (Republic IV; Phaedo 78b–84b)

**Soul (Aristotelian)** — For Aristotle, the soul is the form of the body — the organizing principle that makes a living body alive, not a separate entity that can exist without a body. This is why Aristotle's ethics requires no afterlife and focuses on this-worldly flourishing. (De Anima II.1)

**Symmetry Argument** — Epicurus's argument that pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence are structurally identical; since we are not troubled by the time before our birth, we have no reason to be troubled by the time after our death. (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus)

**Tetrapharmakos** — The Epicurean "four-fold cure": don't fear the gods; don't fear death; what is good is easy to get; what is terrible is easy to endure. A mnemonic for the philosophical treatment of the four main sources of human anxiety. (Epicurus, Principal Doctrines)

**Theōria** — Contemplation; sustained philosophical or intellectual activity aimed at understanding universal truths. Aristotle argues in NE Book X that this is the highest form of eudaimonia — most continuous, most self-sufficient, most divine. (NE X.7–8)

**Third Man Argument** — Aristotle's objection to Plato's Theory of Forms: if a Form of Man explains what all individual men have in common, then a third entity is needed to explain what individual men and the Form have in common — generating an infinite regress. (Aristotle, Metaphysics I.9)

**Tripartite Soul** — Plato's division of the soul into three parts: reason (logistikon), spirit (thymoeides), and appetite (epithymetikon). Justice in the soul is reason governing spirit and appetite; injustice is the disorder or reversal of this hierarchy. (Republic IV, 436a–441c)

**Way of Being / Way of Opinion** — Parmenides' two paths of inquiry: the Way of Being (following reason to unchanging, eternal existence) and the Way of Opinion (the deceptive world as perceived by the senses). Only the former leads to genuine knowledge. (Parmenides, DK28B2–3)
