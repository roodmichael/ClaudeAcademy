## Core Concepts

### Aristotle's Intellectual Context
Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was Plato's most brilliant student and, in many ways, his most penetrating critic. He studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years, then founded his own school, the Lyceum. His approach is empirical where Plato's is rationalist: Aristotle trusts observation and practical experience; Plato trusts pure reason.

The central target in Aristotle's ethics and metaphysics is Plato's Theory of Forms. Aristotle rejects it entirely and builds an alternative framework grounded in what things actually do.

### Metaphysics Book I: The Critique of Forms
Aristotle opens his *Metaphysics* with "All men by nature desire to know" — an empirical orientation from the first line. He surveys previous philosophers as a scientist surveys prior data.

**Critique of Plato's Forms**:
Aristotle's objections include:
- **Forms don't explain anything**: Plato says physical things "participate in" Forms, but this is just a metaphor, not an explanation of causation
- **The Third Man Argument**: if a Form explains why particular men are men by being a perfect Man, then there must be a third entity explaining what the Form and particular men have in common — an infinite regress
- **Forms are uneconomical**: we don't need two worlds (physical + intelligible) to explain one
- **Forms are not causes of motion**: Platonic Forms are static; they cannot explain why things move, change, or come into being

Aristotle replaces Forms with his own framework of **substance** (*ousia*) — actual individual things — and four **causes**: material, formal, efficient, and final.

### Nicomachean Ethics Book I: Eudaimonia
The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens by identifying that "every art and every inquiry... seems to aim at some good." This leads to the question: is there a highest good at which all human activity aims?

**Eudaimonia** (happiness, flourishing, living-well):
- Greek: *eu* (well) + *daimon* (spirit/divinity)
- Not a feeling or a lucky state, but an *activity* of the soul in accordance with virtue
- It is the highest human good — pursued for its own sake, not as a means to something else
- Common candidates (pleasure, honor, wealth) are rejected: pleasure is shared with animals; honor depends on others; wealth is merely instrumental

**The Function Argument**: Aristotle argues that the good for a human being is excellence in performing our distinctive function. Just as the good knife is the one that cuts well, the good human is the one who performs the function distinctive to humans — rational activity — excellently. This defines eudaimonia: *"activity of the soul in accordance with its best and most complete virtue"* (1098a).

**Happiness as an Activity, Not a State**: Unlike modern English "happiness" (a feeling), eudaimonia is something you *do*, sustained over a complete life. A rich banker who is bored and passive is not eudaimon; a philosopher who lives simply but engages deeply with ideas may be.

### Nicomachean Ethics Book II: Virtue as Habit
**Virtue** (*aretē*) in Aristotle is a **hexis** — a stable disposition or state of character. Key points:

- We acquire virtues by practice: "we become just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts"
- The virtues are **not instilled by nature**, nor are they against nature — nature gives us the *capacity*, and habituation actualizes it
- Virtue involves **feeling** as well as action: the truly courageous person not only acts bravely but feels the right amount of fear

**The Doctrine of the Mean**:
Every virtue is an intermediate state between two vices:
- Courage: between cowardice (excess fear) and rashness (deficient fear)
- Generosity: between miserliness and prodigality
- Truthfulness: between self-deprecation and boastfulness

The "mean" is not a mechanical average but what is *right in the circumstances*, determined by practical wisdom.

**Phronesis (Practical Wisdom)**: The intellectual virtue that enables a person to perceive what virtue requires in any particular situation. It cannot be reduced to rules. A person of practical wisdom reads situations correctly and responds accordingly — not through calculation but through developed moral perception. Phronesis is the "master virtue" that coordinates all the others.

### Nicomachean Ethics Book X: Contemplation
Aristotle ends the Ethics by arguing that the highest form of happiness is **theōria** (contemplation) — the sustained exercise of reason on the highest objects, for its own sake. This life most resembles the divine; it is most self-sufficient and most continuous.

This creates a tension in Aristotle's ethics: throughout Books II–IX he emphasizes civic virtue and practical life; Book X seems to crown the life of pure intellectual contemplation as highest. Scholars debate whether these are compatible.

## Key Definitions

- **Eudaimonia**: happiness or flourishing — not a feeling but an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue
- **Aretē**: virtue or excellence — a stable disposition to feel and act in the right way
- **Hexis**: disposition or habitual state; the category under which virtue falls
- **Doctrine of the Mean**: virtue as the intermediate between excess and deficiency in feeling and action
- **Phronesis**: practical wisdom — the ability to perceive what virtue requires in particular situations
- **Theōria**: contemplation — the highest intellectual activity, aimed at truth for its own sake
- **Ousia**: substance — actual individual things, Aristotle's replacement for Platonic Forms
- **Function Argument**: the claim that human good is excellent performance of our distinctive function (rational activity)
- **Four Causes**: material (what it's made of), formal (its structure), efficient (what produced it), final (what it's for)

## Process / Mechanics

**How virtues are built**: Aristotle is specific about habituation. Repeated right action builds the hexis; the hexis then makes right action natural and pleasant. A person in the process of developing courage finds it difficult and somewhat uncomfortable; a fully virtuous person finds it natural. The goal is a character that makes virtue easy, not effortful.

**Moral education in stages**:
1. Childhood: emotional habituation (learning to feel appropriately through reward/punishment and good models)
2. Adolescence/young adulthood: developing reason alongside emotion
3. Maturity: phronesis coordinates feeling and reason; virtue becomes fully integrated

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Thinking Aristotle's "mean" means being moderate in everything — temperance is a mean, but Aristotle does not say courage means being a little bit brave; it means being appropriately brave
- Confusing eudaimonia with pleasure — Aristotle includes pleasure in the flourishing life but says pleasure alone is the life of "grazing animals"
- Thinking Aristotle agrees with Plato on the goal (virtue and knowledge) but just disagrees on method — the disagreement goes deeper, to what knowledge even is and how ethics relates to metaphysics
- Assuming the virtuous person is always certain about what to do — phronesis is precisely the ability to navigate genuine uncertainty

## Hands-On Elements

- Identify a virtue you have been developing and trace the stages: what habits built it? What does it feel like to exercise it now vs. when you started?
- Apply the Doctrine of the Mean to a current moral challenge in your life: what are the two relevant vices? What would the mean look like?
- Write a brief "function argument" for a role you play (student, friend, colleague) — what is the function, and what would excellent performance look like?

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Is the life of theōria (Book X) compatible with the life of civic virtue (Books II–IX), or does Aristotle ultimately endorse an elitist contemplative ideal?
- Does the Function Argument actually succeed? Can we derive what the good for humans is from what humans distinctively do?
- Aristotle says women and slaves are not fully capable of eudaimonia — how does this affect the philosophical value of his ethics?
