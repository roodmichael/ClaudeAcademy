## Core Concepts

### The Hellenistic Turn
After Aristotle, philosophy shifts in emphasis. The great systematic projects of Plato and Aristotle gave way to more urgent practical questions: how should I live? How do I achieve tranquility? How do I face death without fear? The Stoics and Epicureans both emerged in the 3rd century BCE as philosophical schools offering a *way of life*, not just a theory.

Both schools flourished in the Roman world. **Epictetus** (c. 50–135 CE) was a Stoic philosopher who was born a slave; his *Enchiridion* (Handbook) and *Discourses* are the most direct practical guides to Stoic living. **Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE) was Roman emperor and a devoted Stoic; his *Meditations* were private notes written to himself. **Epicurus** (341–270 BCE) founded his school in a garden in Athens; his *Letter to Menoeceus* and *Principal Doctrines* are the primary surviving texts.

### Stoicism: The Dichotomy of Control

**Core Principle**: Some things are up to us; some things are not. Everything depends on getting this distinction right.

**What is up to us** (*eph' hēmin*): our opinions, intentions, desires, aversions — in short, our judgments and moral character (*prohairesis*, or "moral purpose")

**What is not up to us**: our body, health, reputation, property, the actions of others, external events

The Stoic conclusion: *"What disturbs men's minds is not events but their judgements on events."* — Epictetus. Nothing external can harm us unless we judge it to be bad. Freedom is not freedom from external circumstances but freedom from false judgments about them.

**Virtue as the Only Good**: Stoics hold that virtue is the *only* genuine good; everything else (health, wealth, pleasure, reputation) is an *indifferent* — it may be preferred or dispreferred, but it cannot affect one's fundamental happiness. The Stoic sage possesses virtue; everything else is irrelevant to their flourishing.

**The Three Disciplines of Epictetus**:
1. **Discipline of Desire**: desire only virtue; treat everything external as indifferent
2. **Discipline of Action**: fulfill your social roles (parent, citizen, friend) without attachment to outcomes
3. **Discipline of Assent**: rigorously test impressions before accepting them as true — do not give assent to false beliefs about what is good or bad

**Key Passages from the Enchiridion**:
Opening: *"Some things are up to us and some things are not up to us. Our opinions are up to us, and our impulses, desires, aversions — in short, whatever is our own doing."*

On loss: *"Never say about anything, 'I have lost it'; but, 'I have returned it.' Is your child dead? It has been returned. Is your wife dead? She has been returned."*

On roles: *"Remember that you are an actor in a drama, the character of which is determined by the author: if short, a short one; if long, a long one... For this is your duty, to act well the part that is given to you."*

**Marcus Aurelius in the Meditations**:
Marcus returns obsessively to a few central themes: the brevity of life, the indifference of external honors, the rational order of the cosmos, and the duty to act virtuously regardless of outcome. Written to himself, never for publication, the *Meditations* show Stoicism as a daily practice of reminding oneself of what matters.

On death: *"Loss is nothing else but change, and change is Nature's delight."*

On anger: *"Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial."* (Then he explains how to respond calmly to each.)

### Epicureanism: Pleasure, Friendship, and the Fear of Death

**Core Principle**: Pleasure is the highest good — but Epicurus means something specific and counterintuitive by "pleasure."

**Ataraxia and Aponia**: The highest pleasure is not sensory indulgence but **ataraxia** (tranquility of the soul, freedom from anxiety) and **aponia** (freedom from bodily pain). These are *negative* states — the absence of distress, not the presence of excitement.

**Natural and Necessary Desires**: Epicurus categorizes desires:
- **Natural and necessary**: food, shelter, warmth — simple to satisfy, essential to avoid pain
- **Natural but unnecessary**: rich food, fine drink — pleasant but not required
- **Empty desires**: fame, power, unlimited wealth — these can never be satisfied and produce more anxiety than pleasure

The Epicurean prescription: satisfy natural and necessary desires; cultivate simple pleasures; avoid empty desires entirely.

**The Tetrapharmakos (Four-Fold Cure)**:
1. Don't fear god
2. Don't fear death
3. What is good is easy to get
4. What is terrible is easy to endure

**Death is Nothing to Us** (the key argument):
Epicurus argues that death should not be feared because death is the end of all sensation. Since we can only experience good and evil, and death is the absence of experience, death cannot harm us. *"When we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not."* This is the **symmetry argument**: just as we are not troubled by the infinite time before our birth, we should not be troubled by the infinite time after our death.

**Friendship**: Epicurus believed friendship was central to the good life — one of the greatest and most reliable sources of pleasure. His Garden community was built on close philosophical friendship. *"Of all the things which wisdom provides to make us entirely happy, much the greatest is the possession of friendship."*

**Letter to Menoeceus, key passages**:
On the examined life: *"Let no one be slow to seek wisdom when he is young nor weary in the search thereof when he has grown old."*

On fortune: *"The wise man does not deprecate good fortune... but neither does he think it great if it come nor evil if it do not come."*

On pleasure: *"When, therefore, we maintain that pleasure is the end, we do not mean the pleasures of profligates... but we mean the freedom of the body from pain and of the soul from confusion."*

### Plato's Phaedo: The Soul and What Comes After

**Dramatic Setting**: The Phaedo takes place on the day of Socrates' death. His friends have gathered; they discuss the soul's immortality as Socrates prepares to drink hemlock. The conversation is Plato's most explicit treatment of the soul, the Forms, and the philosophical life.

**Four Arguments for Immortality**:

1. **The Cyclical Argument** (70c–72e): All opposites generate from their opposites — the living come from the dead and the dead from the living. Therefore the soul must persist through death and return.

2. **The Argument from Recollection** (72e–78b): Since we recognize perfect equality (the Form of Equality) when we see imperfectly equal things, we must have known this Form before birth. Therefore our souls existed before birth, and learning is remembering. (This revisits the Meno's anamnesis.)

3. **The Affinity Argument** (78b–84b): The soul resembles the eternal, invisible, unchanging Forms; the body resembles the visible, changing, composite world. At death, the soul naturally gravitates toward the divine, intelligible realm where it belongs.

4. **The Final Argument** (102b–107b): The soul brings life with it essentially — as fire essentially brings heat. Since the soul cannot admit the opposite of what it essentially brings (death), it is imperishable.

**The Philosopher's Life and Death**:
Socrates argues that the philosopher practices death throughout life: philosophy is the gradual purification of the soul from bodily desires and distractions, orienting it toward Forms and truth. *"The one aim of those who practice philosophy in the proper manner is to practice for dying and death."* (64a)

Death, for the philosopher, is not tragic but the culmination of a life properly lived.

**The Myth at the End**: Plato ends the Phaedo with a vivid mythological account of what happens to souls after death — the virtuous ascend to higher regions; the impure are punished. Scholars debate whether Plato presents this myth as literally true or as a useful allegory.

## Key Definitions

- **Prohairesis**: moral purpose or will — the faculty by which we judge and choose; in Epictetus, the core of what is "up to us"
- **Ataraxia**: tranquility; freedom from anxiety — the highest Epicurean pleasure
- **Aponia**: freedom from bodily pain
- **Indifferents (adiaphora)**: Stoic category for everything outside virtue — health, wealth, reputation; can be preferred but are not genuinely good
- **Tetrapharmakos**: the Epicurean four-fold cure for fear
- **Symmetry argument**: Epicurus's argument that since pre-natal non-existence doesn't trouble us, post-mortem non-existence shouldn't either
- **Affinity Argument (Phaedo)**: the soul's kinship with the eternal Forms argues for its immortality
- **Anamnesis (in Phaedo)**: recollection of prenatal knowledge of Forms — argument 2 for immortality
- **Enchiridion**: "handbook" — Epictetus's short practical guide, compiled by his student Arrian
- **Meditations**: Marcus Aurelius's private philosophical notebooks

## Process / Mechanics

**Stoic practice** is a daily discipline, not just a belief system. Epictetus recommends:
- Morning: recall what is and is not up to you; prepare for the challenges of the day
- During the day: test impressions; don't assent to false judgments
- Evening: review actions and ask whether you acted in accordance with virtue

**Epicurean community**: Epicurus ran a school organized as a philosophical community ("The Garden") where shared simple pleasures and philosophical conversation were the mode of life. Students memorized his key doctrines.

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Thinking Stoics are emotionless — Stoicism does not eliminate emotion; it aims at appropriate emotion. The sage feels pleasure and pain but is not *governed* by them.
- Confusing Epicureanism with hedonism — Epicurus explicitly rejects the view that pleasure means sensory indulgence
- Assuming Stoics and Epicureans agree on everything just because both prize tranquility — their foundations are very different (virtue-only vs. pleasure as the good)
- Missing the Phaedo's integration of ethics and metaphysics: the arguments for immortality depend on the Theory of Forms being true

## Hands-On Elements

- Apply the dichotomy of control to a current worry: what part of it is genuinely up to you? What part is not? How does separating them feel?
- Try an Epicurean experiment: for one day, satisfy only "natural and necessary" desires — simple food, basic rest. What do you notice?
- Read the Enchiridion's opening paragraphs and write one concrete application to your own life

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Is Stoic "indifference" to external goods compatible with genuine love for family and friends, or does it make relationships hollow?
- Does the Epicurean symmetry argument actually work? Death is different from pre-natal non-existence because we anticipate it — does this matter?
- Do any of the Phaedo's four arguments succeed? Most philosophers think they don't, but they remain powerful starting points for thinking about the soul
- Is Plato's Phaedo a triumphant affirmation of immortality or a dramatic portrayal of Socrates choosing to believe something comforting in the face of death?
