## Core Concepts

### The Historical and Dramatic Context
Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE) wrote nothing. Our knowledge of him comes almost entirely from Plato's dialogues and a few other ancient sources (Xenophon, Aristophanes). The Apology and Meno are considered early dialogues, likely representing Socrates' actual views more closely than the later works where Plato's own philosophy predominates.

In 399 BCE, Socrates was tried by an Athenian jury on charges of (1) impiety — not acknowledging the gods the city acknowledges — and (2) corrupting the youth. He was found guilty by a narrow margin and sentenced to death by hemlock.

### The Apology: Socratic Wisdom
The **Apology** (Greek: *apologia*, a defense speech, not an apology in the modern sense) is Plato's account of Socrates' speech at trial.

**The Oracle's Pronouncement**: The Delphic oracle declared that no one was wiser than Socrates. Socrates, believing himself unwise, investigated this claim by questioning those with reputations for wisdom — politicians, poets, craftsmen. He found that they claimed knowledge they did not possess, while he at least knew that he did not know. Conclusion: *"I am better off than he is — for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know."* This has been called **Socratic ignorance** or the wisdom of knowing one's own ignorance.

**The Elenchus**: Socrates' method of philosophical questioning. He would ask someone to define a virtue ("What is piety?" "What is courage?"), then systematically expose contradictions in their answer until they admitted they did not know. This cross-examination is the **elenchus** (from Greek: "refutation" or "testing").

**The Divine Mission**: Socrates portrays himself as "a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God" — an irritant whose function is to sting Athens into moral self-examination. He views philosophical inquiry as a divine obligation that he cannot abandon, even under threat of death.

**The Examined Life**: The most famous line: *"the greatest good of man is daily to converse about virtue... and that the life which is unexamined is not worth living."* Socrates means that a human life not subjected to rational scrutiny — not asking what we value and why — is not fully human.

**Attitude Toward Death**: When convicted, Socrates refuses to propose exile as a counter-penalty; he would rather die than stop philosophizing. On death itself, he reasons: it is either peaceful dreamless sleep (nothing bad about that) or continued existence in which he could converse with great minds of the past (even better). Either way, a person who has lived justly has nothing to fear.

Final statement: *"The hour of departure has arrived, and we go our ways — I to die, and you to live. Which is better God only knows."*

### The Meno: Can Virtue Be Taught?
The **Meno** opens with a direct question: can virtue (*aretē*) be taught? The question quickly deepens into: what *is* virtue? And even more fundamentally: can we inquire into anything at all?

**Meno's Paradox** (also called the **Paradox of Inquiry**): Meno poses a logical trap for Socratic inquiry:
> "How will you look for it... what sort of thing will you propose to look for? Or even if you meet right up against it, how will you know that this is the thing you didn't know?" (80d)

The dilemma: you can't seek what you already know (no need), and you can't seek what you don't know (you couldn't recognize it). Therefore inquiry seems impossible.

**The Theory of Recollection (Anamnesis)**: Socrates' response: learning is actually *remembering*. The soul existed before birth and acquired knowledge; what we call "learning" is recovering what the soul already knows. He demonstrates this by guiding an uneducated slave boy to recognize a geometrical truth through questioning alone — the boy arrives at correct answers he was never taught, which Socrates takes as evidence of innate knowledge.

This is philosophically rich: it doesn't just dodge Meno's paradox, it repositions the nature of knowledge entirely. Knowledge is not new information received from outside, but something latent in the soul awaiting recovery.

**Virtue as Knowledge (and the Problem)**: The dialogue works toward the view that virtue must be a kind of knowledge — because if it were a mere knack or habit, it would be teachable in a routine way, but we can observe that virtuous people do not reliably produce virtuous children. Yet if it is knowledge, it should be teachable. The dialogue ends inconclusively: virtue may be a divine gift, a kind of **true belief** that guides action correctly without constituting genuine knowledge.

**Knowledge vs. True Belief**: One of the Meno's lasting contributions: the distinction between *epistēmē* (genuine knowledge, stable and explanatory) and *ortho doxa* (correct belief, which may guide action effectively but can be "lost" — it lacks the "tether" of understanding *why* it is true).

## Key Definitions

- **Apologia**: defense speech (the genre of the Apology)
- **Elenchus**: Socratic method of cross-examination; testing claims by exposing contradictions
- **Aretē**: virtue, excellence, or human goodness — the central subject of Socratic ethics
- **Anamnesis**: recollection; the theory that learning is remembering pre-natal knowledge
- **Epistēmē**: genuine knowledge — stable, explanatory, cannot be talked away
- **Ortho doxa**: correct/true belief — may guide action rightly but lacks epistemic security
- **Examined life**: a life subjected to rational self-scrutiny about what we value and why
- **Socratic ignorance**: the wisdom of knowing that one does not know

## Process / Mechanics

The **Socratic Method** has a characteristic structure:
1. Someone claims to know what virtue (or piety, or courage) is
2. Socrates asks for a definition
3. He tests the definition through hypothetical cases
4. He exposes a contradiction between the definition and something the interlocutor agrees is true
5. The interlocutor must revise or abandon their claim
6. Aporia (puzzlement, impasse) is reached — the interlocutor realizes they don't know what they thought they knew
7. Socrates presents this as progress: knowing you don't know is better than false confidence

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- Confusing Socratic ignorance with stupidity — Socrates is intellectually sophisticated, not genuinely unknowing
- Taking Meno's paradox as obviously wrong — it is a genuine logical puzzle
- Assuming the slave-boy demonstration proves recollection — Socrates presents it as evidence, not proof; Meno agrees it seems to show something important
- Reading the Apology as humble self-deprecation — Socrates is also argumentative, provocative, and deeply committed to his own position

## Hands-On Elements

- Apply the elenchus to a common belief ("honesty is always good") — try to find a case that complicates the definition
- Consider: is there something you "know" but cannot explain? Does that support or undermine the knowledge/true-belief distinction?
- Identify a moment in your own life when you discovered you did not know something you thought you knew — how did it feel? Is that experience philosophically valuable?

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- Is Plato's Socrates faithful to the historical Socrates, or is he already expressing Plato's own views?
- Does the slave-boy demonstration actually prove recollection, or just that geometry can be taught by guided questioning?
- Is the Meno's ending (virtue as divine gift) a genuine conclusion or a sign of the dialogue's incompleteness?

## Connections to Adjacent Topics

- The knowledge/true-belief distinction in the Meno connects directly to the Theory of Forms in the Republic and Phaedo
- The Apology's picture of Socrates facing death anticipates the Phaedo's philosophical account of why death is not feared
- The elenchus as a method connects to modern epistemology's concerns with justified belief
