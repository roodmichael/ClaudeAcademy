# Session 06: Aristotle's Revolt
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain Aristotle's main objections to Plato's Theory of Forms, state what eudaimonia means and how Aristotle defines it, and describe what the Function Argument claims.

---

## Opening

Aristotle studied at Plato's Academy for twenty years. He was, by all accounts, Plato's most brilliant student. And then he built a philosophy that dismantles his teacher's from the ground up.

There is a famous Renaissance painting — Raphael's *School of Athens* — in which Plato points upward and Aristotle points downward. Plato gestures toward the heavens, toward the realm of Forms. Aristotle gestures at the earth, at what is actually here. That gesture captures the philosophical difference.

Aristotle begins his *Metaphysics* with a line that announces his entire project: **"All men by nature desire to know."** Not "All men by nature have immortal souls who once grasped eternal Forms." Not "All men can be led to the Good through dialectic." Just: human beings are curious, and that is where philosophy starts. Here. Now. With what we actually observe.

---

## The Critique of Forms

Aristotle's objections to Plato's Theory of Forms are multiple and formidable. Three are central to the course:

**1. Forms Explain Nothing**

Plato says physical things "participate in" their corresponding Forms. A beautiful painting participates in Beauty-itself. But what does "participation" mean? Plato gives us a metaphor, not an explanation.

Aristotle's point: if you want to explain *why* a painting is beautiful, pointing to a Form doesn't help. It just renames the problem. "The painting is beautiful because it participates in Beauty" says no more than "the painting is beautiful because it is beautiful." The Form is supposed to be the explanation, but it does no explanatory work.

**2. The Third Man Argument**

If there is a Form of Man that explains what all individual men have in common, then what explains what individual men and the Form of Man have in common? There must be a second, higher Form — a "Third Man." And now we have three things that need explaining, requiring a fourth, and so on infinitely.

This is a *regress* — the theory intended to explain similarity generates an infinite series of further similarities needing explanation. Aristotle thinks this shows the theory is built on a flawed assumption.

**3. Forms Are Not Causes of Motion**

Even if Forms existed, they could not explain *why things move, grow, or change*. Forms are static and eternal. But the world is dynamic. What makes a seed become a tree? Not a Form — Forms don't act. Aristotle needs causes that are actually operative in the world.

His alternative: four causes embedded in the thing itself — the material cause (what it's made of), the formal cause (its structure or essence), the efficient cause (what produced it), and the **final cause** (what it's *for* — its purpose, telos). The seed becomes a tree because becoming a tree is the seed's *telos*: its natural end.

---

## Aristotle's Starting Point: The Good

Unlike Plato, who begins with the Form of the Good as the foundation, Aristotle begins empirically: what do people actually pursue?

*Metaphysics* Book I opens: "Every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good." But goods are ordered hierarchically — some things are pursued as means to other things. Is there a highest good, pursued for its own sake?

The common answers: pleasure, honor, wealth. Aristotle dismisses them quickly. Pleasure is shared with animals; if that were our good, we would be no different from cattle. Honor depends entirely on the opinions of others — hardly something you can ground your life in. Wealth is only ever a means; no one pursues money as an ultimate end.

What, then?

---

## Eudaimonia

Aristotle's answer: **eudaimonia** — often translated as "happiness" or "flourishing," but neither word captures it fully.

*Eu* means "well"; *daimon* means something like "spirit" or "god" — but Aristotle secularizes the term. Eudaimonia is:

- **An activity**, not a state. You don't *have* eudaimonia like you have a good temperament; you *live* it. It is something you do, sustained over a complete life.
- **Pursued for its own sake**. Every other good — health, wealth, friendship, virtue — is pursued either for eudaimonia or as a component of it.
- **Objective**, not merely subjective. A person who thinks they are flourishing but whose life is actually hollow is not eudaimon. Eudaimonia is a matter of what your life actually is, not how it feels.

A crucial difference from Plato: for Aristotle, eudaimonia is achieved in *this* world, through the activities of a complete human life — not through ascent to a realm of Forms.

---

## The Function Argument

Aristotle argues for his definition of eudaimonia through the **Function Argument**:

Everything has a characteristic function: a knife's function is to cut, a flute-player's function is to play well. What is the function of a human being?

Not mere life — plants have that. Not sentient life — animals have that. The distinctive function of humans is *rational activity*. We are the beings who reason.

Therefore: the good for humans — eudaimonia — is **excellent performance of rational activity**. More specifically: "activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete" (NE, 1098a).

This connects to Sessions 7 on virtue: eudaimonia is not the same as virtue, but it cannot be achieved without it.

---

## The Connection to Plato

Notice the shape of the disagreement:

Plato says: the good is the Form of the Good — an eternal abstract object accessible only through prolonged philosophical training. Most people never reach it.

Aristotle says: the good is eudaimonia — excellent rational activity over a complete life. It is achieved through habituation, good upbringing, practical wisdom, and the exercise of the virtues. It is available to anyone who is appropriately educated and lives well.

Plato's ethics is for philosophers. Aristotle's is for citizens.

This is not just a difference in emphasis. It is a fundamental disagreement about what reality is, what knowledge is, and what ethics is *for*.

---

## Why This Matters

Aristotle's approach has proven more durable as a practical ethics. His emphasis on character, habituation, and practical wisdom — rather than abstract knowledge of Forms — is the foundation of what philosophers call "virtue ethics," which has had a major revival in contemporary moral philosophy. His intuition that the good life is about *what you do* over time, not what abstract truths you grasp, resonates with most people's experience.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Aristotle's critique of Forms**: Forms explain nothing (participation is just a metaphor); generate a regress (Third Man Argument); cannot cause motion (Forms are static)
- **Four causes**: material, formal, efficient, and final — embedded in things themselves, not in a separate realm
- **Eudaimonia**: happiness or flourishing — an activity, not a state; objective, not merely subjective; pursued for its own sake
- **Function Argument**: the good for humans is excellent rational activity — derived from what is distinctive about human beings
- The Plato/Aristotle split: abstract Forms vs. immanent human activity; philosophical contemplation vs. civic virtue; theory vs. habituation
