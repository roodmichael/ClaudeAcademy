# Session 07: Virtue and Habit
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain Aristotle's account of virtue as a disposition (hexis), describe the Doctrine of the Mean, explain the role of phronesis (practical wisdom), and articulate the tension between the active civic life and the contemplative life in Book X.

---

## Opening

You have probably been told, at some point, that character is who you are when no one is watching.

Aristotle would refine this: character is what you have become through repeated action. It is not something you discover, like a talent you were born with. It is something you build, through what you do over time.

But here is the harder question: if we build character through our choices, and our choices depend on our existing character, where does virtue actually come from? Who starts the cycle?

This is one of the questions Aristotle's ethics of habit raises — and it is not trivially answered.

---

## Virtue Is Not Knowledge

Plato's Socrates argued, or came close to arguing, that **virtue is knowledge**: know what the Good is, and you will do good. Ignorance is the only real vice.

Aristotle disagrees sharply. He had observed that knowing the right thing and doing it are not the same. People know what they should eat, how they should treat others, what courage requires — and they fail to act accordingly. This phenomenon — **akrasia** (weakness of will, or acting against one's own better judgment) — is a real feature of human psychology that Plato's view struggles to explain.

Aristotle's account: virtue is a **hexis** — a stable disposition or state of character — not a kind of knowledge. A hexis is a tendency to feel and act in a particular way, developed through habituation. A courageous person doesn't just know courage is right; they have trained themselves to feel the right amount of fear and respond appropriately.

Virtue involves both *action* and *feeling*. A soldier who acts bravely but is overwhelmed by terror the entire time is not yet fully virtuous; they are doing the right thing while fighting against themselves. The fully virtuous person acts *and* feels appropriately — they find right action natural because they have been shaped through habit to respond rightly.

---

## Virtue Is Built Through Habituation

*"We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."* — Nicomachean Ethics, Book II

The mechanism: repeated right action builds the hexis; the hexis makes right action natural and, eventually, pleasant.

This has a profound implication: you cannot wait until you feel virtuous to act virtuously. You become virtuous *by acting* as if you already were. The process is uncomfortable at first — like any training — but it gradually becomes easier and more natural.

Nature gives us the *capacity* for virtue; habituation actualizes it. A person raised without virtue-forming experiences is not inherently bad — they just haven't been properly shaped. Good moral education is not instruction in abstract principles; it is the formation of habits, supervised exposure to right action, and the cultivation of appropriate emotional responses.

## The Doctrine of the Mean

Aristotle's most famous and most easily misunderstood contribution to ethics: **every virtue is an intermediate state (the mean) between two vices**.

- **Courage**: the mean between cowardice (too much fear) and rashness (too little fear)
- **Generosity**: the mean between miserliness (too little giving) and prodigality (too much)
- **Truthfulness**: the mean between self-deprecation (understating yourself) and boastfulness (overstating)

**The mean is not the arithmetic average.** A trainer who prescribes 10 pounds for one athlete and 100 for another does not prescribe 55 for everyone. The mean is what is right *for this person, in these circumstances* — and determining that requires judgment, not calculation.

This is where **phronesis** enters.

---

## Phronesis: Practical Wisdom

**Phronesis** (practical wisdom) is the intellectual virtue that enables a person to perceive what virtue requires in a specific situation. It cannot be reduced to a rule or a formula. It requires:

- Experience — phronesis cannot be learned from books alone; it requires living and observing
- Emotional maturity — the person of practical wisdom perceives correctly *because* their emotional responses are well-calibrated
- A developed sense of the morally relevant features of situations

Aristotle contrasts phronesis with **sophia** (theoretical wisdom): the latter is knowledge of universal, necessary truths (mathematics, metaphysics); the former is knowledge of how to act well in the contingent, variable world of human affairs.

Phronesis is the master virtue that coordinates all the others. Without it, courage becomes rashness; generosity becomes recklessness. The person of practical wisdom sees what each situation demands and responds with the right virtue in the right degree.

## Book X: The Contemplative Life

Book X of the Nicomachean Ethics ends with an argument that might surprise you after nine books of civic virtue. Aristotle argues that the *highest* form of eudaimonia is **theōria** — philosophical contemplation.

His reasoning: if eudaimonia is excellent rational activity, then the *most* rational activity — sustained thinking about the highest and most universal truths — should be the highest form of eudaimonia. Contemplation is self-sufficient (you don't need equipment or other people), continuous (you can do it for longer than anything else), and most divine (the gods, who need nothing, only contemplate).

**The tension with the rest of the Ethics**: Books II–IX emphasize civic virtue — courage, justice, friendship, the engagement of a good citizen. Book X seems to crown the philosopher who withdraws from civic life and simply thinks.

Scholars have debated this for centuries. Two main readings:
1. **Inclusive**: the contemplative life and the civic life are both genuinely good; theōria is the peak but civic virtue is necessary too
2. **Exclusive**: Aristotle ultimately endorses the philosopher's life and civic virtue is a second-best arrangement for those who can't achieve theōria

This tension is not a failure of Aristotle's thinking. It reflects a genuine question: if you have the capacity, is withdrawal from worldly affairs for the sake of truth a betrayal of community, or its highest expression?

---

## Why This Matters

The idea that character is built through habit — not born, not discovered, not achieved through rare philosophical enlightenment — is Aristotle's most influential contribution to ethics. It says: you are, in significant part, what you have been doing. And therefore, what you do *now* shapes who you are becoming.

This is both more demanding and more hopeful than Plato's view. More demanding because there is no shortcut through intellectual insight. More hopeful because it is open to anyone who acts well, regardless of abstract philosophical talent.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Hexis**: stable disposition built through habituation — the category under which virtue falls
- **Akrasia**: weakness of will — acting against one's better judgment — which Plato's intellectualism cannot adequately explain
- **Virtue involves feeling as well as action**: the fully virtuous person feels the right way and acts the right way
- **Doctrine of the Mean**: every virtue is the intermediate between two vices; the mean is relative to circumstances, determined by phronesis
- **Phronesis** (practical wisdom): the master intellectual virtue; cannot be taught from rules alone; requires experience and emotional maturity
- **Theōria** (contemplation): Aristotle's candidate for the highest life — creates productive tension with the civic-virtue emphasis of the rest of the Ethics
