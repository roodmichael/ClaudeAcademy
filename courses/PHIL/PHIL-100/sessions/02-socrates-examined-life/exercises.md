## Exercise 1: Apply the Elenchus
**Type:** Thought experiment / Analysis

Choose one of the following common claims and apply the Socratic elenchus to it. Your goal is not to disprove it but to test whether you can give a definition that holds up.

Choose one:
- "Honesty is always the right thing to do."
- "A good friend always tells you what you want to hear."
- "Courage means not being afraid."

Follow these steps:
1. State the claim as a definition: "X is Y."
2. Invent a case where the definition seems wrong — where X is present but Y is not, or Y is present but X is not.
3. Revise the definition to handle the objection.
4. Try to find an objection to the revised definition.

**What to observe / record:**
- Can you reach a definition that survives all objections?
- If not, does this mean the concept doesn't exist, or just that it's hard to define?
- What does this exercise feel like — frustrating, clarifying, both?

Write 1–2 paragraphs on what you discover.

## Exercise 2: Socratic Ignorance in Your Own Life
**Type:** Reflection

Socrates found that people who claimed expertise often lacked it — and that they were worse off than he was because they didn't know they didn't know.

Think of a belief you hold that you have never seriously questioned. It can be about ethics, politics, religion, relationships — anything you consider important.

**What to observe / record:**
- What is the belief?
- How did you come to hold it? (Experience, upbringing, osmosis?)
- Have you ever tested it? If someone asked you to *defend* it — to give reasons that don't just repeat the belief — could you?
- Does this exercise make you more or less confident in the belief?

This is a genuinely personal reflection. There is no right answer. Write 1–2 paragraphs.

## Exercise 3: The Examined Life — Close Reading
**Type:** Reading / Analysis

Find this passage in your copy of the *Apology* (it appears after Socrates receives the death sentence, when he addresses the jury that voted to acquit):

> "For if you kill me you will not easily find a successor to me... I am a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God... and the state is a great and noble steed who is tardy in his motions owing to his very size."

**What to observe / record:**
1. What is Socrates actually claiming about himself here? Is this arrogant, or something else?
2. Why does he compare Athens to a horse and himself to a gadfly, rather than, say, a teacher and a student?
3. Do you think this self-presentation helped or hurt his case with the jury?

Write 3–5 sentences for each question.
