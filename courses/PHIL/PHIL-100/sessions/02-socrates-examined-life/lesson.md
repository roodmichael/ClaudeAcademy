# Session 02: Socrates and the Examined Life
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to describe the Socratic method (elenchus), explain what Socratic ignorance means and why it is philosophically significant, and articulate what Socrates means by the claim that "the unexamined life is not worth living."

---

## Opening

In 399 BCE, the city of Athens put a 70-year-old philosopher on trial for his life.

The charges: impiety — failing to acknowledge the gods the city acknowledged — and corrupting the youth. He had no army. He had no political office. He wrote nothing. His weapons were questions.

His name was Socrates. He was found guilty by a jury of 500 Athenian citizens. He was sentenced to death.

Before he drank the hemlock, he gave a speech. That speech — Plato's *Apology* — is one of the foundational documents of Western civilization. It is your primary text for this session.

---

## The Oracle and the Puzzle of Wisdom

The story Socrates tells in the *Apology* begins with a friend visiting the Oracle at Delphi — the most authoritative religious voice in Greece — and asking whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The Oracle answered: no.

Socrates was puzzled. He knew he was not particularly wise. So he set out to test the oracle's pronouncement by questioning people with reputations for wisdom: politicians, poets, craftsmen. What he found was consistent and disturbing. These people claimed knowledge they did not have. They believed they knew things they didn't know. Socrates found that he was at least in this respect different from them:

*"I am better off than he is — for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know."*

This is **Socratic ignorance** — not stupidity, and not genuine ignorance about everything, but the wisdom of knowing the limits of one's own knowledge. It is a second-order awareness: knowing what you do not know.

The oracle, Socrates concludes, meant something precise: "true wisdom belongs to the god, and the wisdom of men is little or nothing." The best any human can do is to know that they don't know.

---

## The Elenchus: Testing Claims Through Questions

How did Socrates test wisdom claims? Through the **elenchus** (Greek: "refutation" or "testing") — a method of systematic cross-examination.

The pattern is consistent across Plato's early dialogues:

1. Someone claims to know what a virtue is: *"I know what courage is."*
2. Socrates asks for a definition: *"What is courage?"*
3. The person gives an answer: *"Courage is standing your ground in battle."*
4. Socrates tests it with cases: *"But a soldier who retreats to fight on better terms — isn't that sometimes courageous?"*
5. The original definition fails. The person revises.
6. The new definition fails too.
7. Eventually: **aporia** — impasse, puzzlement. The person realizes they don't know what they thought they knew.

Socrates presents aporia not as failure but as progress. A person who knows they don't know is in a better position than a person who falsely believes they know. The elenchus is a tool for intellectual honesty.

This is also why Athens found Socrates threatening. He was publicly exposing the leading citizens' ignorance — in front of witnesses, including their students and children.

---

## The Divine Mission: The Gadfly of Athens

Socrates defends his practice not as a personal interest but as a divine obligation. He compares himself to a **gadfly** — a stinging insect that keeps a sluggish horse from falling asleep:

*"I am a sort of gadfly, given to the state by God; and the state is a great and noble steed who is tardy in his motions owing to his very size, and requires to be stirred into life."*

The city needed to be questioned. Athenians were asleep, sleepwalking through life with false certainties about virtue, knowledge, and the good. Socrates' job — as he understood it — was to wake them up by asking questions they couldn't answer.

He makes a striking claim: stop him, and you harm yourselves more than you harm him. Another gadfly will not easily be found.

---

## The Examined Life

The most famous line in the *Apology* comes when Socrates imagines being offered acquittal on the condition that he stop philosophizing. He refuses:

*"The greatest good of man is daily to converse about virtue... and that the life which is unexamined is not worth living."*

What does he mean by an **examined life**?

He does not mean being anxious or self-critical. He means living with your commitments under rational scrutiny — asking what you value, why you value it, whether your actions are consistent with what you say you value, and whether what you say you value is genuinely good.

Most people go through life believing things they have never examined: about justice, about what makes someone a good person, about what is worth pursuing. They inherit these beliefs from their culture, their family, their religion. Socrates' point is that *living on inherited beliefs without examination* is living below your capacity as a rational being.

The examined life is not comfortable. It often reveals that things you thought you knew, you don't. But Socrates thought this discomfort was essential to being fully human.

---

## On Death: Neither Good Nor Evil Certain

Socrates is condemned. He addresses his accusers in the famous closing lines:

*"The hour of departure has arrived, and we go our ways — I to die, and you to live. Which is better God only knows."*

He is not performing false calm. His reasoning about death is genuine. Death is either:
1. Dreamless sleep — which would be a tremendous rest, not a harm
2. A continuation of consciousness in which he could converse with Homer, Hesiod, and the great minds of history — even better

A just person who has lived examining virtue has nothing genuinely to fear. The only real harm is **injustice** — acting against virtue, compromising your moral integrity. Death cannot harm a good person in the way that injustice can.

This argument about death connects directly to Sessions 8, 9, and 10, where the Stoics, Epicureans, and Plato (in the Phaedo) each offer their own reasoning about why we need not fear dying.

---

## Why This Matters

You will meet people throughout your life who are certain about things they have never thought through. You will hold beliefs yourself that you have never examined. The examined life — in Socrates' sense — does not mean doubting everything forever. It means caring enough about what you believe to *test* it.

The Socratic method is also a model of intellectual humility that is hard to practice: it requires genuinely wanting to know if you are wrong, rather than wanting to win the argument.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Elenchus**: Socrates' method of testing knowledge claims through systematic questioning; leads to aporia (impasse) when a definition cannot be defended
- **Socratic ignorance**: knowing that you do not know — a second-order wisdom that Socrates considers more valuable than false certainty
- **Aporia**: productive impasse; the state of recognizing you don't know what you thought you knew
- **The examined life**: a life in which one's values, beliefs, and actions are subjected to rational scrutiny
- Socrates refuses to stop philosophizing even to save his life — he holds the examined life to be more valuable than physical survival
