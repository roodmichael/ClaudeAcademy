# Session 08: The Stoics — What Is in Our Control?
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain the Stoic dichotomy of control, describe what prohairesis is, explain why Stoics call everything outside virtue an "indifferent," and articulate how Epictetus applies these principles to practical life.

---

## Opening

Epictetus was a slave.

Born into slavery in the Roman Empire around 50 CE, he was owned by Nero's secretary. He may have been physically abused by his master. He was legally property for much of his life.

And yet his philosophy is one of the most powerful accounts of freedom ever written.

His key insight: your master can control your body, your circumstances, your legal status. He cannot control your judgment. He cannot control what you believe is good or bad. He cannot control the one thing that is genuinely and irreducibly *yours* — your faculty of choosing how to respond to what happens to you.

This is the Stoic philosophy of control, and it begins with a single sentence from the opening of the *Enchiridion* (Handbook):

*"Some things are up to us and some things are not up to us."*

---

## The Dichotomy of Control

The whole of Stoic practical philosophy flows from this distinction. Everything in existence falls into one of two categories:

**What is up to us** (*eph' hēmin*):
- Our opinions and judgments
- Our desires and aversions
- Our intentions and decisions
- In short: our moral character (*prohairesis*)

**What is not up to us**:
- Our bodies (health, appearance, physical condition)
- Our property and wealth
- Our reputation and what others think of us
- Public offices and honors
- The actions of other people
- External events generally

Epictetus is uncompromising: things outside our control are "not our own." We should treat them as externally given — available sometimes, unavailable others, not the foundation of our wellbeing.

The practical consequence: if you ground your happiness in external things — your health, your reputation, your success — you will *always* be vulnerable. Health fails. Reputations are ruined. Successes collapse. But if you ground your wellbeing only in what is genuinely up to you — your judgments and your responses — you can be free regardless of what happens.

---

## Prohairesis: The Seat of the Self

**Prohairesis** (moral purpose or will) is Epictetus's most important concept. It is the faculty by which we judge, choose, and respond to impressions.

When something happens — a friend insults you, you fall ill, you lose a job — you receive an **impression** of the event. The impression carries a judgment: "this is terrible," "this is wonderful," "this is threatening." Epictetus says: do not simply accept the impression. *Test it.* Ask whether the judgment it carries is true.

*"What disturbs men's minds is not events but their judgements on events."*

The friend insulted you. That is a fact. That it is terrible — that is a judgment, and it is not automatic. Someone with different values might not even register an insult as a harm. The judgment is *yours*; therefore you have power over whether it disturbs you.

This is not passive resignation. It is an active, disciplined practice of examining your responses and choosing them rather than being driven by them.

---

## Virtue as the Only Good

The Stoic position on value is radical: **virtue is the only genuine good**. Everything else — health, wealth, pleasure, reputation, even life itself — is an **indifferent** (*adiaphoron*).

This does not mean health and wealth don't matter at all. Stoics distinguish "preferred indifferents" (health, wealth, relationships — things worth pursuing, all else equal) from "dispreferred indifferents" (disease, poverty — things worth avoiding). But neither a preferred nor a dispreferred indifferent can affect your fundamental wellbeing. Only virtue — acting in accordance with your judgment, maintaining your moral integrity — can do that.

*"Nothing is good for a human being which does not make them just, temperate, courageous, free; nothing is bad which does not make them the contraries."* — Marcus Aurelius

The comparison to Aristotle is instructive: Aristotle includes external goods (health, friends, a measure of prosperity) as genuine components of eudaimonia. The Stoics exclude them entirely. For the Stoics, the virtuous person on the rack is still flourishing; the vicious person on a throne is still living badly.

## Epictetus in Practice

The *Enchiridion* is one of the most practically direct philosophical texts ever written. Almost every chapter is an instruction:

On loss: *"Never say about anything, 'I have lost it'; but, 'I have returned it.' Is your child dead? It has been returned. Is your wife dead? She has been returned. Is your estate taken away? Well, and is not that likewise returned?"*

On roles: *"Remember that you are an actor in a drama, the character of which is determined by the author... your duty is to act well the part that is given to you."*

On other people's behavior: *"Seek not that the things which happen should happen as you wish; but wish the things which happen to be as they are, and you will have a tranquil flow of life."*

The three disciplines that organize practical Stoic training:
1. **Discipline of Desire**: direct desire only toward virtue; treat all externals as indifferent
2. **Discipline of Action**: act fully in your social roles (parent, citizen, friend) without attachment to outcomes you cannot control
3. **Discipline of Assent**: test impressions before assenting to them; do not automatically endorse the judgment that an impression carries

## Marcus Aurelius: Stoicism in the World

Marcus Aurelius (121–180 CE) was Roman emperor for nineteen years. The *Meditations* — which he wrote as private notes to himself, never for publication — are the record of a person practicing Stoicism under extreme pressure.

What is remarkable: Marcus was the most powerful person in the known world, surrounded by court intrigue, military campaigns, and the constant flattery of subordinates. The *Meditations* are his attempt to remain grounded, to keep testing his impressions, to remember that glory and power are indifferents.

*"Loss is nothing else but change, and change is Nature's delight."*

*"When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: The people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous and surly... But I have seen the beauty of good, and the ugliness of evil, and have recognized that the wrongdoer has a nature related to my own."*

Marcus is not performing Stoicism for an audience. He is reminding *himself* — repeatedly, because the reminders don't stick automatically. The practice is daily.

---

## The Connection to Plato and Aristotle

The Stoics share with Plato the conviction that what matters is not external fortune but internal condition. With Aristotle, they share the emphasis on virtue as constitutive of the good life. But both comparisons highlight their radicalism:

- More extreme than Plato: for Plato, wisdom is the key; for the Stoics, virtue (right judgment and right action) is both sufficient and the only genuine good.
- More extreme than Aristotle: Aristotle includes external goods; the Stoics exclude them entirely.

---

## Why This Matters

The dichotomy of control is one of the most actionable ideas in this course. You will encounter situations throughout life — failures, losses, events outside your control — where the only real question is: how will you respond? The Stoics are not offering comfort through denial ("nothing bad happened"). They are offering a framework for locating your actual freedom: in your judgment, your response, your character.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Dichotomy of control**: what is up to us (judgments, desires, prohairesis) vs. what is not (body, wealth, reputation, other people's actions)
- **Prohairesis**: moral purpose or will — the faculty of judging impressions and choosing responses; the core of what is genuinely ours
- **Indifferents**: everything outside virtue — health, wealth, reputation — neither genuinely good nor bad; can be "preferred" or "dispreferred" without determining wellbeing
- **Three disciplines**: desire, action, and assent — the framework for practical Stoic training
- Virtue is the only good; the virtuous person is free regardless of circumstances
