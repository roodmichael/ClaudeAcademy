# Session 10: The Soul and What Comes After
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain the four arguments for the soul's immortality in the Phaedo, describe how the Phaedo integrates the Theory of Forms with the doctrine of recollection, and articulate what Plato means by philosophy as preparation for death.

---

## Opening

Today you are with Socrates on the last day of his life.

His friends have gathered — Simmias, Cebes, Phaedo, others — in the prison at Athens. He will drink hemlock before sunset. They have spent the day in conversation. Not in mourning, not in final pleasantries, but in philosophy. The topic: does the soul survive death?

The *Phaedo* is Plato's most emotionally powerful dialogue. It is also where the Theory of Forms and the doctrine of recollection — introduced in Sessions 3 and 4 — are put to their deepest use. And it asks a question that threads through the entire course: how should a person who has lived for truth relate to death?

Before we reach the arguments, notice Socrates' mood. He is cheerful. His friends weep; he does not. This is not a performance. It is the proof, for Plato, of a philosophical life well lived.

---

## Philosophy as Preparation for Death

Socrates opens the dialogue with a striking claim that startles even his friends: *"The one aim of those who practice philosophy in the proper manner is to practice for dying and death."* (64a)

What does this mean?

Plato's argument: philosophy is the practice of freeing the soul from the body's misleading influence. The body's desires distract reason; the senses provide imprecise and fluctuating information; bodily pleasures and pains tie us to the physical world and prevent clear thought.

The philosopher, through a life of disciplined inquiry, has been gradually separating the soul from attachment to the body. Not by mortifying the flesh, but by cultivating the soul's orientation toward the eternal, unchanging Forms rather than the transient pleasures of the physical world.

Death is the *complete* separation of soul from body. The philosopher has been practicing this separation throughout their life. Therefore, death is not a loss for the philosopher — it is the culmination of everything they have been working toward.

This connects the *Phaedo* directly to Sessions 2, 4, and 5:
- Session 2 (Apology): Socrates says a just person has nothing to fear from death
- Session 4: the philosopher's ascent from the cave to the Form of the Good
- Session 5: the soul's proper orientation is toward reason, not appetite

The *Phaedo* gives the metaphysical foundation for all of these.

## The Four Arguments for Immortality

Plato presents four arguments in succession. None is decisive on its own; Plato presents them as mutually reinforcing, building a cumulative case.

**Argument 1: The Cyclical Argument (70c–72e)**

All opposites generate from their opposites: the waking state comes from sleep, the sleeping from waking; the large from the small, the small from the large. By analogy, the living come from the dead and the dead from the living.

If everything that dies simply ceased to exist, the cycle would eventually deplete itself — all things would end up dead, with no source of new life. The soul must persist through death and return to animate new bodies.

*Assessment*: The argument assumes a kind of cosmic balance that is not obviously true. It also applies to all living things, not specifically to human souls — which may not be what Plato wants.

**Argument 2: The Argument from Recollection (72e–78b)**

This argument revisits Session 3 directly. We recognize the Form of Equality when we see imperfectly equal things (two sticks that are approximately equal). But physical objects are never perfectly equal — they always "fall short" of the standard. We must have encountered perfect Equality *before* perceiving physical things — that is, before birth.

Therefore, the soul existed before birth and had knowledge of the Forms. Learning is indeed recollection (as the *Meno* argued). And if the soul existed before birth, there is at least reason to think it might survive death.

*Assessment*: This argument only establishes pre-natal existence — not survival after death. Socrates acknowledges this and connects it to the next argument.

**Argument 3: The Affinity Argument (78b–84b)**

The soul resembles the eternal, invisible, simple, and unchanging realm of Forms. The body resembles the visible, composite, and changeable realm of physical things.

A soul that has pursued philosophy and freed itself from bodily attachment will, at death, naturally move toward what it is akin to — the eternal, intelligible realm of Forms. A soul that has been absorbed in the body and its pleasures may have difficulty separating and may linger near the physical world.

*Assessment*: The argument establishes likelihood, not certainty — Socrates says as much. It depends on the soul's genuine kinship with the Forms, which is not independently proven.

**Argument 4: The Final Argument — The Form of Life (102b–107b)**

The soul is not merely *associated* with life — it has life essentially, the way fire has heat essentially. Fire cannot admit coldness without ceasing to be fire. The soul cannot admit death (the opposite of life) without ceasing to be a soul. Therefore the soul is indestructible.

More technically: the soul belongs to the class of things that "participate in" the Form of Life. Whatever participates in the Form of Life cannot admit its opposite (the Form of Death) while remaining itself. The soul must either withdraw (survive) or be destroyed — and Plato argues the soul's kinship with the eternal makes withdrawal more plausible.

*Assessment*: The most sophisticated argument. Critics note it may prove only that the soul doesn't die, not that it persists — it might simply disperse. Cebes raises something like this objection, and Socrates responds.

## The Forms Revisited

The *Phaedo* is the dialogue where the Theory of Forms is most fully developed. Plato presents Forms not just as abstract objects of knowledge but as:

- The patterns by which physical things are what they are (a stick is approximately equal because it "participates in" the Form of Equality)
- The proper home of the soul — the realm the soul is akin to and toward which it gravitates when purified
- The standard of genuine knowledge — Forms, being unchanging, can be truly known; physical things, being in flux, can only be approximately approximated

This brings together everything from Sessions 3, 4, and 5 into a single unified account: the soul knows Forms through recollection, the Forms are what genuine knowledge is of, and the soul is immortal partly because of its kinship with the eternal Forms.

## The Myth of the Afterlife

The dialogue ends with Socrates narrating a myth about what happens to souls after death: the virtuous ascend to higher regions; those who have indulged the body's pleasures are weighed down. Socrates presents this explicitly as a myth — not a demonstrably true account — but one that it is reasonable to hold given the arguments.

This is characteristic of Plato: at the limits of argument, he shifts to myth — a story that carries philosophical meaning even if its literal truth is uncertain.

The final scene: Socrates drinks the hemlock calmly, comforts his weeping friends, and dies. His last words are to Crito: *"Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; pay it and do not forget."* (Asclepius was the god of healing; a cock was the customary offering for recovery from illness. Scholars have debated for centuries what Socrates meant — perhaps that death is a kind of cure, a recovery from the illness of embodied life.)

## The Course Comes Together

The *Phaedo* is the right place to end because it reaches back and connects to everything:

- **Pre-Socratics** (Session 1): the question of what is truly real — Parmenides' unchanging Being vs. Heraclitean flux — finds its Platonic resolution in the Forms
- **Socrates** (Sessions 2–3): the examined life, Socratic ignorance, recollection — all central to the *Phaedo*
- **Theory of Forms** (Session 4): developed here in its fullest form
- **Soul and justice** (Session 5): the soul ordered toward reason and the Good, not appetite
- **Aristotle** (Sessions 6–7): would reject the soul-body dualism here entirely; the soul for Aristotle is the *form* of the body, not something separable from it
- **Stoics** (Session 8): also face death calmly — but not because the soul is immortal; because death is a natural process and virtue is the only good
- **Epicureans** (Session 9): face death through the symmetry argument — non-existence cannot harm us

Every figure in this course has an answer to the question the *Phaedo* poses directly: what should a person who has lived well think about death? Plato's answer is the most metaphysically ambitious — and the most demanding to evaluate.

---

## Why This Matters

The *Phaedo* asks a question that has not been answered. Whether or not the soul survives death, the way you hold that question — fearfully or philosophically — shapes how you live. Socrates' equanimity in the face of death is not the result of certainty about immortality. It is the result of a life lived consistently with his deepest values.

That is something even the skeptic can learn from.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Philosophy, for Plato, is practice for death: the gradual purification of the soul from bodily distraction
- **Four arguments**: Cyclical (life from death), Recollection (soul pre-existed birth), Affinity (soul akin to eternal Forms), Final/Life-Form (soul has life essentially and cannot admit death)
- The *Phaedo* integrates recollection (Session 3) and Theory of Forms (Session 4) into a single argument
- Aristotle would reject the soul-body dualism entirely; for him, soul is the form of the body, not a separate entity
- The closing scene — Socrates' calm death — is Plato's image of the philosophical life fully realized
