# Session 03: What Can We Know?
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to state Meno's paradox precisely, explain the theory of recollection as Plato's response to it, and distinguish between knowledge and true belief.

---

## Opening

Here is a puzzle that should stop you in your tracks.

You want to learn what virtue is. So you ask: "What is virtue?" — just as Socrates does. But Meno, Plato's interlocutor in the dialogue named after him, raises an objection that threatens to make the whole inquiry pointless:

*"How will you look for it... what sort of thing will you propose to look for? Or even if you meet right up against it, how will you know that this is the thing you didn't know?"*

This is **Meno's paradox**, also called the paradox of inquiry. It says: you either already know what you're looking for (in which case there's no point looking) or you don't know (in which case you couldn't recognize it if you found it). Either way, inquiry is impossible.

If the paradox is right, then not just Socratic questioning but all genuine learning is impossible. This is not a minor puzzle. Plato's response to it shapes much of Western philosophy.

---

## The Dialogue's Starting Point

The *Meno* opens with a direct question from Meno: can virtue be taught?

Before the paradox, Socrates tries to establish what virtue *is* — his standard move. Meno gives several definitions:

- Virtue for a man is managing public affairs well
- Virtue for a woman is managing the household and obeying her husband
- Different virtues for different people

Socrates objects: he asked for virtue in general, not a catalog of virtues. A bee is still a bee regardless of size, shape, or origin — what makes it a bee? Similarly, what makes all virtuous things virtuous?

Meno tries again and again. Each definition fails. Then comes the paradox.

---

## The Paradox of Inquiry

Meno's challenge is logical, not just rhetorical. Put it this way:

- **Option A**: You already know what virtue is. Then there's nothing to inquire about — you already have the answer.
- **Option B**: You don't know what virtue is. Then even if you encountered virtue, you wouldn't be able to recognize it as such.

Either way, inquiry seems pointless.

Socrates calls this "an eristic argument" — a tricky logical puzzle. But he takes it seriously. His response is not to dismiss the paradox but to reframe the entire concept of learning.

---

## The Theory of Recollection (Anamnesis)

Plato's solution: **learning is not receiving new information. It is remembering what the soul already knows.**

The soul, Plato proposes, is immortal and has lived many lives. In its prenatal state, it had access to genuine knowledge — including knowledge of abstract truths like mathematical and ethical principles. When the soul enters a body at birth, this knowledge is obscured but not lost. What we call "learning" is actually *recollecting* what the soul already knows.

This dissolves the paradox: we can recognize what we're looking for because, at some level, we already know it. We're not learning something wholly new — we're recovering something we once knew.

Socrates demonstrates this with a striking experiment. He calls over an uneducated slave boy who has never studied geometry. Through a series of questions — never stating any answers himself — Socrates guides the boy to correctly solve a geometrical problem about doubling the area of a square.

The point: the boy arrived at a mathematical truth through guided questioning, not instruction. Where did the knowledge come from, if not from some prior state?

Socrates is careful: he claims only that the demonstration makes recollection *probable*, not certain. But Meno is persuaded that inquiry is possible, and the dialogue continues.

---

## Can Virtue Be Taught?

The *Meno* returns to its original question: can virtue be taught? The investigation goes through several stages:

**Virtue as knowledge**: If virtue is beneficial, and what is beneficial must be guided by knowledge (since unguided desire causes harm), then virtue must be a form of knowledge or wisdom.

**But wait — virtue doesn't seem to be taught**: If virtue were knowledge, it could be taught the way mathematics is taught. But virtuous parents do not reliably produce virtuous children. There are no consistent teachers of virtue the way there are teachers of mathematics.

**Conclusion (provisional and puzzling)**: Maybe virtue is a kind of **true belief** that is a gift from the gods, not a systematic discipline. The dialogue ends without a settled answer.

---

## Knowledge vs. True Belief

The *Meno* introduces one of philosophy's most important distinctions: **epistēmē** (knowledge) vs. **ortho doxa** (true/correct belief).

Both knowledge and true belief can guide action correctly. A person who *knows* the road to Larissa and a person who just *believes* the road to Larissa (correctly) will both arrive there. So what's the difference?

Socrates says: true belief is like statues of Daedalus — valuable, but they run away unless you "tie them down." Knowledge is tied down by **understanding the reason why** something is true. True belief can be "talked away" — if someone gives you a convincing counter-argument, you abandon the belief because you couldn't defend it. Knowledge, grounded in understanding, cannot be dislodged that way.

Example: You correctly believe that a bridge is safe to cross. Someone argues compellingly that it isn't. You become uncertain. Why? Because you didn't *know* it was safe — you believed it. If you had genuine knowledge (engineering understanding of the bridge's structure), the argument would not shake you.

True belief without understanding is unstable. Knowledge requires grasping not just *that* something is true but *why* it is true.

---

## Why This Matters

The knowledge/true belief distinction is not just academic. Think about how many things you believe because you've always believed them, or because they came from a trusted source — but you couldn't explain *why* they're true. You might be right. But you might also abandon those beliefs under pressure, or fail to apply them correctly in new situations.

The *Meno* asks: what kind of grip do you have on what you believe? And more urgently: is that grip tight enough for the things that matter most?

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Meno's paradox**: the logical puzzle that inquiry is impossible — you either already know what you seek, or you can't recognize it
- **Anamnesis (recollection)**: Plato's response — the soul had prenatal knowledge; learning is recovering it through guided questioning
- **Epistēmē**: genuine knowledge — stable, grounded in understanding *why*, cannot be talked away
- **Ortho doxa**: true belief — guides action correctly but can be dislodged by a clever argument because the knower lacks the explanatory "tether"
- The *Meno* ends inconclusively: virtue might be a divine gift of true belief rather than teachable knowledge
