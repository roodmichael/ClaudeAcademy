# Session 09: Epicurus — Pleasure, Friendship, and the Fear of Death
**Course:** PHIL-100
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain Epicurus's definition of pleasure (ataraxia and aponia), describe his classification of desires, state the symmetry argument against fearing death, and articulate how Epicurus differs from the Stoics on what constitutes the good life.

---

## Opening

The word "epicurean" in modern English means something like "devoted to refined sensory pleasure" — fine food, fine wine, indulgence. The historical Epicurus would find this deeply ironic.

Epicurus founded his school — called the Garden — in Athens around 307 BCE. He lived simply. He ate bread and water. He valued friendship above almost everything else. His philosophy was a therapy for anxiety, a practical guide to living without fear.

His central claim: **pleasure is the highest good**. But what he means by pleasure is almost the opposite of what we usually mean by it.

---

## Pleasure Redefined

For Epicurus, the highest pleasure is not the excitement of sensory indulgence but **ataraxia** — tranquility, the freedom from mental anxiety — and **aponia** — freedom from physical pain.

These are *negative* states: the absence of distress. Not excitement, not ecstasy, not the thrill of a feast. Just this: a mind at peace, a body free from pain.

Why? Because Epicurus observed that the pursuit of intense pleasures — wealth, fame, luxury — tends to produce anxiety, not peace. You fear losing what you have. You want more. The pleasure is brief; the wanting is chronic. The attempt to achieve positive sensory pleasure is, paradoxically, a major source of suffering.

The modest pleasures — simple food, water on a hot day, good conversation with friends, time for reflection — satisfy without leaving hunger. They do not generate more wanting. This is the Epicurean good life: small, stable, sufficient.

---

## The Classification of Desires

Epicurus distinguishes three types of desire:

**Natural and necessary**: food, shelter, warmth, freedom from pain, basic companionship. These are *easy to satisfy* and *essential to remove distress*. Pursuing them is straightforwardly rational.

**Natural but not necessary**: richer food, wine, sexual pleasure. These are *pleasant* when available but not required for the absence of pain. They can be enjoyed moderately without harm.

**Empty desires**: fame, power, unlimited wealth, being remembered after death. These are *groundless* — their satisfaction cannot bring genuine peace because the appetite for them is never finite. The more you have, the more you want.

Epicurus's prescription: satisfy the first category reliably; enjoy the second occasionally; avoid the third entirely. The person who needs only natural and necessary things is almost impossible to disturb.

---

## The Tetrapharmakos: The Four-Fold Cure

Epicurus identified four fears that destroy human peace. His philosophical cure addresses each directly:

1. **Don't fear the gods**: the gods, if they exist, are perfectly blessed and have no reason to interfere in human affairs. Divine punishment is a myth designed to control people.

2. **Don't fear death**: death is nothing to us. (The argument below.)

3. **What is good is easy to get**: the genuine goods — simple food, friendship, philosophical conversation — are readily available to almost anyone.

4. **What is terrible is easy to endure**: truly severe pain tends to be brief; chronic pain, if endured with philosophical understanding, does not destroy the soul.

---

## Death Is Nothing to Us

Epicurus's argument against fearing death is one of the most famous arguments in ancient philosophy:

*"When we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not."*

The logic: death is the end of sensation. Since we can only experience good and evil (benefit or harm), and death involves no sensation at all, death cannot harm us. We will not be there to experience it.

**The Symmetry Argument**: Think about the infinite time before your birth. You were not conscious; you felt nothing; there was no suffering. Are you troubled by that? Almost certainly not. The time after your death will be exactly the same: no consciousness, no suffering, no "you" to experience absence. If pre-natal non-existence doesn't disturb you, post-mortem non-existence should not either.

What people actually fear when they fear death is usually something else:
- Pain in the process of dying (a different issue — a matter of the body, not death itself)
- Being forgotten or losing achievements (the empty desire for fame)
- Grief at losing life's goods (which Epicurus addresses separately)
- Fear of divine punishment (which the first tenet of the tetrapharmakos addresses)

Strip away these substitutes, and the fear of death itself — of simple non-existence — dissolves.

---

## Friendship

Epicurus's account of the good life includes one element that surprises many students: friendship is central. Not as a means to other ends, but as one of the most reliable and deep sources of pleasure.

*"Of all the things which wisdom provides to make us entirely happy, much the greatest is the possession of friendship."*

The Garden was organized around friendship: a community of philosophical companions living simply, sharing ideas, supporting one another. This was not a cold philosophical arrangement. Epicurus wrote affectionate letters to his friends, remembered them in his will, valued their company deeply.

This differs from the Stoics, who tend to treat relationships as duties to be performed without attachment. For Epicurus, genuine warmth and connection are themselves central goods.

---

## Epicurus vs. the Stoics

Both schools seek **tranquility** (*ataraxia*). But their paths diverge:

| | Epicureans | Stoics |
|---|---|---|
| Highest good | Pleasure (ataraxia + aponia) | Virtue alone |
| External goods | Natural goods (simple pleasures, friendship) are genuine | Indifferent — cannot affect flourishing |
| Engagement | Withdrawal from politics and fame recommended | Civic duty and social roles emphasized |
| Death | Non-existence; nothing to fear | Natural part of the cosmic order; accept it |
| Community | Philosophical friendship at center | Social duties, but without attachment |

Epicurus famously recommended **withdrawal** from political life: politics generates anxiety, competition, the pursuit of fame — all sources of disturbance. The wise person cultivates their garden, nurtures friendships, studies philosophy, and stays out of power struggles.

The Stoics hold the opposite view on civic engagement: Marcus Aurelius governed an empire; Epictetus emphasized one's duties as citizen, parent, friend.

---

## Why This Matters

Epicurus's therapy for the fear of death is more than historically interesting. The question he addresses is real: most people spend some portion of their mental energy in a low-level background anxiety about death. Epicurus offers a genuinely reasoned argument that this anxiety is misplaced — not by denying that death happens, but by examining exactly what death is and demonstrating that non-existence cannot harm the non-existent.

Whether or not you accept the argument, thinking it through carefully changes how you hold the fear.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Ataraxia**: tranquility or freedom from anxiety — the highest pleasure for Epicurus
- **Aponia**: freedom from bodily pain
- **Three desire categories**: natural and necessary (pursue) / natural but unnecessary (enjoy moderately) / empty (avoid)
- **Tetrapharmakos**: the four-fold cure — don't fear gods, don't fear death, what is good is easy, what is terrible can be endured
- **Symmetry argument**: pre-natal non-existence is not troubling; post-mortem non-existence is the same; death is nothing to us
- Epicurus on friendship: not a means but one of the greatest goods in itself
- Key contrast with Stoics: both seek tranquility, but through different routes — pleasure-defined freedom vs. virtue-only freedom
