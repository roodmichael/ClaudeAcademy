# Session 09: Potential Form and Relative Clauses
**Course:** JAPN-101
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to form potential sentences using both conjugated forms and ことができる, and construct Japanese relative clauses using plain-form verb phrases before a head noun.

---

## Opening

Here is a sentence in three pieces: "the book," "that I bought," "yesterday." In English, you build it by putting the relative clause after the noun: *the book that I bought yesterday.* In Japanese, the order is reversed and there is no "that" at all: *yesterday bought book* — 昨日買った本. The noun comes last. Everything that describes it comes before.

This word order is not unusual in Japanese — it is the rule. Japanese is a head-final language: the thing being described always comes at the end of its modifier. Once you understand this, relative clauses stop feeling exotic and start feeling inevitable.

---

## Potential Form — Systematic Review

You have already encountered potential form through the られる/れる ambiguity in Session 08. Here it gets its full treatment.

**Formation:**

**Ru-verbs:** Drop る, add られる
- 見る → 見**られる** (can see)
- 食べる → 食べ**られる** (can eat)
- 起きる → 起き**られる** (can wake up)

**U-verbs:** Replace final kana with え-row sound + る
- 書く → 書**ける**
- 飲む → 飲**める**
- 話す → 話**せる**
- 待つ → 待**てる**
- 買う → 買**える**
- 歩く → 歩**ける**

**Irregular:**
- する → **できる** (the potential of する is always できる, not される or すれる)
- 来る → **来られる**

**Alternative form — ことができる:**
Any potential expression can also be written as `[verb plain form] + ことができる`. This is slightly more formal:
- 話せる = 話すことができる (can speak)
- 食べられる = 食べることができる (can eat)

**Three functions of potential:**

1. **Personal ability:** フランス語が話せる。(I can speak French.)
2. **Circumstantial possibility:** 今日は早く帰られる。(I can go home early today — the situation allows it.)
3. **Inherent property/attribute:** この橋は渡れない。(This bridge cannot be crossed — it's structurally insufficient.)

**Transitive vs. intransitive confusion:**

Japanese has paired verb forms for sensation that trip up learners:
- 見る (miru, to look at — transitive) → 見られる (potential: can see)
- 見える (mieru, to be visible — intransitive): describes automatic visibility, not ability

If you ask 「黒板が見えますか」you are asking "Is the blackboard visible?" (anyone can see it if the view is unobstructed).
If you ask 「黒板が見られますか」you are asking "Can you see the blackboard?" (are you personally able to?).

Same pattern applies to 聞く (to listen) vs. 聞こえる (to be audible).

## Japanese Relative Clauses

English uses a relative pronoun (who, that, which) after the noun to introduce a modifying clause:
- *the person **who** called me*
- *the book **that** I read*

Japanese uses **no relative pronoun**. The modifying clause comes **before** the noun in **plain form**:

Structure: **[modifying clause in plain form] + [head noun]**

Examples:
- 昨日買った本 (the book I bought yesterday — 昨日買った modifies 本)
- 田中さんが作る料理 (the dish Tanaka-san makes — 田中さんが作る modifies 料理)
- 先週見た映画 (the movie I watched last week)

**Rule 1 — Plain form always:**

The verb inside a relative clause must be in plain (dictionary/casual) form, regardless of how polite the rest of the sentence is.

- ❌ 田中さんが作ります料理は美味しい。(polite form inside relative clause — wrong)
- ✅ 田中さんが作る料理は美味しい。(plain form inside relative clause — correct)

**Rule 2 — No は inside the relative clause:**

The topic marker は belongs at the sentence level; it cannot appear inside an embedded clause:
- ❌ 田中さんは作る料理 (は inside the clause — wrong)
- ✅ 田中さんが作る料理 (が inside the clause — correct)

Note: は can appear after the modified noun as the main clause topic: **田中さんが作る料理は**美味しい。 (The dish that Tanaka-san makes is delicious — は marks 料理 as the sentence topic, not as part of the clause.)

**Rule 3 — が/の alternation:**

The subject particle inside a relative clause can be either が or の:
- 田中さんが作る料理 = 田中さんの作る料理 (both correct and identical in meaning)

The の version uses an archaic literary particle that is still active inside embedded clauses. Both are grammatically correct.

**The head noun gap:**

The head noun plays an implied role inside the embedded clause. The role is not grammatically marked — the reader infers it:

- 私が読んだ本 (the book that I read — 本 is the implied object of 読む)
- 田中さんが住む町 (the town where Tanaka-san lives — 町 is the implied location)
- 友達が使うペン (the pen that my friend uses — ペン is the implied object)

The gap can represent subject, object, location, time, instrument — any grammatical role. This flexibility is what makes relative clauses powerful.

## Building Complex Noun Phrases

Relative clauses can modify complex nouns and stack with adjectives:

- 昨日駅で会った背の高い人 (the tall person I met at the station yesterday)
- 私が小学生の時に住んでいた古い家 (the old house I lived in when I was in elementary school)

These feel long in English, but in Japanese they read naturally because the head noun anchors everything at the end.

---

## Why This Matters

Relative clauses are fundamental to expressing anything beyond simple statements. The moment you want to say "the coffee I made this morning" or "the project I'm working on" or "the person who taught me programming," you need a relative clause. At N3, these should be automatic.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Potential form: ru-verbs → drop る + られる; u-verbs → え-row + る; する → できる
- ことができる = formal alternative for any potential expression
- 見える/聞こえる are intransitive (automatic visibility/audibility), not ability — distinct from 見られる/聞ける
- Relative clauses in Japanese: [clause in plain form] + [head noun]; no relative pronoun
- Rule: plain form inside the clause; no は inside the clause; が/の are interchangeable for the subject
- Head noun plays an implied grammatical role inside the embedded clause — identify the role from context
