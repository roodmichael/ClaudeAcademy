# Session 01: ている and てある — Aspect and Deliberate State
**Course:** JAPN-101
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to distinguish ongoing action from resultant state using ている, and distinguish natural state from deliberate state using てある.

---

## Opening

Here is a puzzle. In English, "the window is open" and "someone opened the window" describe the same physical situation. In Japanese, these are grammatically distinct sentences that carry completely different information — and a native speaker would never confuse them. What is Japanese doing that English cannot?

The answer is **aspect** — not tense. Japanese grammar is not primarily interested in *when* something happened. It is interested in *how* an action unfolds relative to now: Is it ongoing? Did it reach a definitive endpoint? Is the result still present? Was the result caused by a deliberate human agent? These are the questions that ている and てある are built to answer.

---

## Japanese Marks Aspect, Not Just Tense

Most learners arrive at ている knowing it means "is doing." That is true, but only half the picture.

**ている (te-iru)** — formed by attaching いる to the te-form of a verb — has two distinct meanings depending on the verb it attaches to:

1. **Ongoing action:** The action is in progress right now.
   - 食べている (tabete iru) — is eating (the eating is happening now)
   - 走っている (hashitte iru) — is running

2. **Resultant state:** The action reached completion and its result persists.
   - 結婚している (kekkon shite iru) — is married (got married at some past point; the state persists)
   - 死んでいる (shinde iru) — is dead (died; the state persists)

How do you know which meaning applies? Ask: **can this action happen twice in a row?**

If the answer is yes, it is a **durative verb** (action that takes time), and ている marks ongoing action. You can eat twice in a row, so 食べている = is eating.

If the answer is no, it is an **instantaneous change-of-state verb**, and ている marks the resulting state. You cannot get married twice in a row without a divorce in between, so 結婚している = is married (not "is getting married").

**Motion verbs** follow the instantaneous pattern: 行っている (itte iru) means "went and is there," not "is in the process of going." This surprises many learners.

---

## The Colloquial Contraction

In casual spoken Japanese, ている contracts to てる and ていた contracts to てた. You will hear this constantly in daily conversation.

- 食べている → 食べてる (eating right now)
- 食べていた → 食べてた (was eating)

Both forms are correct in casual registers. In formal writing and polite speech, use the full form.

---

## てある — When Someone Did It on Purpose

Now back to the open window. Japanese has a separate construction for this:

**てある (te-aru)** — formed by attaching ある to the te-form of a *transitive* verb — describes a state that exists *because someone deliberately created it*. The agent does not need to be named. What matters is that a human made a deliberate choice.

- 窓が開けてある (mado ga akete aru) — The window is kept open [someone deliberately opened it and it remains open]
- メモが書いてある (memo ga kaite aru) — A note has been written [someone wrote it intentionally]

Compare these three sentences about the same window:

| Sentence | Japanese | Meaning |
|----------|----------|---------|
| Natural state | 窓が開いている | The window is open (no agent, could have opened by itself) |
| Deliberate state | 窓が開けてある | The window is open (someone deliberately opened it) |
| Preparation in progress | 窓を開けておく | I'll open the window (in advance, for a reason) |

The difference between 開いている (intransitive: opened naturally) and 開けてある (transitive + ある: deliberately kept open) is the entire point. てある requires a transitive verb — a verb that takes an object — because someone had to *do something to* the window.

**Particle behavior with てある:**
- が marks the object/result from a neutral perspective: 窓**が**開けてある
- を foregrounds the agent's intent: 窓**を**開けてある (I've opened the window [for a specific reason])

---

## Why This Matters

These two constructions appear in almost every Japanese conversation and text. If you hear ている without the deliberate-state distinction, you will misread entire passages. If you use てある without understanding transitive/intransitive, you will produce ungrammatical sentences. And — critically for later in this course — the contrast between natural state and deliberate state recurs in passive voice (Session 06) and causative voice (Session 07). Understanding agency now makes those sessions easier.

The cultural implication is also worth noting: てある often implies care and preparation. 料理が作ってある (the food has been prepared) signals that someone thought ahead on your behalf. This is a grammatical construction with a built-in social meaning.

---

## Key Takeaways

- ている with a durative verb = ongoing action (is doing)
- ている with a change-of-state verb = resultant state (is in the state of having done)
- てある = a state created by deliberate human action; transitive verbs only
- The "can it happen twice?" heuristic reliably distinguishes durative from change-of-state verbs
- In casual speech: ている → てる; ていた → てた
