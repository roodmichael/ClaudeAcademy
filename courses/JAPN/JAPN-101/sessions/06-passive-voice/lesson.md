# Session 06: Passive Voice — Direct and Adversative
**Course:** JAPN-101
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to form direct passive sentences in Japanese, form the indirect (adversative) passive, and choose the correct agent particle (に, から, によって).

---

## Opening

In English, passive voice is often a tool for avoiding responsibility: "Mistakes were made." In Japanese, passive has that function too — but it also has another use that English completely lacks. Japanese has a passive construction for describing situations where someone is *negatively affected* by an action that was not even done directly *to* them. The baby cried, and you were affected. The neighbor played loud music, and you were affected. There is a dedicated grammatical construction for this.

This is **adversative passive** — one of the most characteristically Japanese grammatical features you will encounter at N3.

---

## Direct Passive Formation

The passive is formed by attaching **れる** (for u-verbs) or **られる** (for ru-verbs) to the verb base. If you know the negative form, the passive uses the same base.

**Ru-verbs (ichidan):** Drop る, add られる
- 食べる → 食べ**られる** (to be eaten)
- 見る → 見**られる** (to be seen)
- 起きる → 起き**られる** (to be woken up)

**U-verbs (godan):** Replace final kana with あ-row equivalent, then add れる

| Verb | あ-row base | Passive |
|------|------------|---------|
| 書く | 書か | 書か**れる** |
| 話す | 話さ | 話さ**れる** |
| 歩く | 歩か | 歩か**れる** |
| 飲む | 飲ま | 飲ま**れる** |
| 打つ | 打た | 打た**れる** |
| 歌う | 歌わ | 歌わ**れる** |

**Irregular:**
- する → **される**
- 来る → **来られる**

The passive verb is a ru-verb and can be conjugated for tense, politeness, and negation normally:
- 食べられた (was eaten) / 食べられている (is being eaten) / 食べられません (is not eaten)

---

## Agent Particles in Passive

In a passive sentence, the original agent (the doer of the action) is optionally marked with a particle:

| Particle | When to use | Example |
|----------|------------|---------|
| **に** | Default agent marker | 先生**に**褒められた。(I was praised by my teacher.) |
| **から** | When に would be ambiguous (transfer verbs) | 友達**から**プレゼントをもらった。(Received a gift from a friend.) |
| **によって** | For creation, discovery, invention; prevents に being read as "for" | この曲は坂本龍一**によって**作られた。(This piece was composed by Ryūichi Sakamoto.) |

The によって rule matters because に has two roles: it can mark the agent ("by someone") or the beneficiary ("for someone"). With verbs of creation or formal attribution, この絵は田中さんに描かれた could read as "painted for Tanaka" rather than "painted by Tanaka." によって eliminates this ambiguity: この絵は田中さん**によって**描かれた = definitively "painted by Tanaka."

---

## Direct Passive — Structure

Active: 先生 が 学生 を 褒めた。(The teacher praised the student.)
Passive: 学生 が 先生 に 褒められた。(The student was praised by the teacher.)

The original object (学生 — student) becomes the grammatical subject marked with が. The agent (先生 — teacher) is marked with に.

More examples:
- この小説は村上春樹によって書かれた。(This novel was written by Haruki Murakami.)
- 私は上司に仕事を頼まれた。(I was asked to do work by my boss.)

---

## Adversative Passive — The Uniquely Japanese Construction

The **indirect / adversative passive** (迷惑の受け身 — meiwaku no ukemi, literally "inconvenience passive") describes a situation where the grammatical subject is negatively affected by an action, even if the action was not done directly to them.

Critically: the action can be intransitive. Someone can cry, and *you* are affected.

- 赤ちゃんに泣かれた。(The baby cried on me / I was troubled by the baby crying.)
- 電車の中で隣の人に歌われた。(The person next to me sang on the train — it bothered me.)
- 友達に来られて困った。(My friend came over and it was inconvenient for me.)
- 雨に降られた。(I was rained on / the rain fell and it affected me negatively.)

The structure shifts: the person affected becomes the subject; the "agent" of the troublesome action is marked with に; the verb takes passive form even though it may be intransitive.

**Why this matters culturally:**

The adversative passive reflects the Japanese concept of 迷惑 (meiwaku) — being a nuisance or causing inconvenience to others. Japanese is grammatically equipped to express "someone did something, and it created an inconvenience for me" in a single construction. This construction is common in Japanese speech and writing, and recognizing it is essential for understanding natural Japanese texts.

---

## Why This Matters

Passive voice is more common in formal written Japanese than in casual speech. でも (but) — adversative passive appears frequently in casual narration: complaining about a situation, explaining why something went wrong. Both types are essential to read, understand, and produce at N3 level.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Passive formation: ru-verbs → drop る + られる; u-verbs → あ-row + れる; する → される; 来る → 来られる
- Agent particles: に (default), から (transfer), によって (creation/attribution)
- Direct passive: direct object becomes subject; agent marked with に/によって
- Adversative/indirect passive: describes negative impact on the subject; intransitive verbs can be passivized; agent marked with に
- Adversative passive carries implicit nuance of inconvenience or disruption
