# Session 02: てしまう, ておく, てみる — Completion, Preparation, Trial
**Course:** JAPN-101
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to use てしまう to express completion or regret, ておく to express preparation, and てみる to express trying something out — and distinguish each from the others.

---

## Opening

You have a deadline tomorrow. You sit down to review your notes, but realize you have eaten the last of your snacks, accidentally left your notebook at work, and your coffee has gone cold. Now imagine expressing each of those in Japanese — not just the bare facts, but the emotional weight of each situation.

Japanese grammar has a construction for exactly this: **てしまう** (te-shimau). It says "this action reached its definitive, irreversible end — and there is something worth noting about that." It might be relief, regret, or just emphasis. English has no single equivalent, which is why learners who ignore it produce Japanese that sounds oddly flat.

---

## てしまう — The Weight of Completion

**てしまう** attaches to the te-form of a verb and adds one of two nuances:

1. **Thoroughness / completion:** The action was completed fully, leaving nothing undone (or nothing remaining).
   - 宿題を全部してしまった。I completely finished all my homework.
   - ケーキを全部食べてしまった。I ate all the cake.

2. **Regret / accident:** The action happened against the speaker's wishes, by accident, or with unfortunate consequences.
   - 財布を忘れてしまった。I forgot my wallet (dammit).
   - 窓ガラスを割ってしまった。I accidentally broke the window.

The common thread between these two meanings: **the action reached a definitive, irreversible endpoint**. Whether the completion is welcome or unwelcome is context-dependent — but something is now finished and cannot be undone.

A useful adverb: **つい** (tsui, "without thinking / inadvertently") pairs naturally with てしまう to emphasize accidentalness:
- つい食べてしまった。I ate it without thinking.

**Colloquial contractions** (extremely common in spoken Japanese):
- てしまう → **ちゃう** (for verbs whose te-form ends in て)
  - 食べてしまう → 食べちゃう
- でしまう → **じゃう** (for verbs whose te-form ends in で)
  - 飲んでしまう → 飲んじゃう
  - 読んでしまう → 読んじゃう

These contractions are normal and natural in casual conversation. You will hear them constantly in anime, everyday speech, and informal texting.

---

## ておく — Do It Now for Later

**ておく** (te-oku) expresses performing an action *in advance* as preparation for something that will happen later, or to prevent a future problem. The "oku" literally means "to put/set" — you are "setting" an action in place before it is needed.

- テストの前に復習をしておく。I'll review before the test (in preparation).
- パンを買っておいた。I bought bread in advance (for later).
- 電話番号を書いておいてください。Please write down the phone number (so you have it when needed).

**Colloquial contraction:** ておく → **とく**
- 買っておく → 買っとく (buy in advance)
- 書いておく → 書いとく

The key nuance: ておく focuses on the **act of preparing** — the action matters because of what comes later. Compare with てある from Session 01:
- ておく = "I will open the window in advance" (emphasis on the preparatory action)
- てある = "The window is open" (emphasis on the resulting state, agent implied)

These are siblings, not synonyms. ておく is used when the speaker is about to perform or has just performed the preparation. てある describes the prepared state already in existence.

---

## てみる — Try It and See

**てみる** (te-miru) attaches to the te-form and expresses trying something to discover the result — curiosity, experimentation, willingness to attempt. The みる literally means "to see," giving the construction a "do it and see" flavor.

- 寿司を食べてみた。I tried eating sushi (to see if I liked it).
- この薬を飲んでみてください。Please try taking this medicine (to see if it helps).
- 走ってみたが、あまり好きじゃなかった。I tried running, but didn't like it much.

**Critical distinction from English "try to":**

English "try to do X" implies possible failure — you might not succeed. **てみる does not imply difficulty or possible failure**. It implies willingness to attempt and observe the result.

Compare:
- 寿司を食べてみる = I'll try eating sushi (curious, experimental — I expect to manage)
- 寿司を食べようとする = I'm trying to eat sushi (implying it's somehow difficult — perhaps chopsticks are hard, or you're nervous)

Use てみる for experiments, new experiences, and things you are attempting with openness. Use ようとする for genuine attempts at things that might not succeed.

---

## Why This Matters

These three constructions are extremely high-frequency in natural Japanese conversation. Native speakers use てしまう constantly — omitting it often makes sentences sound emotionally flat or incomplete. ておく appears in everyday planning and instructions. てみる is essential for expressing the natural tentativeness of someone trying something new.

Together with ている and てある from Session 01, these five te-form extensions form a complete toolkit for expressing **how an action relates to time, intent, and emotion**. This is the grammar of a person with an inner life, not just a list of facts.

---

## Key Takeaways

- てしまう = the action reached a definitive endpoint (either emphasizing completion or expressing regret/accident)
- つい + てしまう emphasizes that something happened accidentally or without thinking
- Casual contractions: ちゃう (for て-verbs), じゃう (for で-verbs)
- ておく = perform an action in advance as preparation; contracts to とく in casual speech
- てみる = try doing something to see the result; does not imply difficulty, only curiosity or willingness
- てみる ≠ "try to" (ようとする handles genuine attempts that might fail)
