# Research: Potential Form and Noun Modification

**Course:** JAPN-101
**Level:** 101
**Date:** 2026-06-24
**Sources:** 7 (Tier 4: 0 | Tier 5: 7)

## Core Concepts

This research area covers three interlocking topics that together enable a learner to express ability, embed clauses inside noun phrases, and convert verbs into nouns — all essential for intermediate Japanese production.

1. **Potential form (〜られる/〜える):** Expresses ability or possibility ("can do")
2. **Relative clauses:** Modifying nouns with full verb phrases placed before the noun
3. **Nominalizers こと and の:** Converting verb phrases into noun phrases

These three topics are unified by a single underlying capability: embedding a predicate (verb or adjective phrase) inside a larger sentence. This embedding skill is what separates N5/N4-level output from N3-level fluency.

---

## Key Definitions

**Potential form:** A verb form expressing ability, circumstantial possibility, or an attribute of "-ability." Formed from the verb base. Can be expressed either through conjugation (見られる, 書ける) or through the construction ことができる.

**Relative clause:** In Japanese, a clause that directly modifies a noun by appearing immediately before it, with no relative pronoun needed. The modified noun is called the "head noun." Japanese relative clauses are pre-nominal (they come before the noun they modify), unlike English post-nominal clauses ("the book *that I read*").

**Nominalizer:** A word that converts a verb or adjective phrase into a noun phrase. Japanese has two: こと (abstract, formal, conceptual) and の (concrete, sensory, contextual). Both follow plain form verbs.

**Head noun:** The noun being modified by a relative clause. In Japanese, the head noun may have a gap — the role it plays within the embedded clause is understood from context, not marked grammatically.

## Process / Mechanics

### Potential Form Formation

**Ru-verbs (ichidan):** Replace る with られる
- 見る → 見られる (can see)
- 食べる → 食べられる (can eat)
- 起きる → 起きられる (can wake up)

**U-verbs (godan):** Replace final kana with the same-row え-sound + る

| Verb | Base | Potential |
|------|------|-----------|
| 書く | 書 | 書ける |
| 飲む | 飲 | 飲める |
| 話す | 話 | 話せる |
| 待つ | 待 | 待てる |
| 買う | 買 | 買える |
| 読む | 読 | 読める |

**Irregular:**
- する → できる (not される)
- 来る → 来られる

**ことができる alternative:**
All potential expressions can also be formed with `[verb plain form] + ことができる`
- 見られる = 見ることができる (can see)
- 書ける = 書くことができる (can write)

Nuance: ことができる is slightly more polite and formal; られる/える forms are more conversational. When discussing the specific verb できる (meaning "to be able to do" in the context of skill), が is the preferred object particle.

**Colloquial ら-drop:**
In modern spoken Japanese, many speakers — especially younger generations — drop the ら from ru-verb potential forms:
- 見られる → 見れる (colloquial)
- 食べられる → 食べれる (colloquial)
- 来られる → 来れる (colloquial)

This ら-drop is increasingly common but remains grammatically contested. In writing and formal speech, retain ら. On the JLPT exam, ら-drop forms may still be marked wrong.

### Three Functions of Potential Form

| Function | Description | Example |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Ability | Personal capacity to do X | フランス語が話せる (I can speak French) |
| Situation | Circumstantial possibility | 今日早く帰られる (I can go home early today) |
| Attribute | "-able/-ible" quality of a thing | この橋は渡られない (This bridge cannot be crossed) |

**Particle selection with potential:**

| Particle | Use case | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| を | Object of ability with conjugated potential forms | この問題を解ける (I can solve this problem) |
| が | Object with できる; also marks objects of potential for emphasis | 私は経理ができます (I can do accounting) |
| には | Marks who possesses/lacks the attribute; mainly in negatives | ボブにはこの漢字は読めません (Bob cannot read this kanji) |

**Transitive vs. intransitive confusion:**
- 見る (transitive, "to look at") → 見られる (potential: "can see")
- 見える (intransitive, "to be visible") — expresses automatic visibility, NOT ability
- 聞く (transitive, "to listen to") → 聞ける (potential: "can hear")
- 聞こえる (intransitive, "to be audible") — automatic audibility, NOT ability

Saying 見えますか instead of 見られますか changes meaning from "Can you see it?" to "Is it visible?"

### Japanese Relative Clauses

Japanese relative clauses directly precede the noun they modify, with no relative pronoun (no equivalent of "who," "that," "which"):

**Structure:** `[relative clause in plain form] + [head noun]`

Examples:
- 田中さんが旅行する北海道 (Hokkaido where Tanaka-san will travel)
- 北海道を旅行する田中さん (Tanaka-san who will travel in Hokkaido)
- 私が昨日読んだ本 (the book that I read yesterday)

The relative clause uses plain (non-polite) forms regardless of formality level:
- ❌ 田中さんが作ります ごはん (polite form inside relative clause — ungrammatical)
- ✅ 田中さんが作る ごはん (plain form — correct)

**Particle alternation inside relative clauses:**
The subject particle inside a relative clause can be either が or の:
- 田中さんが作るごはん = 田中さんの作るごはん (identical meaning)
This の is an archaic/literary subject particle still used inside embedded clauses.

**Topic particle は restriction:**
The topic marker は cannot appear inside relative clauses because topics are sentence-level phenomena:
- ❌ 田中さんは作るごはん (ungrammatical as a noun phrase)
- ✅ 田中さんが作るごはんは美味しい (は can follow the modified noun as the main clause topic)

**Head noun gap:**
The head noun plays a role inside the embedded clause that is not explicitly marked. Context determines the role:
- 私が読んだ本 (the book that I read — 本 is the object of 読む)
- 田中さんが住む町 (the town where Tanaka-san lives — 町 is the location)
- 私が使うペン (the pen that I use — ペン is the object)

### Nominalizers: こと and の

Both こと and の attach to the plain form of a verb or adjective to turn the predicate into a noun phrase.

**Formation:**
- Verbs: `[plain form verb] + こと/の`
- I-adjectives: `[i-adjective] + こと/の`
- Na-adjectives: `[na-adjective] + な + こと/の`
- Nouns (as predicates): `[noun] + である + こと` / `[noun] + な + の`

**Constraint:** Only plain form works. Polite forms cannot be nominalized:
- ✅ 食べること / 食べるの
- ❌ 食べますこと / 食べますの

### When こと and の are Interchangeable

In most contexts, both こと and の are freely substitutable:
- 日本語を話すことは楽しい = 日本語を話すのは楽しい (Speaking Japanese is fun)
- 早く寝ることが大切だ = 早く寝るのが大切だ (Sleeping early is important)

### When ONLY こと Works

1. **Before だ/です (predicate noun):**
   - 趣味は寝ること**だ** (My hobby is sleeping) ✅
   - ❌ 趣味は寝るの**だ** (unnatural)

2. **Set expressions:**
   - ことができる (can do)
   - ことがある (there are times when / have experienced)
   - ことにする (decide to)
   - ことになる (it has been decided)
   - These fixed expressions require こと — の cannot substitute.

3. **With communication and thought verbs (言う, 思う, 知る, 聞く [meaning "be told"]):**
   - 彼が来ることを知っている (I know that he is coming)
   - ✅ こと in indirect speech/thought clauses

### When ONLY の Works

1. **Perception verbs (see, hear):**
   - 父が話すのを聞く (I hear my father talking) ✅
   - ❌ 父が話すことを聞く

2. **With やめる (stop/quit):**
   - 勉強するのをやめた (I stopped studying) ✅

3. **With action-observation verbs (待つ, 手伝う) when directly perceiving or participating in a situation:**
   - 彼が帰るのを待った (I waited for him to come home)
   - 荷物を運ぶのを手伝った (I helped carry the luggage)

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Using ことができる with intransitive stative verbs:** できる already expresses ability; tautology results from applying ことができる to verbs that inherently express states.

2. **Confusing 見える/聞こえる with potential forms:**
   - 見えますか = "Is it visible?" (intransitive)
   - 見られますか = "Can you see it?" (potential)
   These are often confused in daily expressions like "Can you see the board?" (黒板が見えますか vs 見られますか — context-dependent).

3. **Polite forms inside relative clauses:** This is one of the most persistent errors at N4 level. Students produce 食べますもの instead of 食べるもの. The rule is absolute: relative clauses use plain form.

4. **Using は inside relative clauses:** Topic marker は belongs at the sentence level. Students attempt structures like 田中さんは来る日 (intended: the day when Tanaka-san comes), which is ungrammatical inside the embedded clause.

5. **Forcing こと where の is required:** Using こと with perception verbs (聞く, 見る in the perceptual sense) — ❌ 彼が歌うことを聞いた — should be の.

6. **Forgetting な before の/こと with na-adjectives:** ❌ 静かのがいい → ✅ 静かなのがいい

## Hands-On Elements

**Exercise 1 — Potential form drill:**
Conjugate these verbs to potential form (both conjugated and ことができる):
- 読む (read)
- 来る (come)
- 起きる (wake up)
- 話す (speak)

**Exercise 2 — Build relative clauses:**
Combine these elements into a single noun phrase:
- "the book I bought yesterday" (本, 買う, 昨日)
- "the restaurant where my friend works" (レストラン, 友達, 働く)
- "the movie I haven't seen yet" (映画, まだ, 見る, negative form)

**Exercise 3 — こと vs. の:**
Fill in the blank with こと or の (or either):
- 早く起きる\_\_が難しい。
- 彼女が歌う\_\_を聞いた。
- 趣味は映画を見る\_\_だ。
- 運動する\_\_ができません。

**Exercise 4 — Particle with potential:**
Choose を or が:
- 私はピアノ\_\_弾けます。(playing piano = skill)
- この問題\_\_一人で解けます。(solving this problem)
- 日本語\_\_できますか。(formal ability question)

**Exercise 5 — Translation challenge:**
- "I can hear someone singing outside." (Use the right verb — 聞こえる or 聞ける?)
- "I want to hear you sing." (聞く potential, with の)
- "The person who taught me Japanese is Ms. Tanaka."

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

**ら-drop: acceptable or not?**  
The ら-drop phenomenon (見れる for 見られる) is now so common that some linguists argue it should be treated as a separate accepted form rather than a colloquial error. JLPT scoring still penalizes it. Teachers must decide how to handle it: teach both forms? Teach ら-drop as "spoken" and standard as "written"?

**こと vs. の — the "vague" rule:**  
Wasabi Japanese explicitly acknowledges that the rules for when の is required (with observation/assistance verbs) involve a "vague explanation." The real-world rule appears to be about directly observed or experienced situations versus abstract/reported ones, but this line is difficult to state precisely and learners must develop intuition through exposure.

**Relative clause in Japanese as head-final typology:**  
Japanese is a head-final language — modifiers precede heads. Relative clauses are often taught as unusual for English speakers, but Japanese linguists note this is typologically the expected pattern for a head-final SOV language. Some researchers argue that understanding this typological context helps learners stop searching for a "missing" relative pronoun.
