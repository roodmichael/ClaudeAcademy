  Covers the full family of auxiliary verbs that attach to the te-form.   All four Japanese conditionals with formation rules, function tables, overlap zones, and prohibited contexts. Covers もし as conditional adverb. Includes practitioner guidance for learners: default to たら, add なら for contextual responses.

- **research-03.md** — Passive and Causative Constructions (〜られる/れる, 〜させる, 〜させられる)  
  Formation rules for all three forms across verb classes. Covers the four meanings of られる (passive, potential, spontaneous, respectful), the direct vs. indirect/adversative passive distinction, causative letting vs. making via particle choice, and the long/short forms of causative-passive. Includes Japanese linguistic/cultural context (迷惑).

- **research-04.md** — Potential Form and Noun Modification (〜られる/〜える, relative clauses, こと and の)  
  Potential form formation and its three functions. Full treatment of Japanese pre-nominal relative clauses: plain-form constraint, は restriction, が/の alternation, head noun gap. Nominalizers こと and の: when interchangeable, when each is required exclusively, formation with all word types.

- **research-05.md** — Connecting Clauses Naturally (から/ので, が/けど, のに, たり〜たりする, ながら)  
  The pragmatic layer of clause connection. から vs. ので formality and assertiveness distinction. Neutral contrast (が/けど formality ladder). Emotional contrast with のに: constraints, required な, prohibited volitional contexts. Non-exhaustive listing with たり〜たりする. Simultaneous actions with ながら: same-subject constraint, subordinate vs. main action.

## Key Themes

- **Aspect over tense:** Japanese grammar marks how actions unfold (ongoing, completed, resultant) rather than simply when they happened. ている, てしまう, てある, ておく all encode aspect. Learners who approach Japanese with an English tense-first mindset will consistently misuse these forms.

- **Perspective and agency:** A thread running through te-form giving/receiving (てあげる/てくれる/てもらう), passive/causative, and conditional choice (なら requires prior context) is that Japanese grammar encodes the speaker's position relative to events. Who controls the action? Who benefits? Who is imposing? Choosing the right form requires knowing the answer.

- **Plain form inside embedded clauses:** Relative clauses, nominalization, and conditional clauses all require plain form internally. This is one of the most reliable rules at N3 level and one of the most commonly violated by learners from polite-speech backgrounds.

- **Pragmatic register is part of the grammar:** から vs. ので, が vs. けど vs. のに, てあげる in social contexts — these are not stylistic preferences but grammatically-relevant choices that affect meaning and speaker intent. At N3, grammar cannot be separated from pragmatics.

- **Four meanings of られる:** The single form られる carries passive, potential, spontaneous, and respectful meanings. This ambiguity is a recurring challenge through N4, N3, and beyond. Students must develop disambiguation strategies based on context and particle cues.

## Source Quality Summary

All 38 sources across the five documents are Tier 5 (established practitioner sites with grammar expertise), drawn primarily from two high-quality sites:

- **Wasabi Japanese (wasabi-jpn.com):** 18 sources — structured grammar reference articles with formation tables, nuance explanations, and example sentences. Reliable for rule statements and contrast tables.
- **Bunpro (bunpro.jp):** 20 sources — grammar point database with JLPT level classification, example sentences, and community discussion threads. Reliable for formation rules and learner common-mistake data.

No Tier 1–3 sources were located for this material. The Japan Foundation and JLPT official sites provide JLPT vocabulary/kanji lists but do not publish grammar explanations. Tier 4 sources (Genki, A Dictionary of Japanese Grammar) were not fetched directly but align with the content found — these textbooks are recommended as Course Resources rather than Research sources.

## Coverage Gaps

- **No Tier 1–3 sources found** for any of the five areas. The JLPT organization (jlpt.jp) does not publish grammar explanations. Japan Foundation materials do not cover individual grammar point explanations at this level. Wasabi Japanese and Bunpro are the best available practitioner resources.

- **Academic linguistic treatment:** Linguistic literature exists on Japanese aspect (ている), conditionals, and relative clauses as typological features of SOV/head-final languages, but peer-reviewed sources were not fetched. Course Creator may wish to consult linguistics-oriented resources (e.g., Shibatani's *The Languages of Japan*, Makino & Tsutsui's *A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar*) for session design nuances. These are Tier 4 and not reflected in this research.

- **Authentic corpus data:** No frequency data was gathered on how often each construction appears in natural speech vs. formal writing. Future research could draw on the BCCWJ (Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese) or the Chunagon corpus interface for frequency-based prioritization.

- **Keigo (honorific) extensions:** てくれる/てあげる/てもらう each have formal keigo variants (てくださる, て差し上げる, ていただく). These are mentioned briefly in research-01 but not fully developed. A standalone session or supplementary note may be warranted, especially for learners with workplace Japanese goals.
