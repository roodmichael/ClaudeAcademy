## Core Concepts

The te-form is a conjunctive verb form in Japanese that, when combined with a following auxiliary verb, creates compound aspectual meanings. All te-form extensions share a single structural template: `Verb (te-form) + auxiliary verb`. The auxiliary verb carries the semantic weight; the te-form connects. Mastery of this pattern unlocks a large family of related constructions at once.

The six extensions studied in JAPN-101 break into two groups:

**Aspectual extensions** — ている, てしまう, ておく, てみる, てある  
**Action-benefactive extensions** — てあげる, てくれる, てもらう

---

## Key Definitions

**ている (te-iru):** Te-form + いる. Expresses either an ongoing action ("is doing") or a resultant state ("is in a state resulting from a completed action"), depending on the verb type.

**てしまう (te-shimau):** Te-form + しまう. Expresses (a) complete or thorough completion of an action, or (b) an unintentional, accidental, or regrettable action. Colloquial contractions: ちゃう (from て verbs), じゃう (from で verbs).

**ておく (te-oku):** Te-form + おく. Expresses an action done in advance for future benefit or as preparation. Emphasizes the preparatory act itself.

**てみる (te-miru):** Te-form + みる. Expresses trying something out to see the result; experimenting. The みる is rarely written in kanji.

**てある (te-aru):** Te-form + ある (transitive verbs only). Expresses a resultant state that arose from a deliberate human action. Contrasts with intransitive + ている, which describes naturally occurring states.

**てあげる (te-ageru):** Te-form + あげる. Doing something for the benefit of someone outside the speaker's in-group. Historical root: やる (give to lower/plants/animals), hence still carries a mild patronizing undertone.

**てくれる (te-kureru):** Te-form + くれる. Someone outside the speaker's in-group does something benefiting the speaker (or in-group). Focuses on the giver and their generosity.

**てもらう (te-morau):** Te-form + もらう. The speaker receives an action from someone. Focuses on the receiver obtaining a benefit; slightly more formal and direct than てくれる.

## Process / Mechanics

### ている Formation and Usage

Formation: `[verb te-form] + いる/います`

The meaning depends on the verb's inherent aspect:

| Verb Type | ている Meaning | Example |
|-----------|---------------|---------|
| Action verb (durative) | Ongoing: "is doing X" | 食べている = is eating |
| Motion verb | Resultant state: "went and is there" | 学校に行っている = went to school (and is there) |
| Change-of-state verb (instantaneous) | Resultant state: "is in the state of having done X" | 結婚している = is married |
| Stative verb | NOT used with ている | ❌ 水が要っている (wrong) |

**Heuristic:** If the action cannot logically happen twice in a row, it is an instantaneous change-of-state verb and ている will describe the resulting state. "You cannot get married twice in a row," so 結婚している = "is married" (state), not "is getting married" (action).

**Colloquial contraction:** ている → てる; ています → てます (common in spoken Japanese, acceptable in informal writing)

### てしまう Formation and Nuances

Formation: `[verb te-form] + しまう/しまいます`

Two distinct but connected meanings:
1. **Completion/thoroughness:** 弟は菓子を食べてしまった (My younger brother ate all the candy — completely gone)
2. **Regret/accident:** 私は窓ガラスを割ってしまった (I accidentally broke the window — unfortunate)

The common thread: both express an outcome that has become irreversible or that reached a definitive endpoint.

Colloquial contractions (spoken only):
- てしまう → ちゃう (verbs whose te-form ends in て)
- でしまう → じゃう (verbs whose te-form ends in で)
- 泣いてしまう → 泣いちゃう; 飲んでしまう → 飲んじゃう

Common pairing: つい + てしまう intensifies accidental nuance: つい食べてしまった (I ate it without thinking)

### ておく Formation and Nuances

Formation: `[verb te-form] + おく/おきます`

Colloquial contraction: ておく → とく (common in casual speech)

The action is performed now to prepare for something later, or to prevent a future problem.

Examples:
- テストの前に復習をしておく (I'll review before the test — in preparation)
- パンを買っておいた (I bought bread in advance — for later use)

Distinction from てある:
- ておく = emphasizes the preparatory act itself (action-focused)
- てある = emphasizes the resulting state (state-focused); the act is implied but not foregrounded

### てみる Formation

Formation: `[verb te-form] + みる/みます`

Meaning: "try doing X (to see what happens / what the result is)"

The みる literally means "to see" — the nuance is "do it and see." This differs from the English "try to do" (which implies possible failure). てみる implies willingness to attempt and observe the outcome.

Examples:
- 寿司を食べてみた (I tried eating sushi — to see if I liked it)
- 走ってみた (I tried running — to experience it)
- この薬を飲んでみてください (Please try taking this medicine — see if it helps)

### てある Formation

Formation: `[transitive verb te-form] + ある`

Particle behavior:
- **が** particle: describes the state from a neutral/third-party perspective — 窓が開けてある (The window is kept open)
- **を** particle: describes the state with the doer as subject, foregrounding preparatory intent — 窓を開けてある (I've opened the window [for a reason])

Compare with intransitive + ている:
- 窓が開いている (The window is open — no agent implied; it just is open)
- 窓が開けてある (The window has been opened — someone deliberately opened it)

Only transitive verbs work with てある.

### てあげる / てくれる / てもらう

All three involve performing an action as a favor. The grammatical subject and perspective differ:

| Form | Actor | Recipient | Perspective | Particle for recipient |
|------|-------|-----------|-------------|------------------------|
| てあげる | Speaker/in-group | Out-group person | Giver | に |
| てくれる | Out-group person | Speaker/in-group | Giver | — (implicit: speaker) |
| てもらう | Out-group person | Speaker/in-group | Receiver | に (marks the actor) |

**てあげる example:** 母に花を買ってあげた (I bought flowers for my mother)
**てくれる example:** ケンが日本語を教えてくれた (Ken taught me Japanese [as a favor to me])
**てもらう example:** ケンに日本語を教えてもらった (I had Ken teach me Japanese [I received his teaching])

**Formal variants:**
- てあげる → て差し上げる (highly formal; speaker is clearly subordinate)
- てくれる → てくださる (respectful)
- てもらう → ていただく (humble/polite)

Negative request forms: てくれない/てもらえない function as polite requests ("Won't you do X for me?")

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **ている with stative verbs:** Using ている with verbs like 要る (need), 分かる (understand), or ある (exist) is incorrect. These verbs are already stative and do not combine with ている. ❌ 分かっている (as "I understand") → ✅ 分かる

2. **Confusing てある and てある (transitive only):** Attempting てある with intransitive verbs fails grammatically. Only transitive verbs create meaningful てある constructions.

3. **てみる as "trying despite difficulty":** English "try to do" implies possible failure. てみる does not — it means trying out of curiosity or to test. For attempting something difficult, use ようとする.

4. **てしまう as always negative:** While regret/accident are common, てしまう can also be positive-completion: 宿題を全部してしまった (I completely finished all my homework — positive relief).

5. **てあげる toward superiors:** This sounds patronizing when directed at people of higher status. Use て差し上げる or rephrase entirely.

6. **てくれる vs. てもらう confusion:** Both translate as "someone did X for me," but the grammatical subject differs. てくれる: the giver is the subject. てもらう: the receiver (speaker) is the subject.

7. **Failing to contract ていた in speech:** In spoken Japanese, てた (from ていた) is extremely common. Formal written Japanese retains ていた. Students sometimes sound unnatural by always using the full form in casual contexts.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

**ていた as habitual past vs. past progressive:**  
ていた is used for past ongoing actions (was doing) but also for past habits (used to do). The distinction relies entirely on context, which confuses learners. Some grammarians argue Japanese aspect differs fundamentally from English aspect and direct mapping produces errors.

**てある particle ambiguity (が vs. を):**  
There is some debate among textbook authors about when が versus を is natural with てある. Wasabi Japanese notes the distinction is about perspective (state vs. agent-foregrounding) but some learners find this unstable in native speech.

**The patronizing dimension of てあげる:**  
Some teachers advise avoiding てあげる toward anyone older than you or outside your very close circle. Others note this concern is often overstated and context matters. Students should understand the risk but not become paralyzed by avoidance.
