# Final Project — JAPN-101
**Covers:** All 11 Sessions

## Overview

The final project for JAPN-101 is a written composition with self-analysis. You will write two short paragraphs in Japanese on a topic of your choice, then walk through your own writing explaining every grammar pattern you used and why you chose it. The goal is not length or vocabulary range — it is demonstrating that you know what you are doing and why.

This is the difference between grammar as rules you memorize and grammar as tools you choose.

---

## The Task

### Part 1: Written Composition

Write **two paragraphs** in Japanese. Combined length: approximately 150-250 characters (日本語の文字). Quality over quantity.

**Choose your topic from one of these options, or propose your own:**

- Option A: Describe a process you know well — brewing, cooking, roasting, fermentation, building something. Focus on what happens and what it requires.
- Option B: Describe a challenging experience — a project that went wrong, a situation where you had to do something you didn't want to, something that surprised you.
- Option C: Describe a person who influenced you — what they did for you, what you learned, how things might be different without them.
- Option D: Describe a comparison — two approaches, two places, two ways of doing the same thing. What are the tradeoffs?

Michael's suggested topics given his background: homebrewing a difficult batch (Option A/B), a demanding management situation (Option B/C), comparing brewing styles or roast profiles (Option D). But any topic that generates rich sentences is welcome.

**There is no grammar checklist to force in artificially.** Write naturally. Good Japanese will use these patterns organically. However, the self-analysis in Part 2 should show you *did* use them — if your composition has no passive voice and no conditionals at all, that is a signal to push yourself to write something more complex.

---

### Part 2: Grammar Self-Analysis

For every non-trivial grammar pattern in your composition, write a brief annotation explaining:
1. What the pattern is
2. Why you chose it here (not a different form)
3. Whether you considered any alternatives and why you rejected them

**What counts as "non-trivial":** All te-form extensions, all conditional forms, all passive/causative constructions, nominalizers, relative clauses, and clause connectors from this course. Basic verb conjugations, simple particles, and masu/desu endings do not need annotation.

**Format:** Quote the relevant phrase or sentence, identify the pattern, explain your choice.

Example:
> 「昨日上司に残業させられた」— Causative-passive (させられる). I chose this over plain passive because the nuance is *compulsion* — I didn't choose to stay late; my boss made me. Regular passive (残業を頼まれた) would only say I was asked. Causative-passive makes the forced nature explicit.

---

### Part 3: One Reflection Question

Answer in English (3-5 sentences):

> Which grammar pattern from this course changed how you read Japanese — not how you produce it, but how you understand what you are already encountering? Why?

There is no wrong answer. This question is about your developing awareness as a reader and learner.

## Evaluation Criteria

- **Compositional quality:** Does the Japanese flow naturally? Do sentences connect with appropriate connectors rather than just stopping?
- **Grammar accuracy:** Are the N3 patterns used correctly? This is weighted heavily.
- **Range of patterns:** Does the composition demonstrate multiple pattern types from the course — not just the easiest ones?
- **Self-analysis depth:** Do the annotations show genuine understanding of *why* each form was chosen, or are they rote name-dropping?
- **Register consistency:** Does the politeness level stay consistent throughout?

## Tutor Scoring Guide

**Strong final projects will:**
- Write compositions where grammar patterns arise naturally from the topic rather than being forced in. A homebrewing description, for instance, organically generates: ておく (prepared the yeast in advance), てしまう (accidentally let temperature drop), passive (the grain is crushed/mashed), conditional (if fermentation stalls, you should check the yeast), relative clause (the yeast I pitched last week), ながら (monitoring while doing something else).
- Show Part 2 annotations that go beyond naming — the student explains the choice between competing options
- Address near-misses: "I considered のに here but chose けど because I'm not expressing frustration — it's just a neutral observation"
- Demonstrate awareness of register: "I used ので rather than から because I'm explaining this politely"

**Common weaknesses:**
- Compositions that are too simple — only the bare minimum of grammar, nothing that pushes into the passive/causative territory
- Annotations that just name patterns without explaining the choice ("I used てしまう here")
- Register inconsistencies — mixing very casual and very formal in the same passage without apparent reason
- Missing relative clauses entirely — encourage students to revise any sentence with "the X that" into a proper pre-nominal modifier

**Feedback approach:**
- Lead with what the composition does well before identifying errors
- For any misused pattern: name it, show the error, give the corrected form, explain the rule in one sentence
- Celebrate any composition that uses adversative passive or causative-passive correctly — these are the hallmark of genuine N3 competence
- The reflection question (Part 3) should receive a substantive response, not just "Good observation!" — engage with what they said
