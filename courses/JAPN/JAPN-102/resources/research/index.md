## Documents

- **research-01.md** — N4-to-N3 Vocabulary Acquisition Strategy  
  Covers the scope of the N4→N3 vocabulary jump (~2,200 new words, total ~3,750), the abstraction shift from concrete to conceptual vocabulary, thematic categories at N3 (18+ domains), spaced repetition methodology, I+1 sentence cards, thematic grouping strategy, the register dimension of N3 vocabulary, and key near-synonym pairs (理由/原因, 経験/体験). Includes hands-on acquisition exercises.

- **research-02.md** — Kanji Expansion from N4 (~300) to N3 (~650)  
  Covers the ~370 new N3 kanji (cumulative total ~650), the radical-first mnemonic learning method (Tofugu), jukugo combinatorial unlocking (how knowing 経 and 済 unlocks 経済, 経験, 決済), distinguishing visually similar kanji, stroke order principles, and the argument against treating kanji and vocabulary as separate study tracks. Includes a sample table of key N3 kanji with readings.

- **research-03.md** — On'yomi and Kun'yomi Patterns  
  Covers the historical origins of the two reading systems (three waves of Chinese importation), the four rules for predicting readings (compound=on'yomi, standalone=kun'yomi, okurigana=kun'yomi, names=unpredictable), exceptions (kun'yomi compounds, on'yomi-only kanji, kokuji), the dictionary convention (katakana for on'yomi, hiragana for kun'yomi), rendaku (voiced variants in compounds), and the pedagogical strategy of learning one reading per kanji then acquiring others through vocabulary.

- **research-04.md** — Vocabulary in Context: Collocations, Near-Synonyms, and Nuance  
  Covers the volitional/non-volitional verb split (見る/見える, 聞く/聞こえる) with particle implications (を vs. が), the visiting/inquiring triple (訪ねる/訪れる/尋ねる) with formality register ladder, speaking verbs (言う/話す/述べる), getting verbs (受ける/得る/手に入れる), the full transitive/intransitive verb pair table with 10 key pairs, adjective register pairs (すごい/素晴らしい, 美しい/綺麗な), noun register pairs, and the experience/reason pairs. Includes academic collocation research (Frontiers in Psychology 2023) and implications for teaching.

- **research-05.md** — Reading Comprehension with Authentic Texts at N3 Level  
  Covers the four N3 reading passage types (short/medium/long/information retrieval), passage lengths, JLPT official reading competencies for N3, key discourse connectives organized by logical function (contrast, causation, addition, summary, sequence), the skim-scan-dive reading strategy, authentic text sources (NHK Web Easy in depth, Shin Kanzen Master N3, graded readers), question-type-specific strategies (main idea, referential expressions, trap answers), handling unknown words, and the time management 3-minute rule.

## Key Themes

- **Vocabulary and kanji are inseparable at N3.** The majority of N3 vocabulary consists of two-kanji compounds (jukugo) read in on'yomi. A student who separates kanji study from vocabulary study is doing double work and missing the combinatorial power of the system.

- **Abstraction is the defining challenge of the N4→N3 jump.** N4 vocabulary is largely concrete and situational. N3 vocabulary introduces abstract nouns (影響, 可能性, 原因), formal compound verbs (利用する, 訪問する, 述べる), and register distinctions (友達 vs. 友人) that require contextual judgment, not just memorization.

- **Near-synonyms require paired teaching, not sequential teaching.** The volitional/non-volitional distinction (見る vs. 見える, 聞く vs. 聞こえる) is systematically important and must be taught as a pair — with contrasting sentences and particle implications — not as two separate vocabulary items months apart.

- **On'yomi/kun'yomi is a prediction system, not a memorization list.** The four rules (compound=on, standalone=kun, okurigana=kun, names=unpredictable) handle the majority of cases. Teaching the rules as a navigation tool reduces per-kanji memorization burden significantly.

- **Reading comprehension at N3 is a time-management skill as much as a language skill.** Students who can comprehend N3 text in isolation but run out of time during the exam are failing on strategy, not on Japanese ability. The skim-scan-dive method, question-first reading, and the 3-minute-per-passage rule are teachable exam techniques that must be practiced explicitly.

## Coverage Gaps

- **No Tier 1 source for vocabulary counts or kanji lists.** The JLPT official documentation explicitly does not publish vocabulary or kanji lists by level. The commonly cited figures (~3,750 words, ~370 N3 kanji) are community-derived and consistent across multiple practitioner sources. This is an acknowledged limitation of all JLPT-level vocabulary/kanji research — there is no official list to cross-check against.

- **No Tier 1 or Tier 2 source for on'yomi/kun'yomi pedagogy.** This topic is not addressed in academic linguistics in a form accessible through web sources. Tofugu (Tier 5) is the highest-quality practitioner source for this area and is widely respected in the Japanese learning community. Academic treatments exist in linguistics texts (Shibatani's *The Languages of Japan*, Tsujimura's *An Introduction to Japanese Linguistics*) but are not web-fetchable.

- **Collocation data is thin.** The Frontiers in Psychology study covers collocation processing mechanisms but does not provide a curated list of N3-level collocations. A dedicated N3 collocation reference (such as a corpus-derived frequency list of N3 verb-noun collocations) would improve this research. The Japanese-Language Proficiency Test vocabulary databases on bunpro.jp include example sentences that partially fill this gap and are recommended for course development.

- **No N3-level frequency corpus data.** The BCCWJ (Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese) and the Chunagon interface at the National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics would provide authoritative frequency data for N3 vocabulary prioritization. These require institutional access and were not consulted.

- **Stroke order coverage is thin.** Research-02 covers stroke order principles but does not include a stroke order reference for specific N3 kanji. Course Creator may wish to link to jisho.org's stroke order animations (available for every kanji) or the KanjiVG project as a course resource.
