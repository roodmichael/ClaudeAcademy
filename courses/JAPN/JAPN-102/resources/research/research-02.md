## Core Concepts

### The Numbers

The JLPT N3 level requires mastery of approximately 370 new kanji above the N4 level, for a cumulative total of roughly 650 kanji (80 at N5, 170 at N4, 370 at N3) [nihongo-career.com, jlptsensei.com]. All kanji from N5 and N4 remain in scope at N3 — the exam builds cumulatively. Study time required for the N3 kanji set is estimated at 200–400 hours depending on prior character knowledge (Chinese character background: ~200–250 hours; no prior character experience: 350–400 hours).

There is no single officially published kanji list from JLPT. All "N3 kanji lists" in circulation are community-derived from analysis of past exams, textbooks, and expert consensus. Jisho.org uses Jonathan Waller's JLPT Resources list, which is the most widely used community standard.

### What N3 Kanji Look Like

N3 kanji introduce more complex characters with higher stroke counts, more semantic abstraction, and a greater proportion of characters that exist primarily in compound form (jukugo) rather than standalone use. Sample N3 kanji from jlptsensei.com's list:

| Kanji | On'yomi | Kun'yomi | Core Meaning |
|-------|---------|---------|-------------|
| 政 | SEI | matsurigoto | politics, government |
| 議 | GI | — | deliberation, debate |
| 民 | MIN | tami | people, nation |
| 連 | REN | tsu(reru) | connect, join |
| 対 | TAI, TSUI | — | compare, versus |
| 決 | KETSU | ki(meru) | decide, fix |
| 最 | SAI | motto(mo) | utmost, most |
| 現 | GEN | arawa(reru) | present, actual |
| 経 | KEI | he(ru) | pass through, expire |
| 進 | SHIN | susu(mu) | advance, progress |
| 支 | SHI | sasa(eru) | support, branch |
| 向 | KOU | mu(ku) | face, direction |
| 可 | KA | — | possible, can |
| 様 | YOU | sama | appearance, manner |

Thematic clusters at N3 include: governance and civic vocabulary (政, 議, 権, 民, 府), economic/business vocabulary (経, 済, 利, 益, 費), emotional and relational vocabulary (感, 信, 愛, 望, 悩), and scientific/environmental vocabulary (温, 熱, 化, 核, 型).

### Jukugo: How Compound Kanji Unlock Vocabulary

The power of the N3 kanji set lies in its combinatorial nature. Once you know 政 (politics/government) and 治 (govern), you can parse:
- 政治 (seiji) — politics
- 行政 (gyōsei) — administration
- 政府 (seifu) — government
- 自治 (jichi) — self-governance

Once you know 経 (pass through/expire) and 済 (settle/complete):
- 経済 (keizai) — economy
- 経験 (keiken) — experience
- 経路 (keiro) — route
- 決済 (kessai) — payment/settlement

This combinatorial unlocking is the central pedagogical argument for learning kanji systematically rather than treating each vocabulary word as a new memorization task. A learner who knows 50 N3 kanji well can recognize ~200 N3 vocabulary words through meaning-inference alone [nihongo-career.com].

## Key Definitions

**Bushu (部首):** The radical of a kanji — a smaller component stroke-pattern used to classify kanji in dictionaries and to hint at meaning. There are 214 official bushu. Approximately 51 are highly frequent and most relevant for JLPT study [unseen-japan.com].

**Okurigana (送り仮名):** The hiragana suffix attached to a kanji in a verb or adjective form. 食べ*る*, 高*い*, 早*く*. Okurigana is the reliable signal that the kanji is being read in kun'yomi and is functioning as a verb or adjective.

**Stroke order (筆順, hitsujun):** The conventional sequence for writing each kanji stroke. Stroke order follows consistent principles: top-to-bottom, left-to-right, horizontal before vertical in many cases. Following correct stroke order produces natural, recognizable handwriting and aids memory by reinforcing the logical structure of the character.

**Mnemonic (記憶術):** A memory device that associates kanji components with a story or image. The mnemonic approach treats radicals as building blocks and constructs a memorable narrative connecting components to the kanji's meaning and reading.

**Kanji damage / WaniKani approach:** Two popular spaced repetition kanji systems. WaniKani teaches on'yomi with the kanji itself, then introduces kun'yomi through vocabulary words. Kanji Damage uses humorous mnemonic sentences. Both are Tier 5 practitioner resources.

## Process / Mechanics

### Radical-First Learning Method

Tofugu's "radical mnemonic method" provides the most systematic approach for N3 [tofugu.com/japanese/kanji-radicals-mnemonic-method]:

**Step 1: Learn radicals as named building blocks.**
- 田 = rice paddy
- 雨 = rain
- 口 = mouth
- 言 = speech
- 手 = hand
- 木 = tree
- 日 = sun
- 水 = water

**Step 2: Build kanji from radicals using a mnemonic story.**
- 町 (town) = 田 (rice paddy) + 丁 (street) → "Rice paddies connected by streets form a town"
- 電 (electricity) = 雨 (rain) + 田 (rice paddy) + 乚 (hook) → the story connects rain and rice paddy to electricity

**Step 3: Encode one reading per kanji** — the reading that appears in 80–90% of uses. Don't memorize all readings simultaneously.

**Step 4: Learn remaining readings through vocabulary.** When you encounter 生 in a new compound (先生, 学生, 人生), the context teaches you the reading used there.

**Step 5: Transition through SRS.** As mnemonics become unnecessary (the kanji is automatic), the SRS schedules reviews at increasing intervals. The mnemonic scaffolding can be dropped once recognition is automatic.

### Distinguishing Similar Kanji

N3 introduces more characters that are visually similar to N4 characters, creating confusion:
- 待 (wait, 待つ) vs. 持 (hold, 持つ) vs. 時 (time) — the left radical differentiates: 彳 (stop), 扌 (hand), 日 (sun)
- 見 (see) vs. 貝 (shellfish) — subtle difference in the bottom component
- 己 vs. 已 vs. 巳 — one of the classic confusion triples

The unseen-japan.com approach: learn the distinguishing radical, not just the whole character. "Know what these radicals represent, you can usually guess a kanji's general meaning" and distinguish near-identical characters.

### Stroke Order Principles (for N3 kanji)

While JLPT does not test stroke order directly, correct stroke order supports memory. Core rules:
1. Top before bottom
2. Left before right
3. Horizontal before vertical (in most cases: 土 → 一, 十, not reversed)
4. Outside before inside (口: left stroke, top, right → then horizontal bottom)
5. Center before sides (小: center stroke, then left, then right)

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Trying to memorize both readings at once.** A kanji like 生 has 10+ readings (せい, しょう, い(きる), う(まれる), お(う), なま, き, etc.). Learners who try to memorize all readings simultaneously hit interference and retain none. The correct strategy: learn one reading with the kanji, encounter others through vocabulary.

2. **Treating kanji and vocabulary as separate studies.** N3 students who study kanji in isolation (stroke order, meanings, all readings) and vocabulary separately in romaji or hiragana are doing double work. The efficient path is learning vocabulary items written in kanji — you learn both simultaneously.

3. **Skipping recognition to focus on writing.** JLPT tests recognition, not production. Students who spend most of their kanji time practicing writing are misallocating effort. Reading recognition should be primary; writing practice is a secondary bonus.

4. **Ignoring the combinatorial power of kanji.** Students who treat each new vocabulary item as an unrelated new thing to memorize are working far harder than necessary. Recognizing that 経 appears in 経済, 経験, 経路, and 経歴 allows one kanji to unlock four words.

5. **Not reviewing N4 and N5 kanji actively.** N3 kanji appear in compounds that include N4 and N5 characters. A student who has let N4 kanji atrophy cannot read N3 jukugo compounds with those characters.

## Hands-On Elements

- **Radical decomposition exercise:** Give students 20 N3 kanji they don't know yet. Ask them to identify the radical, name it, and write a one-sentence story connecting the radical to the kanji's meaning. Compare stories.
- **Jukugo web exercise:** Take one N3 kanji (e.g., 経) and use jisho.org to find 10 compound words containing it. Sort by frequency. Notice how the meaning extends across compounds.
- **Kanji confusion trios:** Present groups of similar-looking kanji (待/持/時, 見/貝, 己/已/巳). Students must identify the radical that distinguishes each and write one example word using each.
- **Stroke order tracing:** Practice writing 5 new N3 kanji with correct stroke order using grid paper. Focus on characters with complex radical combinations (議, 職, 環).
- **Cumulative kanji recall sprint:** Students write from memory as many N3 kanji as they can in 10 minutes, then check against the list. Tracks progress session-to-session.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

**Kanji-first vs. vocabulary-first:** Some practitioners (WaniKani, Tofugu) advocate learning kanji in isolation before vocabulary. Others (Krashen-influenced immersion learners) argue vocabulary in context naturally builds kanji recognition without deliberate kanji study. The N3 level is where both approaches converge — by N3, most learners find some structured kanji work necessary because the character set has become too large for purely incidental learning.

**Which list is authoritative?** JLPT does not publish an official kanji list. The most widely trusted community-derived lists (Tanos, Jonathan Waller, JLPTSensei) show some variation in exactly which ~370 characters are "N3." Course materials should select a specific list and be transparent about which one.

**Stroke order for digital-native learners:** Some practitioners argue that as typing replaces handwriting, stroke order study is a poor return on investment. Others note that stroke order study reinforces memory and pattern recognition even for recognition-only goals. For a course targeting exam preparation and real literacy, a moderate position is appropriate: teach stroke order principles rather than mandating production mastery.

## Connections to Adjacent Topics

- **Research-01 (Vocabulary acquisition):** Kanji knowledge and vocabulary knowledge are inseparable at N3. Compound kanji are the medium through which most new N3 vocabulary is learned.
- **Research-03 (On'yomi/Kun'yomi):** Understanding which reading to use in a jukugo compound is the primary application of the on'yomi/kun'yomi system.
- **Research-05 (Reading comprehension):** Kanji recognition is the bottleneck for N3 reading speed. Learners who cannot recognize N3 kanji automatically must sound out or skip words, destroying reading fluency.
