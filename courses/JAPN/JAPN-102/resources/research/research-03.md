## Core Concepts

### Origins: Why Kanji Have Two Kinds of Readings

On'yomi and kun'yomi represent two distinct historical layers of Japanese:

**On'yomi (音読み)** — Chinese-derived pronunciations. Kanji arrived in Japan from China (via Korea) primarily through religious and administrative texts, beginning around the 4th–6th centuries CE. Japanese scholars adopted the Chinese pronunciation of each character alongside its meaning. Because Chinese was imported in waves over centuries, many kanji have multiple on'yomi readings reflecting different eras of Chinese:

- **Go-on (呉音):** 4th–6th century, from the Wu region (Southeastern China)
- **Kan-on (漢音):** 7th–9th century, during the Tang Dynasty — the majority of modern on'yomi readings
- **Tō-on (唐音):** 1185–1573, later medieval Chinese — the closest to modern Mandarin

Example — the kanji 明 has three on'yomi: めい (Kan-on), みょう (Go-on), みん (Tō-on). Most learners encounter めい in compounds (明日/あした is an exception) [tofugu.com].

**Kun'yomi (訓読み)** — Native Japanese readings. Before kanji arrived, Japanese already had a spoken language with words for concepts like "fire," "mountain," "person." When kanji were adopted, Japanese writers mapped native words onto characters whose *meaning* matched, even if the pronunciation was unrelated. So 火 acquired not only the Chinese reading か (KA) but also the native Japanese word ひ (hi) [tofugu.com].

**The practical result:** Most kanji have at least one on'yomi and one kun'yomi. Some have several of each. This appears daunting, but the distribution is highly systematic.

### The Four Rules: When Each Reading Applies

These four rules handle the vast majority of cases. They are not absolute — exceptions exist — but they are reliable enough to be the foundation of any reading strategy [migaku.com, tofugu.com]:

**Rule 1: Two or more kanji with no hiragana between them → On'yomi**

This is the single most important rule. When kanji are combined into a compound word (jukugo) with no hiragana in between, they almost universally use their on'yomi readings.

Examples:
- 東京 (とうきょう, Tokyo) — 東 = TOU, 京 = KYOU
- 電話 (でんわ, telephone) — 電 = DEN, 話 = WA
- 先生 (せんせい, teacher) — 先 = SEN, 生 = SEI
- 地下鉄 (ちかてつ, subway) — 地 = CHI, 下 = KA, 鉄 = TETSU
- 科学 (かがく, science) — 科 = KA, 学 = GAKU
- 火山 (かざん, volcano) — 火 = KA, 山 = ZAN

**Rule 2: Single kanji standing alone → Kun'yomi**

When a kanji appears by itself as a complete standalone word (with no okurigana), it usually takes its kun'yomi reading.

Examples:
- 火 (ひ, fire) — not KA
- 山 (やま, mountain) — not ZAN or SAN
- 人 (ひと, person) — not JIN or NIN
- 目 (め, eye) — not MOKU

**Rule 3: Kanji + okurigana (hiragana suffix) → Kun'yomi**

When a kanji is followed by attached hiragana (a verb ending or adjective ending), the kanji uses its kun'yomi reading.

Examples:
- 食べる (たべる, to eat) — 食 = ta(beru), kun'yomi
- 話す (はなす, to speak) — 話 = hana(su), kun'yomi
- 新しい (あたらしい, new) — 新 = atara(shii), kun'yomi
- 大きい (おおきい, big) — 大 = oo(kii), kun'yomi
- 見る (みる, to see) — 見 = mi(ru), kun'yomi

**Rule 4: Names and places → Unpredictable (nanori readings exist)**

Japanese names use a third category of readings called *nanori* (名乗り) that don't follow on'yomi or kun'yomi rules. 鈴木 (Suzuki), 田中 (Tanaka), 東京 (Tōkyō but also used in unique name pronunciation). Furigana is the only reliable guide for names.

### The Exceptions (and Why They Don't Break the Rules)

**Kun'yomi compounds:** Some compound words use kun'yomi for both kanji, usually when the words are more concrete, nature-related, or directional:
- 月見 (つきみ, moon-viewing) — 月 = tsuki, 見 = mi
- 南口 (みなみぐち, south exit) — 南 = minami, 口 = kuchi
- 手紙 (てがみ, letter) — 手 = te, 紙 = gami (voiced)

**Mixed readings (重箱読み, 湯桶読み):** A small category of compounds uses one on'yomi and one kun'yomi:
- 台所 (だいどころ, kitchen) — 台 = DAI (on'yomi), 所 = dokoro (kun'yomi)
- 組合 (くみあい, union/association) — 組 = kumi (kun'yomi), 合 = AI (on'yomi)

These are minority cases but memorably named: 重箱読み (on-kun compound) and 湯桶読み (kun-on compound).

**On'yomi-only kanji:** Concepts that had no native Japanese equivalent — medical, religious, imported food — often have only on'yomi:
- 肉 (NIKU, meat) — no kun'yomi
- 茶 (CHA, tea) — no kun'yomi
- 医 (I, medicine/doctor) — no kun'yomi

**Kun'yomi-only kanji (kokuji, 国字):** Characters created in Japan that represent native Japanese concepts have only kun'yomi:
- 畑 (はたけ, field/farm) — Japanese-created character
- 姫 (ひめ, princess) — Japanese-created character
- 峠 (とうげ, mountain pass) — Japanese-created character

## Key Definitions

**On'yomi (音読み):** The Sinitic (Chinese-derived) reading of a kanji. Usually used in compound words (jukugo). Conventionally listed in katakana in dictionaries (KA, SEN, MOKU).

**Kun'yomi (訓読み):** The native Japanese reading of a kanji. Usually used for single kanji or kanji with okurigana. Conventionally listed in hiragana in dictionaries (たべ, みず, やま).

**Jukugo (熟語):** A compound word made of two or more kanji, almost always read in on'yomi. The dominant word-formation mechanism in formal Japanese and the primary vessel for N3+ vocabulary.

**Okurigana (送り仮名):** Hiragana attached to a kanji in verbs and adjectives. The key visual signal that kun'yomi applies.

**Nanori (名乗り):** A third category of readings used exclusively in proper names. Not covered in standard on'yomi/kun'yomi pedagogy; learners should defer to furigana.

**Rendaku (連濁):** A phonological process in which the initial consonant of the second element of a compound softens: 手 (te) + 紙 (kami) → 手紙 (te-*gami*). Affects the surface form of kun'yomi readings in some compounds.

## Process / Mechanics

### Pedagogical Sequencing for On/Kun

The practitioner consensus (Tofugu, WaniKani) converges on a single strategy that significantly reduces memorization burden [tofugu.com, knowledge.wanikani.com]:

1. **Teach one reading per kanji** — the reading that appears in 80–90% of uses. For most kanji this is the on'yomi (because jukugo compounds dominate N3+ vocabulary). For a few high-frequency kanji used mainly as standalone words (人, 日, 月), teach kun'yomi first.

2. **Learn secondary readings through vocabulary study** — not in isolation. When 生 (SEI as in 先生, 学生) appears in a new word as なま (生ビール, fresh beer) or いき (生き物, living thing), treat it as a new vocabulary item, not a new "reading to memorize."

3. **Use the four rules as a real-time reading strategy** — when encountering an unknown compound in reading, apply rules 1–3 to make a reasonable first-pass reading. Verify against context or dictionary.

4. **Make the vocabulary-reading connection explicit** — for each new vocabulary item, students should identify which reading type it uses and why. This builds pattern intuition rather than rule dependence.

### Dictionary Conventions

In Japanese dictionaries and course materials:
- On'yomi readings are listed in **KATAKANA**: セン, テイ, カ
- Kun'yomi readings are listed in **hiragana**: いく, みず, やま

This convention is universal and students should internalize it immediately — seeing katakana in a kanji dictionary entry tells you that reading is on'yomi.

### Voiced Variants (Rendaku)

When the second element of a kun'yomi compound is a voiceable consonant (k, s, t, h), it often voices: k→g, s→z, t→d, h→b/p. This doesn't change the reading classification — it's still kun'yomi — but students must recognize the voiced form as the same reading:
- 本 (ほん HON) + 棚 (たな tana) → 本棚 (ほんだな hon-*da*na, bookshelf) — kun'yomi compound with rendaku

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Trying to predict kun'yomi from on'yomi or vice versa.** There is no systematic phonological relationship between the two reading types. 山 (SAN/ZAN on'yomi, yama kun'yomi) — these look and sound completely different. Students who hunt for phonological patterns waste effort; the connection is etymological, not phonological.

2. **Memorizing all readings simultaneously.** The kanji 生 has 10+ readings. Any study method that presents all 10 at once produces interference and near-zero retention. Learning the primary reading (SEI for most compound uses) plus two or three kun'yomi through high-frequency vocabulary is the correct approach.

3. **Applying Rule 1 to names.** Students who encounter 田中 or 上野 and confidently apply "two kanji = on'yomi" produce た-CHUU or JOU-YAKU instead of Tanaka or Ueno. Names are a category apart.

4. **Assuming kun'yomi compounds are on'yomi.** 月見 is easily misread as GATSU-KEN rather than tsuki-mi. The presence of a concrete, nature-based or traditional-culture meaning is a soft cue that kun'yomi may apply.

5. **Forgetting that the same kanji can appear in both reading types.** Students sometimes learn 火 (ひ, fire) and then are confused that 火山 is KA-ZAN, not HI-YAMA. The context rule (compound vs. standalone/okurigana) is the disambiguation key, not the kanji identity.

## Hands-On Elements

- **Reading prediction exercise:** Present 20 two-kanji compounds students haven't seen. Ask them to apply Rule 1 and attempt a reading from the on'yomi they know. Check accuracy. Discuss exceptions.
- **Rule classification drill:** Provide 30 words in a mix of kanji-only, kanji+okurigana, and compound forms. Students classify each as on'yomi, kun'yomi, or "uncertain/exception."
- **Vocabulary sorting:** Give a list of words using the same kanji in different reading contexts (e.g., 生: 先生/SEI, 生まれる/うまれ, 生ビール/なま). Students sort by reading type and identify the pattern.
- **Compound building lab:** Choose 5 N3 kanji. Use jisho.org to find 3 words each (compound, standalone, with okurigana). Students present their findings and the reading rule that applies to each.
- **Katakana dictionary convention practice:** Students look up 10 kanji in jisho.org and practice identifying on'yomi (katakana entries) vs. kun'yomi (hiragana entries) from the dictionary display.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

**Which reading to teach first?** WaniKani teaches on'yomi with the kanji, kun'yomi through vocabulary. Tofugu's mnemonic method encodes one "best" reading (sometimes kun'yomi for high-frequency standalone kanji). There is no consensus — but the "one reading first, others through vocabulary" principle is universally agreed upon.

**How much pattern teaching is productive?** The four rules above are practitioner consensus summaries. Academic Japanese linguistics does not organize the on/kun system the same way. Some linguists note that the rules have so many exceptions they mislead learners. The practitioner counterargument is that even imperfect rules bootstrap reading speed in authentic contexts.

**Is the on/kun distinction relevant for modern learners?** Some immersion-based learners argue they never consciously learned on/kun patterns — they just absorbed them through reading. This may be true for learners with exceptional input volume, but most adult learners benefit from explicit pattern awareness, especially at N3 where compound vocabulary is dense.
