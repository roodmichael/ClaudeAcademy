## Core Concepts

### The Scale of the Jump

Moving from N4 to N3 requires approximately 2,200 new words on top of the ~1,500 learned at N4 level. The total N3 vocabulary requirement is approximately 3,750 words [migaku.com, jlptsensei.com, mojiiq.zistica.com]. This represents a qualitative shift, not merely a quantitative one: N4 vocabulary is largely concrete and situational (body parts, household items, common verbs of daily routine), while N3 vocabulary is predominantly abstract, compound, and register-sensitive.

The JLPT official level description for N3 states: "The ability to understand Japanese used in everyday situations to a certain degree," with reading competence defined as the ability to "grasp summary information like newspaper headlines" and understand "main points of slightly difficult everyday writings when contextual aids are present" [jlpt.jp].

### The Abstraction Shift

Three defining features characterize N3 vocabulary:

1. **Abstraction:** Words move from concrete (水, 食べ物, 電車) to conceptual: 影響 (influence), 可能性 (possibility), 経験 (experience), 改善 (improvement) [mojiiq.zistica.com].
2. **Two-kanji compounds (jukugo):** The majority of new N3 vocabulary consists of paired kanji with combined meanings. Knowing the components helps predict meaning but does not guarantee it.
3. **Register awareness:** Casual vs. formal variants of the same concept now matter: 使う vs. 利用する, 友達 vs. 友人, 先生 vs. 教師. Choosing the wrong register is a mistake at N3 level, not just a stylistic preference.

## Key Definitions

**Jukugo (熟語):** Compound words formed by combining two or more kanji, each read in its on'yomi. The dominant vocabulary form at N3+. Example: 解決 (かいけつ, resolution) = 解 (untie) + 決 (decide).

**SRS (Spaced Repetition System):** A study method that schedules flashcard review at optimal intervals based on the spacing effect — reviewing material right before it would be forgotten. Implemented in tools like Anki.

**I+1 Input:** A vocabulary acquisition strategy derived from Krashen's Input Hypothesis. A sentence is I+1 when it contains exactly one unknown word and all other words are already known. This is optimal for acquisition because context supports inference.

**Collocation:** A statistically stable word combination: 意見を言う (state an opinion) vs. *意見を話す (say opinion, unnatural). At N3 level, students must begin learning not just words but the words they travel with.

**Register:** The formality level of language. At N3, register distinctions become grammatically significant: using 使う where 利用する is expected sounds casual; using 友人 where 友達 is expected sounds stiff.

**理由 vs. 原因:** A key N3 near-synonym pair. 理由 (りゆう) = a stated reason or justification (answer to "why did you do X?"). 原因 (げんいん) = a physical or causal cause (answer to "what caused X?"). Confusing them is a common exam error.

**経験 vs. 体験:** Another key pair. 経験 (けいけん) = accumulated experience over time. 体験 (たいけん) = a specific lived experience, often a one-time event. You 体験 skydiving; you 経験 teaching.

## Process / Mechanics

### The Acquisition Funnel

Research on vocabulary acquisition consistently finds that learners need to encounter a target word 10–20 times in varied contexts before it enters long-term memory [Frontiers in Psychology, NCBIPubMed research on lexical processing]. A vocabulary list encounter counts for far less than a sentence encounter, which counts for far less than using the word productively.

The recommended funnel:
1. **First encounter:** Learn the word's core meaning and reading from a thematic list or SRS deck
2. **Second through fifth encounters:** See it in example sentences (Anki sentence cards, Bunpro)
3. **Sixth through tenth encounters:** Encounter in authentic reading (NHK Web Easy, graded readers)
4. **Beyond tenth:** Use the word in production (writing exercises, speaking)

### Thematic Grouping Strategy

Introduce new vocabulary in semantic clusters rather than alphabetically or randomly. The brain forms stronger associative networks when related concepts appear together. Suggested sequence for course sessions:

1. **Actions and change:** 始める/始まる, 変える/変わる, 決める/決まる (also doubles as transitive/intransitive practice)
2. **Mental states:** 感じる, 気づく, 考える, 判断する
3. **Social relationships:** 協力する, 信頼する, 批判する, 支持する
4. **Formal/abstract nouns:** 影響, 可能性, 経験, 原因, 結果

### SRS Card Design for N3

At N4, simple word-to-English cards are acceptable. At N3, research and practitioner consensus recommend:
- **Sentence cards** with the target word in an I+1 sentence
- **Japanese front** (see the Japanese sentence) → **Japanese + English back** (see meaning + reading)
- Avoid multiple choice — recognition is not recall [tofugu.com/japanese/spaced-repetition]
- Aim for ~15-minute daily sessions rather than marathon cramming

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Speed of recall problem:** Knowing a word passively (can recognize it on a list) is not the same as being able to use it. JLPT tests require recognition within about half a second. Students who rely on vocabulary lists without production practice consistently hit this wall [rapid-jt.com].

2. **Ignoring register:** Learners focus on denotation (what a word means) and ignore register (when you say it). Using 使う in a business writing context or 友達 when introducing a colleague are N3-level register mistakes.

3. **Abstract vocabulary in isolation:** Words like 影響 and 可能性 are memorized in English translation without understanding their collocations: 影響を与える (to give influence, i.e., affect), 可能性がある (there is a possibility). Without the collocations, the words cannot be used.

4. **Skipping review of N4 vocabulary:** The exam presupposes N4 mastery. Students who rushed through N4 vocab and stopped reviewing find N3 reading passages confusing because foundational words are not automatic.

5. **Treating thematic vocabulary as the full job:** Vocabulary lists only establish form-meaning links. Collocational knowledge, register, and contextual use must be built through reading and production, not list review alone.

## Hands-On Elements

- **Thematic word sort exercise:** Give students 30 N3 vocabulary items and ask them to sort into semantic clusters without looking at meanings — then compare. Builds associative thinking.
- **I+1 sentence writing:** Students choose 5 new words per week and write original sentences using only known vocabulary except the target word.
- **Register discrimination exercise:** Present pairs (使う/利用する, 友達/友人, 先生/教師) with situations (business email, casual conversation, official form). Students choose the correct register and justify.
- **理由/原因 discrimination drill:** Provide 10 context sentences and students decide whether 理由 or 原因 fits. Then analyze why.
- **Spaced recall sprint:** Students recall 20 words in 60 seconds from memory (no list). Track score weekly to measure retention speed, not just accuracy.

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

**SRS vs. massive input:** The Japanese learning community is divided between vocabulary-first (build SRS deck → then read) and input-first (read authentic material → mine vocabulary from context) approaches. Frequency data consistently favors the input approach for long-term retention, but learners without a vocabulary base struggle to find I+1 material. Most practitioners at N3 recommend a hybrid: structured SRS for new vocabulary, then reinforcement through reading.

**How many words before N3 reading is viable?** Some practitioners claim N3 reading is accessible above 2,000 words (with dictionary support); others insist the 3,750-word baseline is required. The realistic answer depends on how well N4 vocabulary is mastered.

**Vocabulary counting methodology:** Different sources count differently — some count distinct word forms (食べる and 食べます as separate), others count lemmas (食べ as one unit). The 3,750 figure is a commonly cited estimate, not an officially published JLPT number.
