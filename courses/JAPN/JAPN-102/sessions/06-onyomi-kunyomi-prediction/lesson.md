# Session 06: On'yomi and Kun'yomi — The Reading Prediction System
**Course:** JAPN-102
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to apply the four on'yomi/kun'yomi prediction rules to attempt readings of unknown kanji combinations, identify the dictionary convention for distinguishing reading types, and explain the historical origins of the two-reading system.

---

## Opening

Here is a puzzle: the kanji 火 appears in three different contexts.
- 火 (standalone): ひ — fire
- 火山 (compound): か-ざん — volcano
- 花火 (compound): はな-び — fireworks

The same character, three different readings. And yet there is a logic to all three — a logic you can learn. By the end of this session, you will understand why 火 is pronounced ひ in one context, か in another, and び in a third.

---

## Why Two Reading Systems Exist

Japanese kanji have two kinds of readings because kanji arrived in Japan from a language that already had a spoken form.

**On'yomi (音読み, "sound reading")** are the Chinese-derived pronunciations. When Buddhist monks and scholars imported kanji from China (beginning around the 4th–6th centuries CE), they borrowed both the character and its Chinese pronunciation. This happened in three waves, which is why some kanji have multiple on'yomi reflecting different eras of Chinese:

- **Go-on (呉音):** 4th–6th century CE, from the Wu region
- **Kan-on (漢音):** 7th–9th century, Tang Dynasty — the source of most modern on'yomi
- **Tō-on (唐音):** 1185–1573, later medieval Chinese

Example: 明 has めい (Kan-on), みょう (Go-on), and みん (Tō-on). Most learners first encounter めい in compounds like 説明 (explanation) and 証明 (proof).

**Kun'yomi (訓読み, "meaning reading")** are the native Japanese pronunciations. Before kanji arrived, Japanese was already a spoken language with its own words. When Chinese characters were adopted, Japanese writers mapped native Japanese words onto characters with matching meanings. 火 already had a native word ひ (fire). The character got attached to that native word as its kun'yomi.

The result: most kanji have at least one on'yomi (the Chinese-derived sound) and at least one kun'yomi (the native Japanese word). This looks overwhelming until you learn the four prediction rules.

---

## The Four Rules

These rules handle the vast majority of readings you will encounter in N3 Japanese. They are not absolute — exceptions exist — but they are reliable enough to use as a real-time reading strategy.

---

### Rule 1: Two or more kanji with no hiragana between them → On'yomi

This is the single most important rule. When kanji are combined into a **jukugo compound** with no hiragana between them, they use their on'yomi readings.

Examples:
- 東京 → とうきょう (TOU + KYOU, not higashi + miyako)
- 電話 → でんわ (DEN + WA)
- 先生 → せんせい (SEN + SEI)
- 経済 → けいざい (KEI + ZAI)
- 地下鉄 → ちかてつ (CHI + KA + TETSU)

Why? Because jukugo compounds were created to carry over Chinese vocabulary into Japanese. They use the Chinese-derived sounds. This is why N3 vocabulary (which is dominated by jukugo) is also N3 on'yomi practice.

---

### Rule 2: Single kanji standing alone → Kun'yomi

When a kanji appears by itself as a complete word, with no other kanji adjacent and no attached hiragana, it typically uses its kun'yomi.

Examples:
- 火 (standalone) → ひ (fire) — not KA
- 山 → やま (mountain) — not SAN
- 人 → ひと (person) — not JIN
- 目 → め (eye) — not MOKU
- 道 → みち (road/path) — not DOU

These standalone readings are often the most concrete, everyday words — they are native Japanese vocabulary, not Chinese imports.

---

### Rule 3: Kanji + okurigana (hiragana suffix) → Kun'yomi

When a kanji is followed by hiragana that is grammatically part of the word — a verb ending or adjective ending (called **okurigana**, 送り仮名) — the kanji uses its kun'yomi.

Examples:
- 食べる → た(beru) — 食 in kun'yomi たべ
- 話す → はな(su) — 話 in kun'yomi はなす
- 新しい → あたら(しい) — 新 in kun'yomi あたらしい
- 大きい → おお(きい) — 大 in kun'yomi おおきい
- 変わる → か(わる) — 変 in kun'yomi かわる

The okurigana (the hiragana ending) is the visual signal: "this kanji is functioning as a verb root or adjective root — use the native Japanese reading."

---

### Rule 4: Names and places → Unpredictable (nanori)

Japanese names use a third category of readings called *nanori* (名乗り) that follow neither on'yomi nor kun'yomi patterns reliably. Apply Rules 1–3 to names and you will often be wrong:

- 田中 (Tanaka) — not TAN-CHUU despite Rule 1 (both kanji present, no hiragana)
- 上野 (Ueno) — not JOU-YAKU
- 美咲 (Misaki) — not BI-SEN

For names, furigana is the only reliable guide. Rule 4 is really "do not apply Rules 1–3 to names."

---

## Exceptions That Don't Break the Rules

**Kun'yomi compounds:** A minority of compound words use kun'yomi for both kanji, usually when the concept is concrete, nature-related, or directional:
- 月見 → つきみ (moon viewing) — 月=tsuki, 見=mi
- 手紙 → てがみ (letter) — 手=te, 紙=gami (voiced)
- 南口 → みなみぐち (south exit) — 南=minami, 口=kuchi

How do you spot these? The soft cues are: concrete/nature vocabulary, traditional culture terms, and a slightly "old-fashioned" feeling. When in doubt, check jisho.org.

**Mixed readings:** A small number of compounds mix one on'yomi and one kun'yomi:
- 台所 → だいどころ (kitchen) — 台=DAI (on), 所=dokoro (kun)
- 組合 → くみあい (union/association) — 組=kumi (kun), 合=AI (on)

These mixed readings have memorably awkward names in Japanese linguistics: 重箱読み (on-kun compound) and 湯桶読み (kun-on compound). Treat them as vocabulary items to memorize individually.

---

## The Dictionary Convention: Katakana vs. Hiragana

In Japanese dictionaries (including jisho.org), readings are listed in two scripts:

- **On'yomi → KATAKANA**: セン、テイ、カ
- **Kun'yomi → hiragana**: いく、みず、やま

This is universal. Once you internalize it, a single glance at a dictionary entry tells you whether a reading is Chinese-derived or native Japanese. In jisho.org, click on any kanji and you will see this convention immediately.

---

## Rendaku: Voiced Variants in Kun'yomi Compounds

When the second element of a kun'yomi compound is a voiceable consonant (k, s, t, h), it sometimes voices: k→g, s→z, t→d, h→b/p. This is called **rendaku (連濁)**, "sequential voicing."

Examples:
- 本 (HON) + 棚 (たな, tana) → 本棚 (ほんだな, hon-**da**na) — bookshelf
- 手 (te) + 紙 (かみ, kami) → 手紙 (てがみ, te-**ga**mi) — letter

Rendaku does not change the reading classification — it is still kun'yomi. But the voiced form can confuse students who learned the unvoiced reading of the second element. When you see が where you expected か, or だ where you expected た, consider: is this rendaku in a kun'yomi compound?

---

## The Practical Strategy: One Reading First

The mistake most learners make is trying to memorize all on'yomi and kun'yomi for a kanji simultaneously. 生 has over 10 readings: せい, しょう, い(きる), う(まれる), お(う), なま, き... and so on.

The correct strategy:
1. **Learn one reading per kanji** — the reading that appears in 80–90% of its uses. For most N3 kanji, this is the on'yomi (since jukugo compounds dominate). For a few high-frequency standalone kanji (人, 日, 月, 山), learn the kun'yomi first.
2. **Acquire other readings through vocabulary.** When 生 appears in a new context (生ビール = なまビール, 生き物 = いきもの), treat it as a new vocabulary item — not as "another reading to memorize."

---

## Why This Matters

N3 reading fluency depends on decoding kanji compounds quickly. The four rules give you a first-pass reading strategy for any kanji combination you encounter — even one you've never seen. When you read 改善策 (かいぜんさく, improvement measures), you apply Rule 1 (three kanji, no hiragana → on'yomi) and attempt: KAI-ZEN-SAKU. You have never memorized that compound, but you can read it.

That is the difference between reading Japanese and decoding it.

---

## Key Takeaways

- On'yomi (音読み) are Chinese-derived; kun'yomi (訓読み) are native Japanese. Two reading systems exist because two linguistic layers were fused over 1,500 years of history.
- The four rules: (1) compound/no hiragana → on'yomi; (2) standalone → kun'yomi; (3) kanji + okurigana → kun'yomi; (4) names → unpredictable.
- In dictionaries: on'yomi is listed in KATAKANA; kun'yomi in hiragana.
- Rendaku voices the second element of kun'yomi compounds: か→が, た→だ, etc.
- Learn one reading per kanji first; acquire others through vocabulary.
