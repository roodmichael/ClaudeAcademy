# Session 10: Reading Comprehension I — Strategies and Passage Types
**Course:** JAPN-102
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to apply the skim-scan-dive reading strategy to N3 reading passages, identify the four passage types on the JLPT N3 dokkai section, and use discourse connectives to trace the logical structure of an N3 text.

---

## Opening

Here is an uncomfortable truth about the JLPT N3: you can know all the vocabulary, all the kanji, and all the grammar from Sessions 01–09 — and still fail the reading section because you ran out of time.

The reading section (読解, dokkai) is combined with vocabulary and grammar in a 100-minute block. Expert guidance allocates about 40–45 minutes to reading. N3 has four passage types with multiple passages. That is roughly 3 minutes per passage.

Students who read every word from start to finish, then look at the questions, use significantly more than 3 minutes per passage. The exam does not give that time back.

This session is about strategy. Your Japanese is already there. Now we build the reading behaviors that make it accessible under pressure.

---

## The Four N3 Passage Types

The JLPT N3 reading section contains four distinct formats. Knowing which type you are reading tells you immediately what to look for.

**Type 1: Short passages (短文, ~200 characters, 4–5 questions)**
Brief, focused texts with a single main point. Common formats: short emails, notifications, simple instructions, single-paragraph explanations. Questions test detail comprehension, vocabulary in context, and referential expressions (what does この/それ refer to?).
*Strategy:* Read carefully — these are short enough to read fully. Still, read the question first.

**Type 2: Medium passages (中文, ~300–500 characters, 3 questions)**
Developed argument or comparison texts. Common formats: informal essays, product explanations, advice columns, social notices. Questions test main idea, stated reasons, and contrast/cause-effect structure.
*Strategy:* Apply skim-scan-dive (below). 3 minutes maximum.

**Type 3: Long passages (長文, ~700–800 characters, 2 questions)**
Essay-style texts with a clear argument or narrative arc. Common formats: opinion pieces, explanatory essays, short narratives. Questions focus on the author's main claim and the logical relationship between sections.
*Strategy:* Skim aggressively. The main point is in the last paragraph, almost always.

**Type 4: Information retrieval (情報検索)**
Real-world functional documents: event flyers, Q&A pages, price lists, timetables, notices. The task is to find specific data, not to read the whole document.
*Strategy:* Read the question first. Scan exclusively for the specific item requested. Do not read the whole document.

## The Skim-Scan-Dive Strategy

For Types 2 and 3 (and any time-pressured reading), apply three steps in order:

**Step 1 — SCAN the questions first (30 seconds)**
Before reading the passage, read all questions and answer options. Note the key term or concept being asked about. This is not cheating — it directs your attention so you read the passage purposefully, not blindly.

**Step 2 — SKIM the passage (60 seconds)**
Read:
- The title or heading (if present)
- The first sentence of each paragraph
- The last sentence of the passage

Goal: Identify the author's purpose and the main argument direction. Do not read every sentence. Do not stop at unknown words.

**Step 3 — DIVE into the relevant section**
Return to the passage. With the question's key term in mind, locate the relevant paragraph or sentence. Read that section carefully. Confirm your answer by verifying it matches the passage logic — not just the surface wording.

**Critical point:** This order matters. Questions first, then skim, then dive. Students who read the whole passage first waste time re-reading. Questions direct the dive.

## Discourse Connectives: The Logical Architecture of N3 Text

Discourse connectives (接続詞) are the signals that tell you *how* parts of a passage relate to each other. Recognizing them is the fastest way to map a passage's logic during the skim phase.

**Contrast and concession:**
- しかし (however) — simple contrast; the next sentence goes in a different direction
- でも (but) — casual contrast
- ところが (however/but) — unexpected or surprising result
- 一方 (on the other hand) — parallel contrast between two things
- 〜のに (even though) — emotional contrast (from JAPN-101)

**Causation and result:**
- だから / ですから (therefore) — personal assertion of cause-effect
- ので (so/because) — objective cause (from JAPN-101)
- その結果 (as a result) — signals what followed from what was described
- なぜなら〜からです (because...) — introduces explanation

**Addition:**
- また (also/moreover) — adds another point
- さらに (furthermore/additionally) — adds a stronger or more extreme point
- それに (besides/what's more) — adds a supporting point

**Summary and conclusion:**
- つまり (in other words) — signals a restatement or conclusion
- 要するに (in sum/to put it simply) — signals the punchline
- というわけで (for that reason/thus) — signals a consequence or conclusion

**Sequence:**
- まず (first/to begin)
- 次に (next)
- 最後に (finally)

**During the skim phase:** When you encounter しかし, you know the passage is about to reverse direction — the contrast is usually the main point. When you see つまり or 要するに, the author's conclusion is about to appear. This lets you navigate to the key content without reading every word.

## Question Type Strategies

**Main idea questions:** "What is the author mainly saying?" → Look in the last paragraph. Japanese essay-style writing usually states the conclusion at the end. Watch for つまり, 要するに, というわけで.

**Referential expression questions:** "What does これ in line X refer to?" → Look at the immediately preceding sentence or clause. The referent is almost always in the sentence just before the referential expression.

**Reason/cause questions:** "Why did X happen?" or "For what reason did the author...?" → Look backward from the result. Japanese places reason before result: [reason]ので[result], or look for なぜなら.

**Contrast questions:** "What does the author contrast with X?" → Find the contrast marker (しかし, ところが, 一方). The answer is in the clause after the marker.

**Author's opinion questions:** "What does the author think about X?" → The author's view is stated in the first or last paragraph. Look for 〜と思う, 〜と考える, 〜ではないだろうか (rhetorical question implying the author's view).

**Information retrieval questions:** Read the question and note the specific type of information requested (a price, a date, an eligibility condition). Scan the document for that category. Read only the relevant section.

## Trap Answers to Avoid

N3 multiple-choice questions are designed to test whether you understood the passage or just recognized vocabulary from it. Three common trap answer types:

**Half-truths:** The answer is factually correct about part of the passage but misses a key contrast or condition. Watch for answer choices that omit しかし clauses or ignore のに contrasts.

**Extreme words:** Answers using 絶対に (absolutely), 全然〜ない (not at all), 必ず (without exception). Exam passages rarely make absolute claims. Be suspicious of absolute answers.

**Irrelevant correctness:** An answer that is true but does not answer the question asked. The passage might contain this information, but if the question asks about X and the answer describes Y, it is wrong regardless of whether Y is accurate.

---

## Why This Matters

Reading strategy is a learnable skill, not a fixed trait. Students who approach the N3 reading section with deliberate technique — scanning questions first, skimming for structure, diving to relevant sections — outperform students with equivalent Japanese ability who read linearly. The 3-minute constraint is not a wall; it is a budget. Spend it strategically.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Four N3 passage types: short (detail), medium (argument), long (main idea), information retrieval (specific data). Each requires a different focus.
- The skim-scan-dive order: scan questions → skim for structure → dive to relevant section. Always questions first.
- Discourse connectives are your structural GPS: しかし marks reversal, つまり marks conclusion, なぜなら marks explanation, まず/次に/最後に mark sequence.
- Three trap answer types: half-truths, extreme words, irrelevant correctness. Verify every answer against a specific line in the passage.
