# Session 07: Near-Synonyms I — Volitional vs. Non-Volitional Verbs
**Course:** JAPN-102
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to correctly choose between 見る/見える and 聞く/聞こえる based on the volitional/non-volitional distinction, and apply the correct particle (を vs. が) that accompanies each verb type.

---

## Opening

"I can see the mountain from here." In English, this is one verb — "see." In Japanese, it is a choice between two different verbs — and the wrong choice also changes your particle.

This is the near-synonym problem at its most systematic. 見る and 見える both relate to seeing. 聞く and 聞こえる both relate to hearing. But they encode something English does not: whether the act of perception was intentional or spontaneous. That distinction runs deep in Japanese grammar, and once you understand it, a whole category of N3 errors disappears.

---

## The Volitional/Non-Volitional Distinction

Japanese distinguishes between actions you choose to do (**volitional**) and perceptions or events that happen to you (**non-volitional**). This is not just a vocabulary distinction — it is a structural one that affects which particle you use.

**Volitional verbs** describe deliberate actions: you directed your attention, chose to act, exerted your will. They take **を** for the direct object.

**Non-volitional verbs** describe perceptions or events that enter your awareness without volition: you didn't try to see it, you just noticed it; you didn't try to hear it, it just reached your ears. They take **が** for what is perceived.

---

## 見る vs. 見える

**見る (みる)** — to look at, to watch [volitional]
- You direct your gaze. You choose to look.
- Takes **を** for the thing you look at.
- 星を見る (I look at the stars — I directed my gaze toward the stars)
- テレビを見る (I watch TV)
- 地図を見る (I look at the map)

**見える (みえる)** — to be visible, to appear [non-volitional]
- Something enters your visual field without you directing your gaze.
- Takes **が** for what is visible.
- 星が見える (The stars are visible — they appear in my field of vision)
- 山が見える (You can see the mountain from here — the mountain presents itself)
- 遠くに町が見える (A town is visible in the distance)

**The test:** Can you say "I chose to do this"? If yes → 見る (を). If no, it just happened to you → 見える (が).

---

## Contrast Sentences

The clearest way to see the distinction is in minimal pairs — the same situation described with both verbs:

1. 空の星を見た。→ "I looked at the stars in the sky." (deliberate stargazing)
   空に星が見えた。→ "Stars were visible in the sky." (they appeared without effort on my part)

2. あの映画を見ましたか？→ "Did you watch that movie?" (deliberate choice to watch)
   窓から海が見えます。→ "The sea is visible from the window." (it presents itself)

3. 先生の字が小さすぎて、黒板が見えません。→ "I can't see the blackboard — the teacher's writing is too small." (non-volitional; it's not reaching my perception)
   もっとよく見てください。→ "Please look more carefully." (volitional; I am directing you to look)

---

## 聞く vs. 聞こえる

The same distinction applies to hearing:

**聞く (きく)** — to listen to, to hear intentionally; also "to ask" [volitional]
- Takes **を** for what you listen to.
- 音楽を聞く (I listen to music)
- 授業をよく聞いてください (Please listen to the lesson carefully)
- 先生に質問を聞く (I ask the teacher a question — same verb, different use: "ask")

**聞こえる (きこえる)** — to be audible, to reach one's ears [non-volitional]
- Takes **が** for what is heard.
- 音楽が聞こえる (I can hear music — it's reaching my ears)
- 外から声が聞こえる (I can hear voices from outside)
- よく聞こえない → "I can't hear well" (non-volitional: the sound isn't reaching me)

**The test:** Did you try to hear it? If yes → 聞く (を). If it just reached your ears → 聞こえる (が).

---

## Contrast Sentences

1. ラジオを聞いています。→ "I am listening to the radio." (deliberate choice)
   隣の部屋からテレビの音が聞こえる。→ "I can hear TV sounds from the next room." (reaching me involuntarily)

2. 彼女の歌を聞きたい。→ "I want to listen to her singing." (desire to make the deliberate choice)
   彼女の歌が遠くから聞こえてきた。→ "Her singing reached my ears from far away." (non-volitional)

3. 電話が聞こえましたか？→ "Could you hear the phone?" (could it reach your ears?)
   電話を聞いていましたか？→ "Were you listening to the phone call?" (were you deliberating attending to it?)

---

## Why the Particle Changes

This is where the grammar from JAPN-101 connects directly to vocabulary:

- **を** marks a direct object — something you act upon deliberately.
- **が** marks a subject or a target of involuntary perception.

Non-volitional verbs (見える, 聞こえる) behave grammatically like intransitive verbs: the thing perceived isn't acted upon, it is simply present. It takes が.

If you choose the wrong verb (see 見る when 見える is correct), you are also likely to use the wrong particle. A student who writes 山を見える has made two errors from one misunderstanding — wrong verb AND wrong particle.

Conversely: if you notice が where you expected を, that is a signal that the verb is non-volitional. Use 見える or 聞こえる.

---

## The Broader Pattern

The volitional/non-volitional distinction appears beyond this pair. At N3 and N2, you will encounter other non-volitional verbs that take が:

- **わかる** — "to understand" (non-volitional: understanding arrives, you don't make yourself understand)
  日本語がわかる (I understand Japanese — not 日本語をわかる)

- **できる** — "to be able to, to be completed" (non-volitional: ability exists or doesn't)
  日本語ができる (I can speak Japanese — not 日本語をできる)

These are N4 patterns you already know, and now you can see why they take が: they are non-volitional states, not deliberate actions.

---

## Why This Matters

This distinction is tested directly on the JLPT N3. Vocabulary questions present a sentence with a blank and ask which verb fits. Reading comprehension passages use both verbs, and the distinction affects the meaning: "I looked at the mountain" is a very different experience from "the mountain was visible from there." Getting this wrong in comprehension leads to wrong answers even when the grammar is otherwise understood.

---

## Key Takeaways

- 見る (volitional, を) = you directed your gaze. 見える (non-volitional, が) = it entered your visual field.
- 聞く (volitional, を) = you listened deliberately. 聞こえる (non-volitional, が) = it reached your ears.
- Choosing the wrong verb also corrupts the particle — one misunderstanding creates two errors.
- The volitional/non-volitional distinction is a broader pattern in Japanese grammar: わかる and できる also take が because they are non-volitional states.
