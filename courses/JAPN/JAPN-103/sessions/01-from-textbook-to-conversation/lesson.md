# Session 01: From Textbook to Conversation
**Course:** JAPN-103
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to recognize and produce the six most essential casual speech contractions (てる, とく, ちゃう, んだ, じゃない, なきゃ) and explain why the gap between textbook and natural Japanese exists.

---

## Opening

You have been studying Japanese for a while now. Grammar patterns, vocabulary, kanji. You have put in real work. Then you play a Japanese podcast at normal speed, or watch a drama without subtitles, and it doesn't sound like the Japanese you know. People are mumbling and running words together and leaving sentences half-finished.

Here is the thing: that is not your imagination. There really are two Japaneses. One is the language of textbooks, JLPT tests, and formal writing — clean, fully conjugated, every particle in place. The other is the language people actually use when they talk to each other. This course is about closing the gap between them. This session explains why the gap exists and hands you the six contractions that matter most.

---

## Why Two Japaneses?

Every language has a formal register and a casual register, but Japanese has an especially wide gap between them because the formal register — **丁寧体 (teineigo)**, polite speech with ます and です — is what almost all classroom instruction teaches. You probably spent your first year learning ます/です Japanese almost exclusively.

Natural spoken Japanese among friends and peers uses **タメ口 (tameguchi)**, the plain/casual style. And casual style doesn't just drop ます/です. It also *contracts* — it clips and compresses common verb endings into shorter sounds. These clips are called **縮約形 (shukuyaku-kei)**, contracted forms.

The rule behind most of them is simple: the mora **い (i)** gets dropped from common endings, and the surrounding sounds assimilate. That one process generates most of what you need.

## The Six Most Essential Contractions

These are your first targets — the ones that are safe to produce with close friends and universal enough to hear everywhere.

### 1. ている → てる (Progressive/State)

The most common contraction in Japanese. Drop the い from いる:

- 見ている → **見てる** (watching/looking)
- 食べている → **食べてる** (eating)
- わかっている → **わかってる** (understanding / I know)

You'll hear てる constantly in drama, anime, and conversation. It sounds completely natural. Start using it with friends and in casual writing (texting, etc.).

### 2. ておく → とく (Preparation)

The て form of a verb + おく means "do in advance / do for future use." In casual speech, te+o merges:

- 覚えておく → **覚えとく** (memorize it in advance)
- 調べておく → **調べとく** (look it up ahead of time)
- 予約しておく → **予約しとく** (make a reservation in advance)

Notice that the と in とく is not a particle here — it's a merged sound.

### 3. てしまう → ちゃう / じゃう (Completion/Regret)

**てしまう** means something was completed, often with a sense of "oh no" or "already done." In casual speech:
- If the て-form ends in **て**: しまう → **ちゃう** (食べてしまう → **食べちゃう**)
- If the て-form ends in **で**: しまう → **じゃう** (死んでしまう → **死んじゃう**)

Past tense: ちゃった / じゃった

- 忘れてしまった → **忘れちゃった** (I forgot! / oops I forgot)
- 飲んでしまった → **飲んじゃった** (I drank it all up)

The regret nuance fades in youth speech — ちゃった often just means "completed the action."

### 4. のだ → んだ / んです (Explanation)

You learned のだ and のです in JAPN-101 as the explanatory/assertive form — the one that says "here's the thing" or "let me explain." In natural speech, の always contracts to ん:

- 疲れたのだ → **疲れたんだ** (The thing is, I'm tired.)
- どこへ行くのですか → **どこへ行くんですか** (Where are you going? — tone of curiosity/request for explanation)
- やりたくないんだよ (The thing is, I don't want to do it.)

**んだ** is the most assertive/casual form. **んです** is polite. **んだよ** adds emphasis. You'll hear all three constantly.

### 5. ではない → じゃない (Negative)

This one you may already know. **では** contracts to **じゃ** in casual speech:

- そうではない → **そうじゃない** (That's not it. / That's wrong.)
- 違うのではない → **違うんじゃない** (It's not that it's different…)
- 日本人じゃないよ (I'm not Japanese.)

**じゃないですか / じゃないですよ** are the polite variants — widely used.

### 6. なければ → なきゃ / なくては → なくちゃ (Obligation)

"Have to do X" in polite Japanese is a mouthful: 行かなければなりません. Casual speech compresses the obligation ending:

- 行かなければ → **行かなきゃ** (gotta go / have to go)
- やらなければ → **やんなきゃ** (gotta do it)
- 行かなくては → **行かなくちゃ** (have to go — slightly softer)

**なきゃ** and **なくちゃ** are interchangeable in most casual contexts. Both work sentence-finally: **もう行かなきゃ** = "I've gotta go."

## The Production-Recognition Threshold

Not every contraction should be actively produced at this stage. The rule: **produce what is universal; recognize what is rougher**.

| Form | Produce or Recognize? |
|------|----------------------|
| てる | Produce freely |
| とく | Produce |
| ちゃう / じゃう | Produce |
| んだ / んです | Produce |
| じゃない | Produce |
| なきゃ / なくちゃ | Produce |
| てんの, てんだ (double contractions) | Recognize only for now |
| ら抜き言葉 (食べれる) | Recognize; use cautiously |
| Rough contractions (なんない, やんなきゃ) | Recognize |

---

## Why This Matters

You will never become comfortable speaking Japanese if you produce only textbook forms. Native speakers will understand you, but you will always sound formal and slightly stiff — like someone who speaks perfect literary English and says "whom" and "shall" in every sentence.

These contractions are not mistakes or lazy speech. They are the standard casual register. Using them signals to your conversation partner that you are comfortable with real Japanese, not just exam Japanese. That shift matters enormously for actual conversation.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Textbook Japanese (ます/です) and casual spoken Japanese (タメ口 with contractions) are two different registers, not two versions of the same one
- The い-dropping process generates most casual contractions — once you see it, you recognize it everywhere
- The six essential contractions (てる, とく, ちゃう, んだ, じゃない, なきゃ) are safe to produce in casual contexts immediately
- Register matching matters — these forms belong in casual speech, not business emails or JLPT writing sections
