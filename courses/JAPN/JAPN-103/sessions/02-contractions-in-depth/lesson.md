# Session 02: Contractions in Depth
**Course:** JAPN-103
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to recognize stacked contractions, navigate ら抜き言葉 with register awareness, produce the softened obligation forms, and sort any casual Japanese utterance by register appropriateness.

---

## Opening

Here's a sentence from casual anime speech: 何やってんだよ、もう。

Can you parse it? The underlying form is: 何をやっているのだよ、もう — "What are you doing, seriously." But in natural speech the を disappears, the ている contracts to てん (a further contraction of てる), and のだ becomes ん. The whole thing compresses from seven sounds to five.

This session takes you deeper into the contraction system — the forms that stack, the form that splits the country in a linguistic debate, and the sorting work that keeps you from sounding wrong in the wrong place.

---

## Stacked Contractions

When two or more contractions apply to the same form, they stack. This is why natural speech can be so hard to parse: a clause that takes ten syllables in textbook Japanese can become four.

**The ていのだ → てんの → てんの stack:**

The most common stack comes from ている + の (nominalizer or question marker) + だ:

1. ていのだ → てるのだ (step 1: い drops from いる)
2. てるのだ → てんだ (step 2: の contracts further in the running combination)
3. More emphatic: やっているのだよ → やってんだよ

This is why 何やってんだ sounds like one word. You're hearing: やっ+て+ん+だ = four morae from a six-mora base.

**The てたの stack:**

ていたの → てたの (standard contraction) → てたの (sometimes てたんだ)

- 知っていたのか → 知ってたのか (Did you know that?)
- 食べていたのに → 食べてたのに (Even though I was eating…)

**Practical rule:** If you encounter a casual Japanese utterance that seems impossibly short, count the contracted layers. Usually it is ている → てる → てん (when followed by の), each step legal on its own.

## The ら抜き言葉 Situation

**ら抜き言葉 (ra-nuki kotoba)** means "words with ら removed." It refers to dropping ら from the potential form of Group 2 (ichidan) verbs:

| Standard | ら抜き | Meaning |
|----------|--------|---------|
| 食べられる | 食べれる | can eat |
| 見られる | 見れる | can see |
| 起きられる | 起きれる | can wake up |
| 来られる | 来れる | can come |

**Why does this happen?** The standard potential form of Group 2 verbs (食べられる) looks identical to the passive form (食べられる). Context usually disambiguates, but ら抜き言葉 removes the ambiguity: 食べれる can only mean potential (can eat), never passive.

**The sociolinguistic reality:** Agency for Cultural Affairs surveys show ら抜き usage has risen every decade. Among speakers under 40, it is now a majority form in casual speech. On JLPT exams, in formal writing, and in business contexts, it is still marked as incorrect.

**What this means for you:**
- You will hear ら抜き言葉 constantly. Recognize it, understand it.
- Using it yourself in casual conversation sounds natural among friends.
- Do not use it in the JLPT writing section, business emails, or formal speech.
- If a Japanese speaker corrects your ら抜き in casual conversation, they are being very formal — note the register signal.

## Obligation Forms: The Full Picture

Session 01 introduced なきゃ and なくちゃ. The full picture of spoken obligation contractions:

| Full Form | Contraction | Register | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|---------|-------|
| なければならない | なければ / なきゃならない | Formal | The uncontracted form |
| なければ (alone) | なきゃ | Casual | Ends sentence by implication |
| なくてはいけない | なくちゃいけない / なくちゃ | Casual | The ては → ちゃ contraction |
| なければならない | なきゃいけない | Semi-casual | Common middle ground |
| なければならない | なんない | Rough casual | Very compressed |

The full expression なければならない ("must do; if not [X], it won't do") is almost never said in full in casual speech. 行かなきゃ (I gotta go) gets the meaning across with two morae instead of nine.

## I-Adjective Compression (The Rough Register)

These forms are common in the speech of young men and in anime/manga, but rare in polite or mixed-gender conversation:

| Full Form | Compressed | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| 高い | 高ぇ | 高ぇな… (that's expensive…) |
| 強い | 強ぇ | あいつ、強ぇよ (that guy is strong) |
| 凄い | すご | すごい → すご (often: すごい → すご!) |

**Register note:** These are very masculine and rough. If you are a woman, or in mixed company, or trying to build rapport rather than sound tough, avoid producing these. Recognize them in media; don't adopt them unless you understand the social signal they send.

## Sorting Contractions by Register

Here is a working framework for deciding what to produce:

**Always safe in casual contexts (produce freely):**
てる, ちゃう, じゃう, ちゃった, じゃった, とく, といた, んだ, んです, じゃない, じゃないですか, なきゃ, なくちゃ

**Casual-rough (produce only with close friends; recognize everywhere):**
てん (in てんの, てんだ), なんない, やんなきゃ, ら抜き言葉

**Strongly masculine/rough (recognize only unless deliberate):**
高ぇ, 強ぇ, 知らん (= 知らない in Kansai / rough masculine), ぞ, ぜ (more on these in Session 04)

**Formal/standard-only (never contract):**
Business emails, JLPT writing, academic writing, public speech — use ます/です with no contractions

---

## The Context That Decides Everything

Register is not about the form in isolation — it is about the relationship and situation. The same person might use:
- てる and ちゃった talking to their friend on the phone
- んですが and ますが in a work meeting with their manager
- ます/です (no contractions) writing an email to a client

Fluency means switching between these registers automatically, the way an English speaker doesn't consciously choose whether to say "I'm gonna go" or "I am going to leave now" — context dictates it before conscious thought.

---

## Why This Matters

Contractions are the grammar of belonging. Using the right register tells your conversation partner where you stand: inside their circle (casual) or outside it (formal). Getting this wrong doesn't just sound unnatural — it can signal the wrong social relationship. A highly formal stranger who then suddenly uses ぜ sounds confused or rude. Someone who addresses their boss with ちゃう sounds disrespectful.

The goal of this session is not just to know the forms but to sort them fast enough that they stop requiring conscious attention.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Contractions stack — ていのだ → てんだ involves two layers of compression; parsing them is a counting exercise
- ら抜き言葉 is pervasive in casual speech and has a functional advantage (disambiguating potential from passive) but remains incorrect in formal contexts
- Obligation contractions (なきゃ, なくちゃ) are the standard casual way to say "have to"
- I-adjective vowel compression (高ぇ) is rough and gendered; recognize it but produce it deliberately
- Register sorting — knowing which forms belong in which context — is the meta-skill underlying everything
