# Session 08: Listening I — Connected Speech and Pitch Accent
**Course:** JAPN-103
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain vowel devoicing, nasal allophony, mora timing, and the four pitch accent types — and identify why familiar Japanese words often sound unrecognizable at natural conversational speed.

---

## Opening

Here is an experiment worth trying. Find a clip of casual Japanese conversation — not an anime with exaggerated speech, but a real podcast or YouTube video of Japanese people talking to each other. Play it at 1.0x speed. Then play the same clip at 0.75x speed.

At 0.75x, you can probably recognize quite a few words. At 1.0x, they collapse into each other. That collapse is not a vocabulary gap. You know those words. The problem is that natural speech runs at 5–7 mora per second, and at that rate, the phonological system does things to sounds that your ear hasn't calibrated to yet.

This session explains exactly what those things are.

---

## The Speed Gap

**JLPT listening audio:** approximately 3 mora per second. Clean articulation. Full vowels. Standard spacing between utterances.

**Natural conversational Japanese:** approximately 5–7 mora per second. Devoiced vowels. Merged sounds. Sentences that trail off before their grammatical endpoint.

This is not a minor difference. It is nearly twice the speed, combined with phonological processes that change how individual sounds are realized. The good news: these processes follow rules. Once you know the rules, your ear can track them.

---

## Phonological Process 1: Vowel Devoicing (母音の無声化)

**What happens:** The high vowels **い (i)** and **う (u)** become devoiced — whispered or nearly inaudible — when they appear between two voiceless consonants, or at the end of a word after a voiceless consonant.

**Why it matters:** です (desu) sounds like "des" — the u is almost entirely absent. ます (masu) sounds like "mas." 聞きます (kikimasu) sounds like "kik'mas." If you are listening for the full vowel, you won't hear it.

**Voiceless consonants in Japanese:** k, s, t, ts, ch, f, h, p

**Devoicing examples:**

| Full Written Form | How It Actually Sounds | What Devoices |
|------------------|----------------------|---------------|
| です (desu) | "des" | The u after s |
| ます (masu) | "mas" | The u after s |
| 机 (tsukue) | "ts'kue" | The u between ts and k |
| 切符 (kippu) | "kip'pu" | The i between k and p |
| 好きです (suki desu) | "s'ki des" | The u after sk cluster |

**The practical rule:** When you encounter an い or う between voiceless consonants, expect it to be devoiced or inaudible. Train your ear to reconstruct the full word from the surrounding sounds.

---

## Phonological Process 2: Nasal Allophony (ん の変化)

**What happens:** The moraic nasal **ん** (written as N) sounds different depending on what follows it. It is still one mora — one beat — but its actual phonetic realization changes.

| Environment | ん sounds like | Example |
|-------------|---------------|---------|
| Before p, b, m | [m] — a lip closure | 新聞 shin-bun: the ん is like 'm' |
| Before t, d, n | [n] — tongue tip at ridge | 本当 hon-tō: standard 'n' |
| Before k, g | [ŋ] — back of tongue, like 'ng' in "sing" | 三月 san-gatsu: nasalized |
| Before vowels | Nasalized vowel — nasalized and open | 恩 on — the ん nasalizes the preceding vowel |
| Sentence-final | Open nasal — varies by speaker | そうなん (casual) |

**Why it matters:** If you're listening for ん to always sound the same way, you will miss it in many environments. It is still one mora of time even when it sounds like 'm' or 'ng.'

---

## Phonological Process 3: Mora Timing (モーラ拍)

**What a mora is:** The basic timing unit of Japanese phonology. Each mora takes approximately the same amount of time, regardless of whether it is a full syllable (ka, mi, to), a long vowel (the second half of おお or えい), or a special mora (ん, っ, the second half of a double consonant).

**Why this matters for listening:** Japanese words have a fixed number of morae, and each mora is one beat. In natural speech at 5-7 mora/second:

- ビール (bi-i-ru, 3 morae) passes in half a second
- テレビ (te-re-bi, 3 morae) passes in half a second
- 東京 (to-u-kyo-u, 4 morae) passes in less than a second

**Mora counting practice:** Count the morae in each word by tapping your finger:
- コーヒー (ko-o-hi-i) = 4 morae
- 切手 (ki-t-te) = 3 morae (き, っ, て)
- 雑誌 (za-s-shi) = 3 morae (ざ, っ, し)

**The double consonant trap:** っ (small tsu) counts as one mora — it is a beat of silence + consonant. At natural speed, 切って (ki-t-te, 3 morae) passes very quickly and the closure for the double t is brief. Learners often miss it.

## Phonological Process 4: Pitch Accent (ピッチアクセント)

Japanese uses a **pitch-based accent system** — each mora in a word is either high (H) or low (L) in pitch. This is different from English stress, where one syllable gets heavier emphasis. In Japanese, it is pitch, not volume, that marks accent.

**The fundamental rule:** Within a word, once the pitch drops from high to low, it does not rise again.

**Four pitch accent types in Tokyo/Standard Dialect:**

| Type | Name | Pattern | Example |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| Flat (heiban) | 平板型 | L → H, stays H | 橋 (bridge): L-H; particle も stays H |
| Head-high (atamadaka) | 頭高型 | H → L immediately after first mora | 雨 (rain): H-L |
| Middle-high (nakadaka) | 中高型 | L → H, drops somewhere in middle | 卵 (egg): L-H-H-L |
| Tail-high (odaka) | 尾高型 | L → H, drops only after a following particle | 橋 (bridge) vs. 箸 (chopsticks): L-H (drop before particle) |

**The good news:** Approximately 80% of Japanese vocabulary is heiban (flat). The pitch rises after the first mora and stays high. No drop.

**Classic minimal pairs** (why pitch accent matters for listening):

| Word | Reading | Pitch | Meaning |
|------|--------|-------|---------|
| 雨 | ame | H-L | rain |
| 飴 | ame | L-H | candy |
| 橋 | hashi | L-H (drop after particle) | bridge |
| 箸 | hashi | L-H (same as 橋!) | chopsticks |
| 端 | hashi | H-L | edge |
| 花 | hana | L-H | flower |
| 鼻 | hana | L-H | nose |

Note that 橋 and 箸 have identical pitch patterns — they're disambiguated by context and, in connected speech, by the pitch of the following particle.

**For learners at N3:** The goal is not pitch-perfect production. The goal is **awareness** — understanding why familiar words can sound unrecognizable when the pitch pattern is unexpected, and beginning to notice pitch patterns as an additional information channel.

---

## How These Processes Combine

In natural speech, multiple processes occur simultaneously on the same utterance. Consider:

好きですか？(Do you like it?)

- す: s is voiceless → き's い devoices between s and k
- です: the u devoices after s
- か: pitch drops after the question particle

At 5-7 mora/second, the entire utterance is less than a second long, with devoiced vowels making the total audible sounds: "sk'des-ka" — four recognizable sound units from a 5-mora word sequence.

This is why familiar vocabulary can seem unrecognizable in natural speech. The words are there. The phonology is compressing them.

## Why This Matters

This session provides the explanatory framework that makes shadowing (Session 09) effective. When you know *why* natural Japanese sounds different, you stop trying to parse it word-by-word and start training your ear to track patterns — the mora rhythm, the pitch contour, the vowel absences. That shift is the difference between struggling to understand and beginning to follow.

Michael's background in fermentation chemistry offers a useful parallel: knowing that diacetyl comes from a specific metabolic pathway doesn't let you taste it without practice, but it tells you what you're training your palate for. This session tells you what you're training your ear for.

## Key Takeaways

- Natural Japanese runs at 5-7 mora/second vs. JLPT audio at ~3 — nearly twice the speed
- Vowel devoicing: い and う become whispered or inaudible between voiceless consonants; です sounds like "des"
- Nasal allophony: ん sounds different before different consonants but is always one mora
- Mora timing: each mora takes one beat — ん, っ, and long vowel halves all count
- Pitch accent: four types; ~80% of vocabulary is flat (heiban); minimal pairs exist but context usually disambiguates
- These processes operate simultaneously in natural speech — the goal at N3 is awareness, not perfect perception
