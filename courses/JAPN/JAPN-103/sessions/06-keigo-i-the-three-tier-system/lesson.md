# Session 06: Keigo I — The Three-Tier System
**Course:** JAPN-103
**Learning Objective:** After this session, you will be able to explain the three keigo tiers (teineigo, sonkeigo, kenjōgo), identify which tier is expected in a given social situation, and apply the uchi/soto distinction correctly.

---

## Opening

Here is a situation that trips up many Japanese learners. You've been studying Japanese for years. Your grammar is solid, your vocabulary is strong. Then you walk into a Japanese interview or call a Japanese company for the first time, and the interaction immediately signals that you're out of your depth — not because you used the wrong word, but because the entire register you're operating in is wrong.

Keigo is that register. And it's not optional — it's grammatically encoded into Japanese in a way that makes it unavoidable in professional, service, and formal contexts. The good news: you don't need to master it completely at N3. You need to understand the system, know when each tier is expected, and be able to produce the most common forms reliably.

---

## What Keigo Is — And What It Isn't

**敬語 (keigo)** means "respectful language," but that translation undersells what it actually is. Keigo is a systematic grammatical encoding of social relationships. When you use keigo, you are not just being polite — you are explicitly marking:

- Who is "up" and who is "down" in the social relationship
- Whether the action being described belongs to the "up" person or the "down" person
- Whether you are inside or outside the relevant group

Getting keigo wrong doesn't make you rude the way using slang in a business email makes you rude. It creates a specific kind of misfire — like a software bug where the right function runs on the wrong object.

**Teineigo is not keigo in the narrow sense.** Using ます and です is polite — it is the baseline of non-casual Japanese. But sonkeigo and kenjōgo involve actual vocabulary substitution. The common learner mistake is thinking that ます/です alone = keigo. It does not. It is the starting point.

## The Three Tiers

### 1. 丁寧語 (Teineigo) — Polite Language

**What it is:** The polite baseline. Uses ます and です endings. No special vocabulary substitution.

**When it's used:**
- With strangers, acquaintances, first meetings
- Standard professional speech
- Service contexts (both directions)
- Any time you don't know the social relationship well enough to go casual

**What it is NOT:** Teineigo does not elevate others or humble yourself — it simply marks that you're maintaining polite distance. Think of it as the neutral professional register.

**Example:** 田中さんはどこに行きますか。(Where is Tanaka-san going?) — neutral polite, no special vocabulary.

### 2. 尊敬語 (Sonkeigo) — Honorific/Respectful Language

**What it is:** Language that *elevates the actions of others*. When you use sonkeigo, you are describing what someone of higher status (a customer, your boss, a teacher) is doing or did — and you are using special vocabulary or constructions that mark their action as elevated.

**The critical rule:** You never use sonkeigo for your own actions. Sonkeigo is for others. If you accidentally use sonkeigo to describe yourself, you have made a significant social error.

**How it works:**
- Some verbs substitute entirely: いる → いらっしゃる; 食べる → 召し上がる
- Productive pattern: お + [verb ます stem] + になる (for verbs not on the irregular list)

**Example:** 先生がいらっしゃいます。(The teacher is here / The teacher has arrived.) — いらっしゃる elevates the teacher's action of being/coming.

### 3. 謙譲語 (Kenjōgo) — Humble Language

**What it is:** Language that *lowers your own actions* (or those of your in-group) to show deference to the person you're speaking with. When you use kenjōgo, you are describing what you are doing — and you are using vocabulary that marks your action as humble.

**The critical rule:** You never use kenjōgo for others' actions. Kenjōgo is for yourself (or your uchi group). Using kenjōgo to describe a superior's action would be an insult — it humbles them.

**How it works:**
- Some verbs substitute entirely: 行く → 参る; もらう → いただく
- Productive pattern: お + [verb ます stem] + いたす

**Example:** 明日、参ります。(I will come/go tomorrow.) — 参る humbles the speaker's own action.

---

## The Fourth Category: 美化語 (Bikago) — Beautification Language

**美化語** uses お or ご prefixes not to mark social relationships but to make speech more elegant and refined. It's about cultivating the speaker, not elevating a specific person.

- お酒 (sake — the お makes it more refined than just 酒)
- ご飯 (meal/rice — more elegant than just 飯)
- お料理 (cooking — elegant phrasing)

Bikago is common in everyday speech, especially among women in standard dialect, but is not strictly about hierarchy. It doesn't obligate any specific response or create a social mismatch. Learn it as a separate layer from the three-tier system.

## The Uchi/Soto Distinction

**内 (uchi)** means inside — your group, your company, your family.
**外 (soto)** means outside — people from other groups, customers, people you're speaking to formally.

Keigo operates differently depending on which direction you're speaking:

**Key rule:** When speaking to someone from soto about your uchi, you use kenjōgo (humble) for your uchi group even when describing a superior — because relative to the soto person, your whole uchi group is "down."

**Example:** Your boss's name is Yamamoto-bucho. When you talk to a client about Yamamoto:

- To the client: 山本は明日参ります。(Yamamoto will come tomorrow.) — 参る (kenjōgo for going) because Yamamoto is uchi relative to the client

**But:** When talking to anyone about the client:

- 田中様はいらっしゃいますか。(Is Tanaka-sama here?) — いらっしゃる (sonkeigo) because the client is soto and outranks your uchi

This is the most consistently confusing aspect of keigo for learners. The rule: **relative to the outside world, your whole in-group uses humble forms.**

---

## When Each Tier Is Expected

| Context | Expected Register |
|---------|------------------|
| Close friends, family | Plain form — no keigo |
| Colleagues at the same level | Teineigo |
| Boss, senior colleague | Teineigo + kenjōgo for your own actions |
| Customer / client (any direction) | Teineigo for yourself; sonkeigo for their actions |
| Job interview | Teineigo + kenjōgo when describing your experience/plans |
| Formal public speech | Teineigo throughout |
| Business phone call | Full keigo expected; formal opening/closing phrases required |
| Department store staff to customer | Heavy sonkeigo — service Japanese is its own register |

## What N3 Learners Need to Produce

The Agency for Cultural Affairs (文化庁) notes that even native Japanese speakers find keigo challenging, and that most Japanese adults continue to learn it throughout their careers. Foreign learners at N3 are not expected to have mastered full sonkeigo/kenjōgo — they are expected to:

1. Understand the system conceptually (this session)
2. Produce the most common irregular verb forms correctly (Session 07)
3. Use the productive patterns (お〜になる, お〜いたす) in appropriate contexts
4. Not make the most consequential errors (using sonkeigo for yourself, kenjōgo for others)

Teineigo (ます/です) fluency is always the foundation. No one expects perfect keigo from a foreign learner who speaks excellent teineigo.

---

## The Most Consequential Keigo Errors

1. **Sonkeigo for yourself:** 私がいらっしゃいました (I came — using honorific for yourself). Sounds bizarre or rude.
2. **Kenjōgo for others:** 先生が参りました (The teacher came — using humble for someone you're elevating). Sounds like you're insulting the teacher.
3. **Keigo with close colleagues all day:** Creates social distance with people you should be casual with.
4. **No keigo at all with customers or in job interviews:** Signals unawareness of professional context.

---

## Why This Matters

Keigo is not optional for professional Japanese. It is a grammatical system, and operating in professional contexts without it is the equivalent of not knowing past tense — you can communicate, but something is obviously broken. The good news is that the three-tier framework is simple once you internalize the directionality: sonkeigo = elevating others; kenjōgo = humbling yourself.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Keigo is a grammatical system encoding social relationships — not just politeness vocabulary
- Three tiers: teineigo (polite baseline), sonkeigo (elevates others' actions), kenjōgo (humbles your own actions)
- Critical rule: sonkeigo is never used for yourself; kenjōgo is never used for others
- The uchi/soto distinction means your whole in-group uses humble forms when speaking to outsiders — even when describing your boss
- At N3, the goals are understanding the system, mastering the core irregular verb table, and avoiding the most consequential errors
