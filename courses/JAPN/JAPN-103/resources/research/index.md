## Documents

- **research-01.md** — Casual Speech Contractions and Colloquial Patterns
  Full taxonomy of spoken Japanese contractions: ている→てる, ておく→とく, てしまう→ちゃう/じゃう, の→ん, のだ→んだ, では→じゃ, という→って, ら抜き言葉 (ra-nuki). Includes a production-safety rating for each form (safe to produce vs. recognize only), register guidance (casual vs. formal), dialectal variants, and common learner errors. Pedagogical focus on the いdropping process underlying most contractions.

- **research-02.md** — Sentence-Final Particles and Discourse Markers
  Complete treatment of the 12 core sentence-final particles: ね, よ, よね, な/なあ, か, かな, わ, ぞ, ぜ, さ, もん/もの, し, and って (hearsay/topic). Gendered particle inventory with modern usage notes (traditional descriptions vs. actual young speaker behavior). Full discourse marker and filler word reference: えーと, あの, まあ, うーん, なんか, そういえば, ところで, じゃあ, だから, でも, ちなみに, それで. Aizuchi (back-channeling) system. Includes academic sourcing from Saigo (2011) on interactional functions.

- **research-03.md** — Keigo — Polite and Formal Speech Basics
  The three-tier keigo system: teineigo, sonkeigo (尊敬語), and kenjōgo (謙譲語). Complete verb transformation table of 15 core irregular keigo verbs: いる, 行く, 来る, 言う, する, 食べる, 飲む, もらう, あげる, くれる, 見る, 知っている, 会う, 聞く, 訪問する. Productive お/ご patterns for verbs not on the irregular list. Contextual when-to-use table by social situation. Agency for Cultural Affairs cited as Tier 1 source.

- **research-04.md** — Listening at Natural Speed — Strategies and Connected Speech
  Connected speech phenomena in Japanese: vowel devoicing (母音の無声化), nasal allophony, rendaku, mora compression, sentence-final truncation. Speech rate: natural conversation ~5-7 mora/second vs. JLPT test audio ~3 mora/second. Pitch accent basics: four Tokyo-dialect accent types (heiban, atamadaka, nakadaka, odaka), minimal pairs, interaction with vowel devoicing. Complete shadowing methodology (three-phase, 8-step progression from scripted to blind to native speed). Listening strategies: top-down vs. bottom-up, intensive listening protocol, weekly practice framework (75-125 min/week). Academic support: Hamada (2016) on shadowing effectiveness.

- **research-05.md** — Expressing Nuance, Uncertainty, and Emotion Naturally
  Unified certainty-evidence-emotion framework mapping all major uncertainty/evidential expressions from lowest to highest certainty: かな → かもしれない → っぽい → 気がする → みたいだ → そうだ (appearance) → ようだ → らしい → と思う → だろう → はずだ → に違いない. Detailed profiles of each expression with formation, evidence basis, colloquial variants, and formality. The hearsay vs. appearance そうだ distinction (most tested N3 nuance). Softeners ちょっと, なんか, まあ. Discovery expressions: なるほど, そうか, へえ, あ！. Agreement and disagreement systems including indirect refusal strategies, topic-change phrases, and conversation-closing gambits.

## Key Themes

- **The production-recognition gap is the central challenge of N3 conversation.** A student who has completed JAPN-101 and JAPN-102 can read and process N3 Japanese accurately. Speaking and listening naturally requires a second set of competencies — contracting automatically, producing particles fluently, hearing through phonological reduction — that must be trained separately and explicitly. These are not extensions of grammar knowledge; they are overlaid skills.

- **Register is not optional — it is grammatical.** Casual contractions (research-01), sentence-final particles (research-02), and keigo (research-03) are not stylistic choices. Using ぞ with a superior, ちゃう in a business email, or kenjōgo to describe someone else's actions are grammatical errors in the pragmatic/social sense. Register awareness must be taught as a formal system, not as vague "politeness."

- **Japanese encodes epistemic stance grammatically.** The certainty spectrum in research-05 — かもしれない through に違いない — is not optional hedging vocabulary. It is how Japanese encodes whether information comes from the speaker's imagination, their ears, their eyes, their reasoning, or their conviction. Choosing the wrong expression is not imprecise; it is inaccurate.

- **Listening is a separate skill from comprehension, requiring separate training.** The phonological gap between textbook Japanese (3 mora/second, full vowels, no contractions) and natural conversation (5-7 mora/second, devoiced vowels, contracted forms, particles at speed) is larger than most learners anticipate. Shadowing is the most efficient technique for closing this gap, but it requires a specific method: scripted → blind → natural speed, daily 10-15 minutes rather than weekend sessions.

- **Japanese conversation management is culturally systematic.** Aizuchi, indirect refusal, ちょっと as a signal, topic change markers, and conversation-closing phrases are not cultural curiosities — they are conversational competencies that must be explicitly learned. Missing aizuchi communicates disengagement; missing ちょっと's refusal function creates cross-cultural misunderstanding.

## Recommended Session Count

Based on material volume, pedagogical complexity, and the need for substantial practice rather than just explanation: **10–11 sessions**

Proposed distribution:

1. **From textbook to conversation: The casual speech layer** — orientation to the gap; most essential contractions (てる, とく, ちゃう, んだ, じゃない); production threshold discussion [research-01]
2. **Contractions in depth: Stacking and register** — compound contractions, ら抜き言葉, adjective compression, i-adjective slurring; register-sorting practice [research-01]
3. **Sentence-final particles I: The core four** — ね, よ, よね, な/なあ; the ね/よ distinction; かな; particle-swapping drill [research-02]
4. **Sentence-final particles II: The rough, gendered, and complex** — わ, ぞ, ぜ, さ, もん/もの, し, って (hearsay); modern gendered usage vs. textbook descriptions [research-02]
5. **Discourse markers and aizuchi** — filler words (えーと, あの, まあ, なんか), transition markers (ところで, じゃあ, そういえば, ちなみに), reaction words (なるほど, へえ, そうか), aizuchi system; conversation flow practice [research-02]
6. **Keigo I: The three-tier system and teineigo baseline** — overview of teineigo/sonkeigo/kenjōgo; when each is used; the uchi/soto distinction; social context table [research-03]
7. **Keigo II: Core verb pairs and productive patterns** — the 15-verb irregular table; お〜になる (sonkeigo) and お〜いたす (kenjōgo) productive patterns; the mixing error (using sonkeigo for oneself) [research-03]
8. **Listening at natural speed I: Connected speech and pitch accent** — vowel devoicing, nasal allophony, mora timing; pitch accent four types; minimal pairs; mora counting drill; why familiar words sound different [research-04]
9. **Listening at natural speed II: Shadowing method** — shadowing theory and evidence; three-phase method; progressive speed increase (0.8x → 1.0x → 1.2x); recommended materials for N3; weekly practice framework; dictation challenge [research-04]
10. **Expressing uncertainty and evidentiality** — the certainty spectrum; evidence-source basis of らしい vs. ようだ vs. そうだ vs. はずだ vs. に違いない; hearsay vs. appearance そうだ distinction; つもり for intention and self-conception [research-05]
11. **Softening, emotional coloring, and conversation management** — ちょっと/なんか/まあ as hedges; indirect refusal with ちょっと; discovery expressions and prosody (なるほど, へえ, あ！); agreement and disagreement systems; topic-change phrases; conversation closing [research-05]

Total: 11 sessions. Sessions 1-2 can be compressed into one dense session if students demonstrate strong casual speech recognition from immersion exposure, reducing to 10 sessions. Sessions 8-9 pair as a natural unit and could be extended to the full two sessions for students who have had minimal listening training.

## Coverage Gaps

- **No Tier 1 source for casual contractions, sentence-final particles, or discourse markers.** The JLPT official organization (jlpt.jp) does not publish content-level guides to these topics. The Agency for Cultural Affairs publishes survey data on language attitudes but not teaching materials for these specific areas. The Tier 2 academic sources (Saigo, MDPI) partially cover sentence-final particles with academic rigor; the other areas rely on Tier 5 practitioner sources which are mutually corroborating but not peer-reviewed.

- **Pitch accent data is underspecified.** Research-04 covers pitch accent conceptually but does not include a word-level pitch accent inventory for N3 vocabulary. The NHK Pitch Accent Dictionary (NHKアクセント辞典) is the authoritative reference but is a print resource not web-fetchable. Course Creator should recommend jisho.org's pitch accent data and the Pitch Accent Anki deck (by Dogen) as supplementary resources, with the caveat that these are Tier 5-6 in quality.

- **Connected speech phonology has thin academic sourcing.** The phonological phenomena documented in research-04 (devoicing, allophony, assimilation) are well-established in descriptive phonology (e.g., Vance's *The Sounds of Japanese*, Kubozono's *Handbook of Japanese Phonetics and Phonology*) but these texts are not web-fetchable. The CJKI and Japanese Professor sources used are Tier 5. Course Creator may wish to consult the academic sources for session design depth.

- **Keigo for specific workplace scenarios is not fully covered.** Research-03 covers the system and the core verb table but does not include scripts for specific workplace keigo situations (telephone answering, business email openings, greeting clients, apologizing formally). The AJALT "Japanese for Busy People" series (Tier 4) covers these scenarios and is recommended as a course resource. A dedicated workplace Japanese session (potentially a future JAPN-104) could cover this territory in depth.

- **Prosody and intonation beyond pitch accent are barely documented in accessible sources.** How Japanese speakers use tempo, elongation, pause, and pitch range to express emotion is an active area of linguistics research but not well-represented in learner-accessible materials. Research-05 documents the conversational expressions (なるほど, へえ, etc.) but cannot fully characterize the prosodic features that accompany them. Real audio examples (drama clips, podcast excerpts) will be essential for the Course Creator to convey this dimension in sessions.

- **Dialect variation is acknowledged but not covered.** Research-01 notes Kansai dialect contractions (とる for ておる) but does not systematically cover regional variation. Standard/Tokyo Japanese is the target; dialects are mentioned as recognition items only. If the student has specific dialect exposure goals (e.g., Osaka Japanese), a separate course or supplementary resource would be needed.
