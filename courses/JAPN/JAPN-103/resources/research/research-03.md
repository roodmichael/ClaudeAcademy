## Core Concepts

### What Keigo Is and Why It Matters

Keigo (敬語, literally "respectful language") is a register system within Japanese that marks social relationships through specific vocabulary and grammatical forms. It is not simply "extra polite" — it is a systematic encoding of social hierarchy, mutual obligations, and situational context into the language itself. Using the wrong level of keigo in the wrong context communicates the wrong social message, regardless of what the words literally mean.

Keigo is required in workplace interactions, customer-facing contexts, formal introductions, public speaking, and any situation involving unfamiliar people or those of higher status. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' periodic surveys (nihongo.bunka.go.jp) confirm that the vast majority of Japanese adults consider keigo proficiency essential for professional life, and that even native speakers continue to find keigo challenging. For foreign learners, the goal at N3 level is not mastery but functional awareness — knowing which register is expected, and being able to produce the most common forms reliably.

### The Three-Tier System

Japanese keigo comprises three distinct sub-systems that operate differently:

**1. 丁寧語 (teineigo) — Polite Language**
The most accessible tier for learners because it is what ます/です-form Japanese is. Teineigo adds no special vocabulary — it uses the same verbs with polite conjugations. It is the appropriate register for: strangers, acquaintances, initial workplace interactions, service contexts, and any situation where social distance is appropriate. Teineigo is the baseline from which sonkeigo and kenjōgo elevate or humble specific actions.

**2. 尊敬語 (sonkeigo) — Honorific/Respectful Language**
Elevates the actions of the other person (or third parties you are speaking about respectfully). The speaker does not use sonkeigo to describe their own actions. Sonkeigo operates by either substituting a different verb entirely (いる→いらっしゃる) or by using the construction お/ご + verb stem + になる. Sonkeigo is used when: speaking about what a superior, customer, teacher, or elder is doing or did.

**3. 謙譲語 (kenjōgo) — Humble Language**
Lowers the speaker's own actions to show deference to the listener. The speaker uses kenjōgo to describe their own actions or those of their in-group when speaking to an out-group superior. Kenjōgo operates by either substituting a different verb (行く→参る) or using the construction お/ご + verb stem + いたす/する. Kenjōgo is used when: describing what you will do, have done, or are offering to do in a service or respectful context.

**Fourth category (often added):** 美化語 (bikago) — beautification language — uses お/ご prefixes not to mark respect but to make speech more refined and elegant: お酒 (sake), お料理 (cooking), ご飯 (rice/meal). Bikago does not encode social hierarchy; it encodes the speaker's cultivation. It is common in everyday speech, especially among women in standard dialect, but is separable from the core three-tier system.

## Key Definitions

**敬語 (keigo):** The collective term for all forms of Japanese honorific language.
**丁寧語 (teineigo):** Polite language; uses ます/です endings.
**尊敬語 (sonkeigo):** Honorific language that elevates others' actions.
**謙譲語 (kenjōgo):** Humble language that lowers one's own actions.
**美化語 (bikago):** Beautification language; お/ご prefixes for elegance.
**内 (uchi):** In-group; people on "your side" (your company, your family).
**外 (soto):** Out-group; people outside your circle (other companies, customers).
**上司 (jōshi):** Superior / boss.
**お客様 (okyaku-sama):** Customer/client; always receives sonkeigo.

## Process / Mechanics

### The Core Verb Transformation Table

This table covers the verbs most frequently tested and most frequently needed in real-life keigo contexts. Learners should memorize these as pairs.

| Plain Form | Meaning | Teineigo | Sonkeigo (elevating OTHER) | Kenjōgo (humbling SELF) |
|-----------|---------|----------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| いる | to be/exist | います | いらっしゃる | おる / おります |
| 行く | to go | 行きます | いらっしゃる / おいでになる | 参る / 参ります |
| 来る | to come | 来ます | いらっしゃる / おいでになる | 参る / 参ります |
| 言う | to say | 言います | おっしゃる | 申す / 申します / 申し上げる |
| する | to do | します | なさる | いたす / いたします |
| 食べる | to eat | 食べます | 召し上がる | いただく / いただきます |
| 飲む | to drink | 飲みます | 召し上がる | いただく / いただきます |
| もらう | to receive | もらいます | — | いただく / いただきます |
| あげる | to give (to others) | あげます | — | 差し上げる |
| くれる | to give (to speaker) | くれます | くださる | — |
| 見る | to see/look | 見ます | ご覧になる | 拝見する |
| 知っている | to know | 知っています | ご存知 (noun: "Do you know?") | 存じる / 存じております |
| 会う | to meet | 会います | — | お目にかかる |
| 聞く | to ask/hear | 聞きます | — | 伺う / 承る |
| 訪問する | to visit | 訪問します | — | 伺う / お伺いする |

**Key overlaps to note:**
- いらっしゃる serves as sonkeigo for three verbs: いる, 行く, and 来る. Context disambiguates.
- 参る serves as kenjōgo for both 行く and 来る.
- いただく serves as kenjōgo for both 食べる/飲む AND もらう — two entirely different meanings distinguished by context.
- Sonkeigo for もらう does not exist because the direction of giving in もらう (receiving toward the speaker) cannot be elevated.

### Productive Patterns: お/ご + Stem Constructions

Beyond the irregular lexical substitutions above, two productive patterns allow forming keigo for verbs not on the irregular list:

**Sonkeigo:** お + [verb stem in ます form] + になる
- 使う → お使いになる (to use — respectful)
- 読む → お読みになる (to read — respectful)

**Kenjōgo:** お/ご + [verb stem in ます form] + いたす / する
- 届ける → お届けいたします (to deliver — humble)
- 連絡する → ご連絡いたします (to contact — humble)

**Note on お vs. ご:** Native Japanese words (和語, wago) take お; Sino-Japanese/Chinese-origin words (漢語, kango) take ご. Examples: お名前 (name, native); ご住所 (address, sino-Japanese). Some words have accepted either: お電話 and ご連絡 are both standard.

### When Each Register Is Expected in Daily Life

| Context | Expected Register | Notes |
|---------|------------------|-------|
| Close friends, family | Plain form (タメ口) | No keigo |
| Colleagues same level | Teineigo (ます/です) | Keigo optional inside the group |
| Boss, senior colleague | Teineigo + some kenjōgo for your actions | Especially in emails |
| Customer/client | Teineigo + sonkeigo for their actions | Always |
| Job interview | Teineigo + sonkeigo/kenjōgo as needed | Kenjōgo for what you have done |
| Phone call (business) | Full keigo expected | Formal opening/closing phrases required |
| Department store / service | Heavy sonkeigo (worker toward customer) | Retail keigo is its own register |
| Meeting new people (non-business) | Teineigo | Sonkeigo/kenjōgo unnecessary |
| Medical appointments | Teineigo | With doctors, slightly more formal |

## Common Beginner Mistakes

1. **Using sonkeigo for your own actions.** This is the most consequential error in keigo: 私が参りました (kenjōgo, correct) vs. accidentally saying 私がいらっしゃいました (sonkeigo, elevating oneself — a serious social error).

2. **Using kenjōgo for others' actions.** The mirror error: saying 先生が参りました (humble form for the teacher's arrival) when it should be 先生がいらっしゃいました.

3. **Overusing keigo with equals and close colleagues.** "Overusing it can create unnecessary distance or sound robotic" (Kotoba Garden Japanese). Using heavy sonkeigo with a teammate you see every day is socially odd.

4. **Forgetting that いただく has two meanings.** いただきます before a meal (receiving the meal, thankfulness) vs. 先生にいただきました (I received it from the teacher) vs. 食べていただけますか (humble request for someone to eat). All are いただく but different constructions.

5. **Treating すみません and 失礼します as keigo.** These are polite, but they are teineigo-level expressions, not keigo per se. They are always safe but do not substitute for keigo in formal contexts.

6. **Conflating politeness with keigo.** Teineigo (ます/です) is polite but not keigo in the narrow sense. True keigo (sonkeigo and kenjōgo) involves lexical substitution or structural marking beyond ます/です.

7. **Attempting keigo before mastering teineigo.** Native speakers are tolerant of learner errors in sonkeigo/kenjōgo; they are not tolerant of rude speech. Teineigo first, always.

## Hands-On Elements

- **Verb pair drilling:** Flashcard-style drilling of the 15 core irregular verb pairs (plain → sonkeigo → kenjōgo), with discrimination exercises ("who is doing what?").
- **Error correction exercise:** Provide 10 sentences with mixed-up sonkeigo/kenjōgo assignments; students identify and correct.
- **Role-play scenario:** Student plays a new employee receiving a senior colleague's visit and calling their own boss to notify them. Must use sonkeigo for the visitor's actions and kenjōgo for their own.
- **Register-sorting task:** Present 8 social scenarios; students identify which register tier is expected and why.
- **Email transformation:** Take a plain Japanese email; transform underlined verbs to appropriate keigo (mix of teineigo and kenjōgo for self-referential verbs).

## Expert Debates / Open Questions

- **The "バイト敬語 (baito keigo)" phenomenon:** Young part-time workers have developed a non-standard honorific register with forms like ～になります (where it becomes) for simple statements (e.g., ご注文はXXになります for "Your order is XX"). Prescriptivists and older speakers find this incorrect; younger speakers consider it natural service language. The Agency for Cultural Affairs has addressed this in its language surveys, noting it as a disputed usage without issuing a binding correction. Learners should recognize it but not adopt it.

- **Is sonkeigo/kenjōgo appropriate for foreign learners to produce?** There is genuine disagreement among Japanese language educators. Some argue that producing even imperfect keigo is essential for professional contexts in Japan; others argue that foreign learners who attempt keigo awkwardly create worse impressions than those who use excellent teineigo. The consensus leans toward: master teineigo thoroughly, learn the core lexical substitutions (the table above), and add productive patterns (お〜になる, お〜いたします) as production-level goals at N2.

- **The decline of keigo among younger generations:** Some sociolinguists document a gradual simplification of keigo in urban Japan, with younger speakers using fewer sonkeigo forms and more teineigo-baseline interactions even in formal contexts. This does not eliminate keigo but may reduce the penalty for imperfect keigo usage.

- **てくれる/てあげる/てもらう (JAPN-101 research-01):** These have keigo equivalents covered briefly there. The current document provides the kenjōgo forms in full context: くださる (sonkeigo of くれる), 差し上げる (kenjōgo of あげる), いただく (kenjōgo of もらう).
